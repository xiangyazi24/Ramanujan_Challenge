# Finite-field hypergeometric expression for Q_p(u) and root count

## Executive conclusion

The hypergeometric/K3 viewpoint is the right geometric language, but the proposed supersingular argument is not correct. The observed bound of at most 3 roots of Q_p(u) is a much stronger phenomenon than generic supersingular-fiber counting and cannot be obtained from general K3 non-isotriviality.

The key points are:

1. The Apéry polynomial Q_p(u) is not simply a one-variable finite-field hypergeometric function obtained by substituting the quadratic inverse r(u). The quadratic quotient is a descent/invariant problem, not a hypergeometric transformation.
2. Hypergeometric trace-function bounds naturally give O(sqrt(p)) zero counts, not O(1).
3. The equation Q_p(u)=0 is not equivalent to the ordinary supersingular locus of the K3 family. It is a Hasse/unit-root vanishing condition for a marked Mellin coordinate.
4. A proof of O(1) roots would require a special rigidity theorem (monodromy, invariant theory, or a low-degree factorization), not generic Weil/Ogus theory.

---

## (A) Is Q_p(u) itself hypergeometric?

The starting point is correct:

$$b_r=b_{p-1-r}$$

so b_r descends through

$$u=r(p-1-r).$$

The quotient map is a double cover

$$r\mapsto u,
\qquad
r^2+r+u=0.
$$

However, the finite-field hypergeometric representation does not behave like an ordinary analytic function under this substitution. The variable r in Greene/McCarthy hypergeometric expressions is a character parameter or a Mellin index, not a geometric coordinate in which one may simply replace r by

$$\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-4u}}2.$$

The correct object is the invariant part under the involution

$$r\leftrightarrow -1-r.$$

Therefore Q_p is better viewed as a descended trace function on the quotient curve

$$\mathbf A^1_r/(r\sim -1-r),$$

not as a new classical ${}_4F_3$ with argument u.

A possible expression exists through character sums:

$$Q_p(u)=\frac12\sum_{r^2+r+u=0} b_r,$$

but this introduces the quadratic-character projector

$$1+\eta(1-4u),
$$

and does not reduce to a lower hypergeometric family in general.

---

## (B) Does the McCarthy-Papanikolas formula simplify after the quotient?

Generally no.

The Apéry Mellin identity has the shape

$$b_r=-\sum_t \theta_p(t)t^r,$$

where theta is the Frobenius trace function of the geometric family.

The quotient gives

$$Q_p(u)=-\frac12\sum_{r^2+r+u=0}\sum_t\theta_p(t)t^r.$$

Interchanging sums gives a quadratic Gauss-type kernel in t. Schematically:

$$Q_p(u)=\sum_t\theta_p(t)K_u(t),$$

where

$$K_u(t)=\frac12(t^{r_+}+t^{r_-}).
$$

This is a quadratic descent transform, not another rank-4 hypergeometric function.

The geometric complexity remains that of the original Mellin sheaf plus the quadratic quotient.

---

## (C) Why root counting theorems only give O(sqrt(p))

The cited hypergeometric point-counting results count rational points on auxiliary curves. Weil gives

$$N_p=\text{main term}+O(\sqrt p).$$

There is no general mechanism forcing the error term to vanish.

To obtain

$$\#\{u:Q_p(u)=0\}=O(1),$$

one would need the zero condition to define a finite etale set of bounded degree. That means proving that the trace-zero condition is algebraically constrained before reduction.

A generic trace function does not have this property.

For example, elliptic families already show the obstruction: supersingular fibers can have O(sqrt(p)) points.

---

## (D) Is Q_p(u)=0 the supersingular K3 locus?

No. This is the main conceptual correction.

For the Apéry K3 family, the Frobenius trace contains several pieces of H^2. The condition

$$Q_p(u)=0$$

means that a particular marked Mellin coefficient vanishes:

$$b_r\equiv0\pmod p.$$

Geometrically this is a Hasse/unit-root condition, not the full supersingular condition.

Supersingularity means the whole Newton polygon has maximal slope behavior. Vanishing of one Fourier coefficient only detects one component of the Frobenius representation.

The implication

$$Q_p(u)=0\Rightarrow \text{supersingular fiber}
$$

is therefore false in general.

The correct analogy is:

$$Q_p(u)=0
$$

is a Hasse divisor condition for a marked unit-root section.

---

## (E) Why the Legendre example is the right warning

For the Legendre family,

$$a_p(t)=0$$

has approximately

$$\frac p2-h(-p)
$$

solutions.

This is not a failure of geometry; it is the expected size of a trace-zero condition on a one-dimensional family.

The Apéry observation of at most 3 roots means the Apéry family has additional arithmetic rigidity beyond generic K3 behavior.

Possible sources:

1. The involution quotient forces a much smaller marked family.
2. The Apéry Frobenius polynomial has extra factorization.
3. The relevant trace is a rank-two unit-root coordinate rather than the full K3 trace.
4. There may be a hidden modular parametrization.

But generic K3 supersingular theory cannot prove it.

---

## What would actually prove O(1) roots?

A successful theorem would look like one of the following.

### 1. Low-degree descended equation

Show that the zero condition satisfies

$$R_p(u)=0$$

where R_p has degree bounded independently of p.

Then the root count is immediate.

### 2. Monodromy rigidity

Show that the marked Frobenius coordinate is a nonconstant section of a rank-two local system whose vanishing divisor has bounded degree.

### 3. Branch-coupled Mellin theorem

Prove that simultaneous vanishing of different tame characters is controlled by a fixed-rank Frobenius representation.

This is precisely the TCMA2 direction.

## Final answers

**(A)** Q_p(u) is a quadratic descent of the Apéry Mellin trace, not generally a new finite-field hypergeometric function.

**(B)** The McCarthy-Papanikolas expression does not collapse to a lower-dimensional hypergeometric family; the quotient introduces a quadratic-character kernel.

**(C)** Hypergeometric root-count theorems give O(sqrt(p)), not O(1), because Weil errors do not vanish automatically.

**(D)** Q_p(u)=0 is not the full supersingular locus; it is a marked Hasse/unit-root vanishing condition.

**(E)** The observed O(1) root count is special Apéry rigidity, not a consequence of general K3 theory. The missing theorem is a bounded-degree/branch-coupling statement for the Apéry Mellin sheaf.
