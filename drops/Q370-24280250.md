# Q371 — Transfer matrices and multi-prime divisibility of the Apéry numbers

## Executive conclusion

The transfer-matrix formulation is exact and useful for organizing the recurrence, but **the product length, the low degree of the individual factors, and the fixed determinant do not by themselves improve the height bound for the number of distinct primes dividing one fixed Apéry number**.

There are two preliminary corrections to the setup:

1. With column vectors and
   \[
   M(c)=T(c-1)\cdots T(1),
   \qquad
   \binom{b_c}{b_{c-1}}=M(c)\binom51,
   \]
   the zero condition is
   \[
   5M(c)_{11}+M(c)_{12}=0,
   \]
   not \(5M(c)_{11}+M(c)_{21}=0\).

2. The modular product \(M_p(c)\in\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) exists in this form only when \(p>c\). If \(c\ge p\), the factor \(T_p(p-1)\) contains the undefined denominator \(p^3\), so one must instead use a block/Lucas decomposition. This does not change the final cross-prime conclusion, but it invalidates a naive product through the pole.

The decisive reduction is

```text
for p > c:

    first coordinate of M_p(c)(5,1)^T is 0
          <=> p divides b_c.

For distinct p_1,...,p_T > c, all T conditions together are simply

          p_1 ... p_T divides b_c.
```

Thus there are not \(T\) overdetermining equations on one matrix of fixed dimension. There is one integral scalar \(b_c\), viewed in \(T\) different residue fields. CRT converts the conditions into one congruence modulo \(p_1\cdots p_T\).

The best unconditional pointwise bounds immediately available are

\[
\omega(b_c)\le (\log(17+12\sqrt2)+o(1))\frac{c}{\log c}
\]

and, for primes \(p>\sqrt N\),

\[
\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_c)
\le
\frac{2\log(17+12\sqrt2)+o(1)}{\log N}\,c
\approx
\frac{7.05099\,c}{\log N}.
\]

No uniform Apéry-specific theorem \(\omega(b_c)=O(\log c)\), or even \(O(c^\varepsilon)\) for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), is presently supplied by the transfer matrices, the supersingular interpretation, the \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\)-root phenomenon, or reflection symmetry. Those are local-in-\(p\) structures; the missing input is a **vertical, cross-characteristic incidence theorem** for a fixed index \(c\).

## 1. The exact transfer identity

Write

\[
P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)
\]

and

\[
T(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)/(n+1)^3&-n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For

\[
v_n=\binom{b_n}{b_{n-1}},
\]

the recurrence is exactly \(v_{n+1}=T(n)v_n\). Hence, with the order of multiplication displayed explicitly,

\[
M(c):=T(c-1)T(c-2)\cdots T(1),
\]

we have

\[
M(c)\binom51
=
\binom{b_c}{b_{c-1}}.
\]

If

\[
M(c)=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&b\\ d&e
\end{pmatrix},
\]

then

\[
5a+b=b_c,
\qquad
5d+e=b_{c-1}.
\]

Therefore the relevant linear form in the entries is \(5M_{11}+M_{12}\).

### The pole at \(n=p-1\)

Reduction of every factor modulo \(p\) requires \(n+1\not\equiv0\pmod p\). Consequently,

\[
M_p(c)=T_p(c-1)\cdots T_p(1)
\]

is a product in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) only for \(c<p\). If \(c\ge p\), the recurrence has a singular step at \(n=p-1\). One may cross that step using the known Lucas/block laws, but not by pretending that \(T_p(p-1)\) is invertible.

In particular, if the intended application has \(c\le\sqrt N\) and all considered primes satisfy \(p>\sqrt N\), then \(p>c\) automatically and the displayed transfer product is legitimate. If \(c>\sqrt N\), the primes in \((\sqrt N,c]\) require a separate treatment.

## 2. Clearing denominators shows the exact CRT collapse

Define the integral matrices

\[
A(n):=(n+1)^3T(n)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)&-n^3\\
(n+1)^3&0
\end{pmatrix}
\in M_2(\mathbf Z)
\]

and

\[
\mathcal M(c):=A(c-1)\cdots A(1)\in M_2(\mathbf Z).
\]

Since

\[
\prod_{n=1}^{c-1}(n+1)^3=(c!)^3,
\]

we have

\[
M(c)=\frac{\mathcal M(c)}{(c!)^3}
\]

and the exact integral vector identity

\[
\mathcal M(c)\binom51
=(c!)^3\binom{b_c}{b_{c-1}}.
\]

Thus, if

\[
L_c:=5\mathcal M(c)_{11}+\mathcal M(c)_{12},
\]

then

\[
L_c=(c!)^3b_c.
\]

For every prime \(p>c\), the factor \(c!\) is a unit modulo \(p\), so

\[
L_c\equiv0\pmod p
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
b_c\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Consequently, for distinct primes \(p_1,\dots,p_T>c\), with \(Q=p_1\cdots p_T\),

\[
L_c\equiv0\pmod{p_i}\quad(1\le i\le T)
\]

is equivalent to

\[
Q\mid L_c,
\]

and because \((Q,c!)=1\), this is equivalent to

\[
Q\mid b_c.
\]

This is the central obstruction. The different characteristics do not produce additional independent integral quantities. They all test the same scalar coordinate.

A small height clarification is useful here. The unreduced common-denominator numerator \(L_c=(c!)^3b_c\) has size roughly \(\exp(3c\log c+O(c))\), not merely \(\exp(O(c))\). The factorial part is irrelevant for primes \(p>c\), and after cancelling it the pertinent integer is exactly \(b_c\), whose logarithmic height is \(\asymp c\). Thus the good exponential height bound comes from the integrality of the output coordinate, not from a small common numerator for the full matrix.

### Projective formulation

The transfer orbit gives the rational projective point

\[
x_c=[b_c:b_{c-1}]\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf Q).
\]

For \(p>c\), invertibility of \(M_p(c)\) implies that this point never reduces to \([0:0]\), and

\[
p\mid b_c
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x_c\bmod p=[0:1].
\]

The general height inequality is then simply

\[
\sum_{\substack{p>c\\x_c\bmod p=[0:1]}}\log p
\le \log|b_c|.
\]

Beating it requires a special gcd-height or vertical-incidence theorem for this particular orbit. The determinant and the length of the word do not furnish such a theorem automatically.

## 3. Why the degree of the factors does not bound the number of prime divisors

The statement that the product has entries that are “polynomials of degree at most \(c\) in \(n\)” conflates two different roles of \(n\).

In

\[
T(c-1)\cdots T(1),
\]

\(n\) is a dummy step index. After the multiplication, there is no free variable \(n\): for fixed \(c\), one obtains one rational matrix. As a function of the endpoint \(c\), its entries form P-recursive sequences; they are not fixed low-degree polynomials in \(c\).

One can introduce a genuine shift variable \(x\), for example

\[
A(x+h-1)\cdots A(x),
\]

whose entries are polynomials in \(x\) of degree \(O(h)\) after clearing denominators. That construction is useful for **same-characteristic collision questions**: two zeros at indices separated by \(h\) force the starting index to satisfy a gap polynomial modulo the same prime, and coprimality/resultants can then control common roots.

That is precisely the setting in which the project’s gap-polynomial machinery has force. It does not convert into a bound for the number of distinct characteristics \(p\) dividing one fixed evaluation \(b_c\). For a fixed integer value \(F(c)\), even a degree-one polynomial can have many distinct prime divisors; degree controls roots in one field, not the number of residue characteristics dividing one value.

Thus the product structure can help after one introduces another variable and obtains two or more algebraically independent conditions. With only the one condition \(p\mid b_c\), it has not yet done so.

## 4. The fixed determinant: exact dimension and exact local density

For \(p>c\),

\[
\det T_p(n)=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}
\]

and telescoping gives

\[
\det M_p(c)=\frac1{c^3}.
\]

Geometrically, prescribing a nonzero determinant cuts \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) from dimension \(4\) to dimension \(3\). But the zero-coordinate condition is still one independent equation, so it cuts the fixed-determinant fiber from dimension \(3\) to dimension \(2\). Over \(\mathbf F_p\), this remains a proportion \(\asymp1/p\), not \(\asymp1/p^2\).

There is an exact count. Let \(d\in\mathbf F_p^\times\), let

\[
\mathcal G_d=\{M\in\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf F_p):\det M=d\},
\]

and let \(u=(5,1)^t\ne0\). Then

\[
|\mathcal G_d|=|\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)|=p(p^2-1).
\]

For each nonzero vector \(w\in\mathbf F_p^2\), exactly \(p\) matrices in \(\mathcal G_d\) satisfy \(Mu=w\). There are \(p-1\) nonzero vectors with first coordinate zero. Hence

\[
\#\{M\in\mathcal G_d:(Mu)_1=0\}=p(p-1),
\]

and therefore

\[
\frac{\#\{M\in\mathcal G_d:(Mu)_1=0\}}{|\mathcal G_d|}
=\frac1{p+1}.
\]

This is the same local proportion obtained in all of \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\). Fixing the determinant does not even improve the first-order local density.

There is also a direct normalization to determinant one. Put

\[
D_c=\begin{pmatrix}c^3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\widehat M_p(c)=D_cM_p(c).
\]

Then

\[
\det\widehat M_p(c)=1,
\]

while multiplication of the first coordinate by the nonzero scalar \(c^3\) does not change whether that coordinate vanishes. Thus the determinant can be gauged away without changing the event of interest.

The count above is only a diagnostic; it is not claiming that the deterministic matrices \(M_p(c)\) are uniformly distributed. It shows something stronger for the present question: **even perfect equidistribution in the fixed-determinant space would give local probability about \(1/p\), exactly the ordinary divisibility scale.** The determinant constraint alone cannot yield a saving.

## 5. Cross-characteristic rigidity gives exactly the height bound

For a fixed \(c\), the matrices \(M_{p_i}(c)\) are not unrelated orbit points. For every \(p_i>c\), they are the reductions of the same rational matrix \(M(c)\). Hence

\[
5M_{p_i}(c)_{11}+M_{p_i}(c)_{12}=0
\]

for all \(i\) is merely the reduction of

\[
5M(c)_{11}+M(c)_{12}=b_c
\]

in several residue fields.

In CRT language,

\[
\mathbf Z/(p_1\cdots p_T)\mathbf Z
\simeq
\prod_{i=1}^T\mathbf F_{p_i},
\]

so the \(T\) displayed scalar congruences are one scalar congruence modulo \(Q=p_1\cdots p_T\). There is no dimension contradiction: equations over different fields cannot be added as codimensions in one fixed algebraic variety over a field.

The determinant merely says that the matrix is invertible modulo each \(p_i\). In particular, if the first coordinate of \(M_{p_i}(c)u\) is zero, the second coordinate is nonzero. This is a nonvanishing statement, not a second divisibility statement. A useful cross-prime argument would need, for example, to force each \(p_i\) to divide two genuinely independent small integers; then their product would divide a small gcd or resultant. Nothing of that kind follows from the present determinant identity.

### A general barrier lemma

The phenomenon is not special to the numerical constants in the Apéry recurrence.

> **Lemma.** Let \(R_c\in M_2(\mathbf Q)\), let \(u,\lambda\in\mathbf Z^2\), and suppose
> \[
> a_c=\lambda^tR_cu\in\mathbf Z.
> \]
> For every prime avoiding the denominators of \(R_c\),
> \[
> \lambda^t(R_c\bmod p)u=0
> \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
> p\mid a_c.
> \]
> Therefore simultaneous incidences at distinct good primes are exactly divisibility of the one integer \(a_c\) by their product.

Here \(R_c=M(c)\), \(u=(5,1)^t\), \(\lambda=(1,0)^t\), and \(a_c=b_c\). Any improvement must exploit an additional arithmetic property of the sequence \(a_c\), not just the existence of the matrix representation.

## 6. The best unconditional bounds obtainable from size

The dominant root of the limiting Apéry recurrence is

\[
\alpha=17+12\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^4,
\]

and standard recurrence asymptotics give

\[
\log b_c=c\log\alpha+O(\log c),
\qquad
\log\alpha\approx3.52549.
\]

Let

\[
T_Y(c):=\#\{p>Y:p\mid b_c\}.
\]

Since the primes are distinct,

\[
Y^{T_Y(c)}
<\prod_{\substack{p>Y\\p\mid b_c}}p
\le b_c,
\]

and therefore

\[
T_Y(c)\le\frac{\log b_c}{\log Y}
=\frac{(\log\alpha)c+O(\log c)}{\log Y}.
\]

For \(Y=\sqrt N\), this becomes

\[
T_{\sqrt N}(c)
\le
\frac{2\log\alpha}{\log N}c
+O\!\left(\frac{\log c}{\log N}\right),
\]

that is,

\[
T_{\sqrt N}(c)
\le
\frac{7.05099\ldots\,c}{\log N}
+O\!\left(\frac{\log c}{\log N}\right).
\]

This is exactly the weighted-height argument. The transfer matrix reproduces it but does not improve it.

### The generic primorial improvement for all prime factors

There is a stronger generic bound than \(\omega(m)\le\log m/\log2\). If \(\omega(m)=k\), then \(m\) is at least the product of the first \(k\) primes. The prime number theorem gives

\[
\log\prod_{j\le k}q_j=(1+o(1))k\log k,
\]

so

\[
\omega(m)\le(1+o(1))\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}.
\]

Applying this to \(m=b_c\) yields the unconditional pointwise estimate

\[
\boxed{
\omega(b_c)\le
(\log(17+12\sqrt2)+o(1))\frac{c}{\log c}
}
\]

and hence

\[
\boxed{
\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_c)
\ll
\min\!\left\{\frac{c}{\log N},\frac{c}{\log c}\right\}.
}
\]

The second estimate is still entirely generic: it uses only \(|b_c|=\exp(\Theta(c))\). It gives no power saving \(c^{1-\delta}\), and for \(N\) polynomially comparable to \(c\) it remains of order \(c/\log c\).

In the all-\(n\) project, this distinction matters. If \(c=o(N)\), the elementary height estimate may already give \(o(N/\log N)\). The hard regime is \(c\asymp N\), where both the height estimate and the maximal-order primorial estimate live at the same \(N/\log N\) scale.

## 7. Can one prove \(O(\log c)\) or \(O(c^\varepsilon)\) specifically for Apéry numbers?

No such pointwise theorem is currently obtained by the methods in the repository, and I did not find a published theorem establishing either

\[
\omega(b_c)=O(\log c)
\]

for every \(c\), or

\[
\omega(b_c)=O_\varepsilon(c^\varepsilon)
\]

for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).

The cited paper of Luca and Shparlinski is correctly relevant but points in the opposite direction. It proves, for a density-one set of indices, lower bounds of the form

\[
\omega(b_n)>c_0\log\log\log n
\]

and

\[
P(b_n)>c_0(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2}.
\]

It does not give an upper bound for \(\omega(b_n)\). See Florian Luca and Igor E. Shparlinski, [“Arithmetic properties of Apéry numbers,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 78 (2008), 545–562](https://doi.org/10.1112/jlms/jdn031).

Primitive-divisor theorems and lower bounds for the largest prime factor generally also work in the wrong direction for this question: they prove that new or large primes must occur, not that only a few distinct primes can occur.

A pointwise \(O(\log c)\) theorem would require very strong information, such as one of the following:

- almost all prime divisors of \(b_c\) being exponentially large in a quantitatively uniform sense;
- the prime divisors lying in a genuinely sparse set whose counting function is much smaller than \(x/\log x\);
- a bounded-degree norm/factorization with additional coprimality and height constraints that limit the number of rational prime ideals involved;
- a uniform vertical sieve showing that a fixed index \(c\) cannot lie in \(Z_p\) for many different \(p\).

None of these follows merely from the current transfer product.

## 8. Correction to the Erdős–Kac heuristic

For a random integer \(m\le x\), Erdős–Kac predicts

\[
\omega(m)\approx\log\log x
\]

with fluctuations of size \(\sqrt{\log\log x}\). Since \(b_c\) has size \(x\asymp\exp(\kappa c)\), the random-integer heuristic is

\[
\omega(b_c)\asymp\log c,
\]

not \(\kappa c/\log c\).

The quantity

\[
\frac{\log x}{\log\log x}
\asymp
\frac{c}{\log c}
\]

is the **maximal-order scale** for the number of distinct prime factors of an integer of size at most \(x\), not its normal order. Thus:

- \(\omega(b_c)\sim\log c\) is a reasonable random-value heuristic;
- \(\omega(b_c)=O(\log c)\) for every \(c\) is a much stronger uniform assertion and is not an Erdős–Kac theorem;
- the presently provable generic pointwise scale is \(c/\log c\).

There is also no logical reason that a special deterministic sequence must obey the typical behavior of random integers at every index. A uniform theorem needs arithmetic input, not only a probabilistic analogy.

## 9. Why the Apéry-specific local structures do not yet give a cross-prime upper bound

### 9.1 Reflection symmetry

For a fixed prime \(p\), reflection gives

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
\]

Thus the index-zero set

\[
Z_p=\{r\in[0,p-1]:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\}
\]

is paired by \(r\leftrightarrow p-1-r\). This controls the shape of \(Z_p\) inside one field. It does not prevent the same fixed integer \(c\) from belonging to \(Z_p\) for many different primes \(p\). The partner \(p-1-c\) changes with \(p\), so it does not yield a second fixed integer divisible by all those primes.

### 9.2 The \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\)-root structure of \(B_p\)

The factorization

\[
A_p(T)=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rT^r
\equiv \Delta(T)^{\varepsilon_p}B_p(T)^2\pmod p
\]

is a statement about roots of the truncation polynomial in the **parameter variable \(T\)**. The divisibility condition \(p\mid b_c\) is a statement that one **coefficient** of that polynomial vanishes. Roots of \(B_p(T)\) lying in \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\) do not directly bound the number of zero coefficients, and still less the number of different primes for which one prescribed coefficient is zero.

Even a square factorization modulo \(p\) does not imply \(p^2\mid b_c\). It gives convolution identities among coefficients modulo \(p\), not repeated rational-prime divisibility of each zero coefficient.

### 9.3 Supersingular/K3 interpretation

The supersingular interpretation is again local in the characteristic. It may lead to strong bounds for \(|Z_p|\), correlations within one \(p\), or equidistribution as the index varies modulo \(p\). Those would be valuable average tools. But even a hypothetical uniform bound \(|Z_p|\le C\) would not alone imply a pointwise bound for

\[
\#\{p:c\in Z_p\}.
\]

A family of sets can have uniformly bounded horizontal size while one vertical point belongs to many of them. One needs a vertical incidence theorem coupling the sets as \(p\) varies.

### 9.4 What the determinant/Casoratian does say

Invertibility implies that when \(p>c\) and \(p\mid b_c\), the companion coordinate \(b_{c-1}\) is nonzero modulo \(p\). This is the no-consecutive-zero phenomenon in this nonsingular range. It prevents a rank-zero vector but supplies no second vanishing coordinate. Therefore it cannot turn \(p\mid b_c\) into divisibility of a small gcd of two independent integers.

The current repository state in `problems/3.2/campaign3_questions/CTX_LASTSTAND.txt` and `problems/3.2/AVENUES.md` is consistent with this diagnosis: the determinant/casoratian and supersingular avenues provide important local structure, but no completed cross-characteristic mechanism that bounds the number of primes dividing one fixed \(b_c\).

## 10. The central-binomial counterexample remains decisive

For every prime \(p\) with

\[
n<p\le2n,
\]

Legendre’s formula gives

\[
v_p((2n)!)=1,
\qquad
v_p(n!)=0,
\]

and hence

\[
v_p\binom{2n}{n}=1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\omega_{>\sqrt n}\!\binom{2n}{n}
\ge \pi(2n)-\pi(n)
\sim\frac{n}{\log n}.
\]

The general maximal-order bound gives the matching \(O(n/\log n)\), so this is \(\Theta(n/\log n)\).

This example rules out any argument based only on:

- holonomicity or a polynomial-coefficient recurrence;
- representation as a product of low-complexity transfer factors;
- exponential height of the sequence;
- a fixed determinant or an \(\mathrm{SL}_2\)-normalization.

Apéry-specific geometry could still change the answer, but only after one proves a theorem translating that geometry into a restriction on **rational prime divisors across varying characteristics**. The currently known local root and symmetry statements do not make that translation.

## 11. What a successful transfer-matrix improvement would have to add

The transfer approach is not intrinsically useless; it is incomplete at exactly one point. To beat height, it must extract more than the scalar equation \(p\mid b_c\). Viable forms of additional input would include:

1. **A second independent forced congruence.** If every prime in the multi-prime set divided both \(F_c\) and \(G_c\), with \(\gcd(F_c,G_c)\) provably subexponential, then the product of the primes would be small. The determinant currently gives nonvanishing, not such a second congruence.

2. **A common low-height minor or resultant.** If the different prime conditions could all be transferred to divisibility of one fixed minor/resultant whose logarithmic height is \(o(c)\), that would improve the count. At present the common integer is \(b_c\) itself, of height \(\Theta(c)\).

3. **A vertical large sieve or cross-prime correlation theorem.** One would need a uniform estimate for the incidence sequence
   \[
   p\longmapsto 1_{Z_p}(c),
   \]
   strong enough for every fixed \(c\), not merely an average over \(c\).

4. **Strong information on the sizes or multiplicities of prime divisors.** For example, a theorem forcing all but \(O(\log c)\) prime divisors to have enormous logarithmic size, or forcing large valuations for most of them, could convert the exponential height into a small unweighted count. No such theorem is presently available.

5. **A genuine global Galois object with fixed \(c\).** A compatible system in which \(p\mid b_c\) is a special Frobenius condition might permit a Chebotarev/sieve argument. The known geometric descriptions vary with the characteristic and currently do not give such a fixed global extension whose splitting primes are exactly the divisors of \(b_c\).

The gap-polynomial/resultant machinery addresses a different configuration: multiple indices in the same characteristic. It cannot simply be transposed to one index in multiple characteristics, because there is then no pair of polynomial equations in a common finite field.

## Direct answers to the six questions

### 1. Height bound versus transfer product

**No improvement follows from the product structure as stated.** The factors are evaluated at deterministic successive indices; their product is one rational matrix, not a low-degree polynomial in a free variable. After applying it to \((5,1)^t\), the relevant coordinate is exactly \(b_c\). A new orbit theorem could in principle exploit the product, but the length and degree data alone do not.

### 2. The \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) constraint

**Yes, fixed determinant reduces the ambient algebraic dimension from \(4\) to \(3\), but it does not improve the divisibility exponent.** The target condition is still codimension one and occupies exactly a proportion \(1/(p+1)\) of the fixed-determinant fiber. Moreover, multiplying on the left by \(\operatorname{diag}(c^3,1)\) normalizes the determinant to \(1\) without changing the zero-coordinate event.

### 3. Cross-characteristic rigidity

**The simultaneous conditions collapse exactly to \(p_1\cdots p_T\mid b_c\).** They are independent only in the elementary CRT sense, not as equations that overdetermine a common geometric variable. With only one integral scalar, the product formula gives precisely the height bound and no more.

### 4. A Diophantine upper bound for this specific sequence

**The strongest immediate pointwise theorem is the generic**

\[
\omega(b_c)=O(c/\log c).
\]

Neither \(O(\log c)\) nor \(O(c^\varepsilon)\) is currently proved by the cited literature or the present project machinery. Luca–Shparlinski gives density-one lower bounds, not upper bounds.

### 5. Is \(\omega(b_n)=O(\log n)\) for every \(n\)?

**This is a plausible random-value heuristic but not a known theorem here, and it is not what Erdős–Kac states.** Erdős–Kac suggests typical order \(\log n\) for integers of size \(\exp(\Theta(n))\); a uniform pointwise \(O(\log n)\) bound for the deterministic sequence would be substantially stronger.

### 6. Do the supersingular, \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\)-root, and pairing properties provably limit \(\omega(b_n)\)?

**Not at present.** They constrain the structure within each characteristic and may support average or same-prime results. They do not currently couple different primes dividing one fixed integer \(b_n\). In particular, polynomial roots in the parameter variable are not coefficient zeros, and reflection pairs indices separately for each \(p\).

---

## Bottom line for the project

The transfer matrix gives a clean reformulation but, for the fixed-index multi-prime problem, it exposes only the already known scalar \(b_c\). The determinant supplies invertibility and a local codimension-one condition of density \(\asymp1/p\); it does not supply a second small divisible quantity. Therefore the route stops at

\[
\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_c)
\le
\frac{(2\log(17+12\sqrt2)+o(1))c}{\log N}
\]

and at the generic maximal-order bound

\[
\omega(b_c)=O(c/\log c).
\]

Any genuine improvement must be a new cross-characteristic arithmetic theorem—most naturally a vertical incidence/large-sieve statement, a common low-height resultant/minor, or a strong theorem on the sizes and multiplicities of the prime divisors—not a dimension count in \(\mathrm{SL}_2\).
