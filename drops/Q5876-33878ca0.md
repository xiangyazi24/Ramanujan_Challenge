# Prime-free smooth-radical attack: exact obstruction and the optimal \(n^\alpha\)-split

## Executive verdict

Let

\[
 b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2,
 \qquad
 D_n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n),
\]

and

\[
 \mathcal R_n:=\operatorname{rad}\gcd(b_n,D_n)
 =\prod_{\substack{p\le n\\p\mid b_n}}p.
\]

I found **no unconditional pointwise theorem in the audited primary literature** that proves

\[
 \log \mathcal R_n=o(n)
\]

for the variable-coefficient Apéry recurrence. The available theorems that genuinely apply to the Apéry sequence are pointwise Lucas congruences, supercongruences, lower \(p\)-adic valuation bounds, or upper denominator-clearing theorems. None gives an upper bound for the distinct prime support of an integral coefficient. The powerful cyclotomic and primitive-divisor theorems for Lucas and Lehmer sequences require a constant-coefficient divisibility sequence and do not apply to \((b_n)\).

There is, however, a clean unconditional pointwise reduction, sharper than the raw linear bound and optimal within the requested \(p=n^\alpha\) splitting method.

For \(2\le y\le n\), define the genuine lower-digit radical

\[
 \mathcal M_n(y)
 :=\prod_{\substack{y<p\le n\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}p.
\]

Then

\[
 \boxed{
  \mathcal M_n(y)\mid \mathcal R_n,
  \qquad
  0\le \log\mathcal R_n-\log\mathcal M_n(y)
  \le \vartheta(y)+\sum_{q\le n/y}\log b_q.
 }
 \tag{1}
\]

Consequently, uniformly for fixed \(0<\alpha<1\),

\[
 \boxed{
  \log\mathcal R_n
  =\log\mathcal M_n(n^\alpha)
   +O\!\left(n^\alpha+n^{2-2\alpha}\right).
 }
 \tag{2}
\]

The exponent supplied by this ledger is

\[
 E(\alpha)=\max\{\alpha,2-2\alpha\},
\]

so it is minimized uniquely at

\[
 \boxed{\alpha=\frac23,\qquad E(2/3)=\frac23.}
\]

Thus the sharpest power-form split is

\[
 \boxed{
  \log\mathcal R_n
  =\log\mathcal M_n(n^{2/3})+O(n^{2/3}).
 }
 \tag{3}
\]

Using the standard Apéry asymptotic

\[
 \log b_q=q\log(17+12\sqrt2)+O(\log(q+2)),
\]

one can optimize the constant as well. With

\[
 y=c\,n^{2/3},
\]

(1) gives

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n-\log\mathcal M_n(y)
 \le
 \left(c+\frac{\log(17+12\sqrt2)}{2c^2}+o(1)\right)n^{2/3}.
\]

The minimum occurs at

\[
 c=\bigl(\log(17+12\sqrt2)\bigr)^{1/3},
\]

and equals

\[
 \boxed{
 \left(\frac32\bigl(\log(17+12\sqrt2)\bigr)^{1/3}+o(1)\right)n^{2/3}.
 }
 \tag{4}
\]

Equations (1)--(4) are proved below. They do **not** prove the conjecture: they isolate the entire pointwise obstruction in \(\mathcal M_n(n^{2/3})\). No theorem located in the literature bounds that moving lower-digit radical by \(\exp(o(n))\).

# 1. What a qualifying theorem would have to say

The exact object is squarefree:

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n
 =\sum_{\substack{p\le n\\p\mid b_n}}\log p.
 \tag{5}
\]

A theorem solves the problem only if it is:

1. **pointwise** for every sufficiently large \(n\);
2. an **upper** bound for distinct prime support, not a lower bound for valuations or for the greatest prime factor;
3. valid for the actual variable-coefficient recurrence
   \[
   (m+1)^3b_{m+1}
   -(34m^3+51m^2+27m+5)b_m
   +m^3b_{m-1}=0;
   \]
4. uniform for the growing set of primes \(p\le n\), not for a fixed finite set \(S\).

No audited theorem meets all four requirements.

The best completely general pointwise estimate is still

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n\le\vartheta(n)=n+o(n),
 \tag{6}
\]

because \(\mathcal R_n\mid\operatorname{rad}(D_n)\). The coefficient-size estimate is weaker on the linear scale:

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n\le\log b_n
 =n\log(17+12\sqrt2)+O(\log n).
\]

# 2. Cyclotomic and Möbius factorizations

## 2.1 The lcm has a cyclotomic factorization, but it is universal

The exact identity

\[
 \boxed{D_n=\prod_{m=2}^n\Phi_m(1)}
 \tag{7}
\]

follows from

\[
 \Phi_m(1)=
 \begin{cases}
 p,&m=p^a\text{ for a prime }p,\ a\ge1,\\
 1,&m>1\text{ is not a prime power}.
 \end{cases}
\]

Every prime \(p\le n\) therefore enters (7) once for each power \(p^a\le n\), giving exactly \(v_p(D_n)=\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor\).

This factors the **carrier**, not the Apéry coefficient. It has no selectivity: the factor \(\Phi_{p^a}(1)=p\) is present whether or not \(p\mid b_n\). Taking a gcd with \(b_n\) merely returns (5).

## 2.2 The Lucas-sequence Möbius factorization fails at the first test

For a divisibility sequence one defines primitive/cyclotomic parts by

\[
 C_n=\prod_{d\mid n}u_d^{\mu(n/d)},
 \qquad
 u_n=\prod_{d\mid n}C_d.
 \tag{8}
\]

For Lucas sequences the quantities \(C_n\) are integral homogeneous cyclotomic values, and primitive-divisor theory can be applied to them.

For the Apéry sequence,

\[
 b_1=5,\qquad b_2=73,
\]

so the formal transform already gives

\[
 C_2=\frac{b_2}{b_1}=\frac{73}{5}\notin\mathbb Z.
 \tag{9}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
 b_1\nmid b_2,
 \qquad
 \gcd(b_1,b_2)=1\ne b_{\gcd(1,2)}=5.
\]

Thus \((b_n)\) is not a divisibility sequence and has no index-cyclotomic factorization of the Lucas type. Formula (8) survives only as a tautological identity in \(\mathbb Q^\times\), with denominators that destroy primitive-prime interpretation.

## 2.3 An Euler product for the generating series is not a factorization of \(b_n\)

Because

\[
 B(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nz^n\in1+z\mathbb Z[[z]],
\]

one may write a formal big-Witt/Euler product

\[
 B(z)=\prod_{m\ge1}(1-z^m)^{-e_m}
\]

with integral exponents \(e_m\). This is an additive partition expansion for the coefficients of a generating series. It does **not** express the integer \(b_n\) as a product of numerical cyclotomic factors. Prime divisors of one coefficient can be created by addition and cancellation among many partition terms, so this formal factorization gives no bound for (5).

## 2.4 Why Lucas and Lehmer primitive-divisor theorems cannot be imported

Bilu--Hanrot--Voutier prove that every Lucas or Lehmer number of index \(n>30\) has a primitive divisor:

- Y. Bilu, G. Hanrot and P. Voutier, [*Existence of primitive divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers*](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.2001.080), J. Reine Angew. Math. 539 (2001), 75--122.

Their hypotheses include a fixed Lucas/Lehmer pair and the exact Binet/cyclotomic structure associated with a constant-coefficient recurrence. The Apéry transfer matrix changes with the index, and (9) shows that even the basic divisibility structure is absent. Moreover, the conclusion is a **lower** bound--existence of a new divisor--whereas (5) asks for an upper bound on all smooth divisors.

Accordingly, BHV is not an input to the present problem; it is an exact hypothesis-failure comparison.

# 3. Diagonals, Hadamard products, and globally bounded G-functions

## 3.1 What is true for Apéry

The Apéry generating function is a globally bounded \(G\)-function with integer coefficients, and the numbers are diagonal coefficients of a fixed rational function. Straub gives the particularly simple realization

\[
 \frac{1}{(1-x_1-x_2)(1-x_3-x_4)-x_1x_2x_3x_4};
\]

see A. Straub, [*Multivariate Apéry numbers and supercongruences of rational functions*](https://doi.org/10.2140/ant.2014.8.1985), Algebra & Number Theory 8 (2014), 1985--2008, [arXiv:1401.0854](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.0854).

This representation proves or explains powerful congruence structure. It does not constrain the pointwise prime support of one coefficient.

Recent structural results do not change that conclusion:

- A. Harder and J. Kramer-Miller, [*The diagonal and Hadamard grade of hypergeometric functions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10354), prove that the Apéry generating function has diagonal grade \(3\).
- X. Caruso, F. Fürnsinn, D. Vargas-Montoya and W. Zudilin, [*Galois groups of Apéry-like series modulo primes*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0004972725100932), Bull. Aust. Math. Soc. (online 2026), compute Galois groups of the reductions of the generating series.
- B. Adamczewski, A. Bostan and X. Caruso, [*Diagonals and algebraicity modulo \(p\): a sharper degree bound*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14920), prove a general polynomial bound for algebraic degrees of diagonal reductions.

These are primewise algebraicity/Galois/representation theorems. None states an upper bound for the set of primes dividing a moving coefficient.

## 3.2 A class-wide theorem is impossible: the central-binomial counterexample

Let

\[
 c_n=\binom{2n}{n}.
\]

Then

\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^n=(1-4z)^{-1/2}
\]

is algebraic, globally bounded, hypergeometric, holonomic, and a diagonal of the rational function \(1/(1-x-y)\).

For every sufficiently large prime

\[
 \frac n2<p\le\frac{2n}{3},
\]

Legendre's formula gives

\[
 v_p\binom{2n}{n}
 =\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{p}\right\rfloor
  -2\left\lfloor\frac n p\right\rfloor
 =3-2=1.
\]

Therefore

\[
 \log\operatorname{rad}\gcd\!\left(\binom{2n}{n},D_n\right)
 \ge
 \vartheta(2n/3)-\vartheta(n/2)
 =\left(\frac16+o(1)\right)n.
 \tag{10}
\]

This proves a sharp no-go:

> Integrality, holonomicity, global boundedness, being a rational diagonal, or having finite Hadamard grade cannot by themselves imply \(\log\operatorname{rad}\gcd(u_n,D_n)=o(n)\).

Any successful theorem must use arithmetic specific to the distinguished Apéry coefficient sequence, not its ambient function class.

# 4. Denominator theorems control the wrong arithmetic side

Fischler--Rivoal prove the following. If

\[
 g(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n
\]

is annihilated by a geometric \(G\)-operator of order \(\mu\), then there are fixed positive integers \(b,b_0,C\) such that, for every \(n\),

\[
 D_{bn+b_0}^{\mu-1}C^{n+1}a_n
\]

is an algebraic integer. Their Corollary 2 gives the corresponding statement for any algebraic-valued solution of the associated polynomial-coefficient recurrence. See S. Fischler and T. Rivoal,
[*On the denominators of the Taylor coefficients of G-functions*](https://doi.org/10.2206/kyushujm.71.287), Kyushu J. Math. 71 (2017), 287--298, Theorems 1--2 and Corollary 2; [arXiv:1606.00706](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.00706).

This theorem is pointwise and its geometric case is unconditional. It still gives nothing here:

1. the Apéry coefficients \(b_n\) are already integers, so their least common coefficient denominator is \(1\);
2. the theorem gives an **upper denominator clearing**, while the present problem needs an upper bound for a numerator radical, or equivalently a **lower bound on what remains after reduction** in the approximant setting;
3. applying the theorem to another solution of the Apéry recurrence controls a common clearing, not the gcd cancellation between two cleared solutions;
4. the coefficientwise reciprocal sequence \((1/b_n)\) is not supplied as a solution of a fixed \(G\)-operator, so one cannot turn numerator factors into coefficient denominators by inversion.

This is the precise direction mismatch. A denominator theorem is not a reduced-denominator theorem and is not a smooth-numerator theorem.

# 5. Pointwise \(p\)-adic theorems and why the valuation sum stalls

## 5.1 The exact Lucas input

Gessel proved the pointwise congruence

\[
 \boxed{b_{ap+r}\equiv b_ab_r\pmod p}
 \qquad(a\ge0,\ 0\le r<p),
 \tag{11}
\]

for every prime \(p\); see I. Gessel, [*Some congruences for Apéry numbers*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-314X(82)90071-3), J. Number Theory 14 (1982), 362--368.

This is the load-bearing theorem in the split proved in Section 6.

## 5.2 Delaygue gives lower valuations, not support upper bounds

Delaygue proves pointwise lower bounds for \(p\)-adic valuations of the relevant factorial-ratio multisums and constant terms, including the classical Apéry numbers, and effective Lucas criteria; see É. Delaygue,
[*Arithmetic properties of Apéry-like numbers*](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0010437X17007552), Compos. Math. 154 (2018), 249--274, [arXiv:1310.4131](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4131).

These results say that certain zero digits force at least the corresponding amount of \(p\)-adic divisibility. They do not say that few characteristics have a zero digit.

The elementary valuation ledger is

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n
 \le\sum_{p\le n}v_p(b_n)\log p
 \le\log b_n=O(n).
 \tag{12}
\]

To improve (12) to \(o(n)\) through multiplicities, one would need a theorem forcing the average valuation of every selected smooth prime to tend to infinity. No such theorem holds at the first layer: for example

\[
 b_5=819005=11\cdot74455,
 \qquad 11\nmid74455,
\]

so \(v_{11}(b_5)=1\). A single zero digit can carry only one forced factor of \(p\).

For \(p>\sqrt n\), writing \(n=qp+r\) gives only two base-\(p\) digits. If exactly one of \(b_q,b_r\) vanishes modulo \(p\), the Lucas/Delaygue machinery forces only one zero-digit contribution. Supercongruences at special multiplicative indices do not change this generic moving-digit situation.

Thus a valuation-sum attack stops at the same linear scale as (12).

# 6. The exact \(y\)-split theorem

## Theorem 6.1

Let \(2\le y\le n\), put

\[
 Q=\left\lfloor\frac n y\right\rfloor,
 \qquad
 P(y)=\prod_{p\le y}p,
\]

and define

\[
 \mathcal M_n(y)
 =\prod_{\substack{y<p\le n\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}p.
\]

Then

\[
 \boxed{
  \mathcal M_n(y)\mid\mathcal R_n
 }
 \tag{13}
\]

and

\[
 \boxed{
  \frac{\mathcal R_n}{\mathcal M_n(y)}
  \mid
  P(y)\prod_{q=1}^{Q}\operatorname{rad}(b_q).
 }
 \tag{14}
\]

In particular,

\[
 0\le\log\mathcal R_n-\log\mathcal M_n(y)
 \le\vartheta(y)+\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\log b_q.
 \tag{15}
\]

### Proof

Fix a prime \(p>y\), and write

\[
 n=qp+r,
 \qquad q=\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor,
 \qquad 0\le r<p.
\]

By (11),

\[
 b_n\equiv b_qb_r\pmod p.
 \tag{16}
\]

If \(p\mid b_r=b_{n\bmod p}\), then (16) gives \(p\mid b_n\); hence every factor of \(\mathcal M_n(y)\) belongs to \(\mathcal R_n\), proving (13).

Now let \(p\mid\mathcal R_n/\mathcal M_n(y)\). If \(p\le y\), then \(p\mid P(y)\). If \(p>y\), then

\[
 p\mid b_n,
 \qquad p\nmid b_r.
\]

Equation (16) forces

\[
 p\mid b_q.
\]

Moreover \(q\le Q\). For each fixed \(q\), the distinct primes assigned to that quotient have product dividing \(\operatorname{rad}(b_q)\). Every \(p\) has a unique quotient \(q=\lfloor n/p\rfloor\). Multiplying over \(q\) proves (14), and logarithms give (15). \(\square\)

## 6.2 A self-contained growth bound

For every \(0\le k\le q\),

\[
 \binom qk\le2^q,
 \qquad
 \binom{q+k}{k}\le2^{q+k}\le4^q.
\]

Hence

\[
 b_q\le(q+1)64^q,
\]

and therefore

\[
 \sum_{q\le Q}\log b_q=O(Q^2+Q\log Q).
 \tag{17}
\]

Chebyshev's bound \(\vartheta(y)=O(y)\), (15), and \(Q\le n/y\) give the unconditional uniform estimate

\[
 \boxed{
  \log\mathcal R_n
  =\log\mathcal M_n(y)
   +O\!\left(y+\frac{n^2}{y^2}\right).
 }
 \tag{18}
\]

No unproved asymptotic is needed for (18).

## 6.3 The \(n^\alpha\) ledger and its optimum

Take \(y=n^\alpha\). Equation (18) becomes

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n
 =\log\mathcal M_n(n^\alpha)
 +O\!\left(n^\alpha+n^{2-2\alpha}\right).
\]

Both error terms are \(o(n)\) exactly when \(\alpha>1/2\). Within this split, the error exponent is

\[
 \max\{\alpha,2-2\alpha\}.
\]

Balancing the two terms gives

\[
 \alpha=2-2\alpha,
 \qquad
 \alpha=\frac23.
\]

This proves (2)--(3).

## 6.4 Leading-constant optimization

The standard Poincaré asymptotic for the positive Apéry solution gives

\[
 \log b_q=q\log\lambda+O(\log(q+2)),
 \qquad \lambda=17+12\sqrt2.
\]

Thus

\[
 \sum_{q\le Q}\log b_q
 =\frac{\log\lambda}{2}Q^2+O(Q\log Q).
\]

For \(y=c n^{2/3}\), the prime number theorem and (15) give

\[
 \log\mathcal R_n-\log\mathcal M_n(y)
 \le
 \left(c+\frac{\log\lambda}{2c^2}+o(1)\right)n^{2/3}.
\]

Differentiating the coefficient shows that its minimum is attained at

\[
 c^3=\log\lambda,
\]

with minimum \(\frac32(\log\lambda)^{1/3}\). This proves (4).

# 7. Why the \(2/3\) exponent is rigid for this method

The two costs in (18) have different and unavoidable origins.

1. **Small-prime cost.** Without using any Apéry information, the radical supported on \(p\le y\) can have logarithmic weight as large as
   \[
   \vartheta(y)\asymp y.
   \]

2. **Outer-digit false positives.** For \(p>y\), a divisor of \(b_n\) not caused by the lower digit \(b_{n\bmod p}\) must divide \(b_q\) with \(q\le n/y\). The only unconditional pointwise upper bound available is
   \[
   \prod_{q\le n/y}\operatorname{rad}(b_q)
   \le\prod_{q\le n/y}b_q,
   \]
   whose logarithm is \(\Theta((n/y)^2)\).

Balancing \(y\) and \((n/y)^2\) forces \(y\asymp n^{2/3}\). Any better exponent from this decomposition would require a genuinely new pointwise theorem such as

\[
 \sum_{q\le Q}\log\operatorname{rad}_{p>n/Q}(b_q)=o(Q^2),
 \tag{19}
\]

uniformly in the coupled parameters. That is itself a smooth-radical theorem for initial Apéry terms; none of the audited sources proves it.

A small refinement--observing that \(b_q<y\) for \(q\ll\log y\), so those \(q\) cannot have a prime divisor greater than \(y\)--removes only \(O((\log y)^2)\) from the \(Q^2\) ledger and does not change the exponent.

# 8. The exact surviving theorem

For any fixed \(\alpha\in(1/2,1)\), (2) proves the equivalence

\[
 \boxed{
  \log\mathcal R_n=o(n)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \log\mathcal M_n(n^\alpha)=o(n).
 }
 \tag{20}
\]

The quantitatively strongest version furnished by the split is

\[
 \boxed{
 \log\mathcal R_n
 =
 \log\prod_{\substack{p>n^{2/3}\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}p
 +O(n^{2/3}).
 }
 \tag{21}
\]

The right side is the genuine lower-digit, cross-characteristic alignment problem. It is not controlled by:

- a cyclotomic factorization of \(b_n\), because none exists in the Lucas-sequence sense;
- a denominator theorem, because \(b_n\) is already integral;
- diagonal or Hadamard structure, because (10) rules out any class-wide theorem;
- a valuation sum, because a selected digit can contribute valuation exactly one;
- primitive-divisor theory, because the Apéry sequence is not a constant-coefficient divisibility sequence and primitive-divisor theorems point in the opposite direction.

Accordingly, the sharp proof-grade conclusion is:

> **Unconditional partial theorem.** All primes \(p\le n^{2/3}\) and all high-prime divisors arising from the outer Lucas digit contribute only \(O(n^{2/3})\) logarithmic weight. The full conjecture is equivalent to sublinear logarithmic weight for the remaining high primes satisfying the true lower-digit condition \(p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\).

This is pointwise for every \(n\), not density-one. It is also the limit of the present smooth-radical, denominator, cyclotomic, and one-prime \(p\)-adic methods.

# 9. Facts, rejected transfers, and open statements

## Proved here

- The exact divisibility sandwich (13)--(15).
- The uniform \(y\)-bound (18).
- The optimal power split \(\alpha=2/3\).
- The optimized leading constant (4).
- Failure of the standard index-Möbius factorization at \(n=2\).
- The diagonal/globally-bounded no-go example (10).

## Published inputs actually used

- Gessel's pointwise Lucas congruence (11).
- Standard prime-number estimates for \(\vartheta\).
- The standard Apéry asymptotic only for the optional leading-constant refinement (4); the exponent \(2/3\) follows from the elementary bound (17).

## Theorems checked but not transferable

- Lucas/Lehmer primitive-divisor theorems: wrong sequence class and wrong direction.
- Fixed-\(S\) and algebraic-torus gcd theorems: fixed finite \(S\), constant coefficients, or fixed torus orbit.
- G-function denominator theorems: denominator clearing rather than numerator support.
- Diagonal algebraicity/Galois theorems modulo \(p\): primewise structure without a coefficient-zero count across varying primes.

## Open

- \(\log\mathcal M_n(n^{2/3})=o(n)\) pointwise.
- Any pointwise smooth-radical theorem for the distinguished variable-coefficient Apéry solution strong enough to imply (19) or (20).
- Any cross-prime arithmetic relation that couples the selected lower digits \(n\bmod p\) for different characteristics.

No equality, probabilistic independence, or density-one statement is used as a substitute for these open pointwise assertions.
