There is a strong exact theorem, but it does **not** produce a new subexponential carrier.

\[
A=d_{n-q}(J_n),\qquad B=d_{n-q-1}(J_{n-1}),\qquad

\[
m=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{q+1}\right\rfloor,\qquad

\eta_q(n):=\frac{L_m}{L_{m^-}}.

\boxed{\eta_q(n)\mid n.}

## Lemma — one common target row

Suppose columns have the form

\[
d_i=a_ie_{u_i}+\lambda_ie_v,
\]

where the \(u_i\) are distinct and \(v\ne u_i\) for all \(i\). Then every square minor of the matrix \([d_i]\) is either:

- independent of all \(\lambda_i\), or
- a linear combination of terms each containing **exactly one** \(\lambda_j\).

In particular no minor contains a product \(\lambda_i\lambda_j\) with \(i\ne j\).

### Proof

A determinant is multilinear in the columns. In its expansion choose from each column either the \(e_{u_i}\) term or the \(e_v\) term. If two columns contribute their \(e_v\) terms, two selected columns are proportional to the same basis vector \(e_v\), so that determinant term is zero. Hence at most one \(\lambda\)-term can survive. \(\square\)

Applying the lemma to (9.1):

- exterior minors may multiply many universal factors \((qp_i)^3\), which recreates the full candidate shell;
- they can see one target-sensitive \(\lambda_{qp_i}\) at a time;
- they cannot produce a determinant whose target-sensitive part contains the product of two distinct target primes, let alone the full target radical.

This is the exact Apéry-specific reason the Jacobi Smith construction can detect all target primes locally while failing to provide a small characteristic-zero common numerator for them globally.

Any attempt to obtain the full target product must therefore either:

1. multiply separate one-prime residual scalars, immediately reintroducing a numerator at least \(R_q(n)\); or
2. find a genuinely new identity that moves the different residual conditions into one common characteristic-zero scalar before taking determinants.

Desnanot--Jacobi does neither.

# 10. The first jet does not rescue the adjacent carrier

The restart kernel has Taylor expansion

\[
U_r(X)=b_r+W_rX+u_{r,2}X^2+\cdots,
\]

where \(W_r\) is the genuine Gessel first jet.

Thus, for \(p>r\),

\[
U_r(cp)=b_r+cpW_r\pmod{p^2}.
\tag{10.1}
\]

At a target \(b_r=p\beta_{p,r}\),

\[
\frac{U_r(cp)}p
\equiv \beta_{p,r}+cW_r\pmod p.
\tag{10.2}
\]

The divided value \(\beta_{p,r}\) is private to the prime. A finite difference in \(c\):

- either leaves the coefficient of \(\beta_{p,r}\) nonzero;
- or annihilates the constant term, in which case the corresponding undivided combination is divisible by \(p\) for **every** prime, target or not.

Moreover only \(c=q\) keeps the endpoint on the fixed row \(n\):

\[
cp+r=n+(c-q)p.
\]

So the first jet does not furnish a second same-row target equation with which to eliminate the private quotient.

This agrees with the one-common-target-row obstruction in Section 9.
