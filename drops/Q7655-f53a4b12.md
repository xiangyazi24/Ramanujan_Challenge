# 1. Mellin proof of the Ramanujan identity

Define, for `y>0`,

\[
S(y)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{m^3(e^{2\pi my}-1)}.
\tag{1.1}
\]

The claimed transformation is

\[
\boxed{
S(y)+y^2S(1/y)
=-\frac{1+y^2}{2}\zeta(3)
+\frac{\pi^3}{180}(y^3+5y+y^{-1}).
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

This follows directly from Mellin inversion and the functional equation of `zeta`.

## 1.1 Mellin representation

Expand the Bose factor:

\[
\frac1{e^{2\pi my}-1}
=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-2\pi mky}.
\]

For any real `c>1`, absolute convergence permits termwise Mellin inversion:

\[
e^{-x}
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)x^{-s}\,ds.
\]

Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
S(y)
&=\sum_{m,k\ge1}\frac{e^{-2\pi mky}}{m^3}\\
&=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}
\Gamma(s)(2\pi y)^{-s}
\left(\sum_{k\ge1}k^{-s}\right)
\left(\sum_{m\ge1}m^{-s-3}\right)ds.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
S(y)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}A(s)y^{-s}\,ds,
\qquad
A(s)=\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(s+3)(2\pi)^{-s}.
}
\tag{1.3}
\]

## 1.2 Functional-equation involution

Use

\[
\zeta(s)
=2^s\pi^{s-1}\sin\frac{\pi s}{2}
\Gamma(1-s)\zeta(1-s)
\]

and

\[
\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\frac\pi{\sin\pi s}.
\]

Then, meromorphically in `s`,

\[
\boxed{
A(s)=
\frac{\zeta(1-s)\zeta(s+3)}{2\cos(\pi s/2)}.
}
\tag{1.4}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{A(-s-2)=-A(s).}
\tag{1.5}
\]

Now

\[
y^2S(1/y)
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}A(s)y^{s+2}\,ds.
\]

Substitute `u=-s-2`.  The reversal of the vertical line and `ds=-du` give

\[
\boxed{
y^2S(1/y)
=-\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(-c-2)}A(u)y^{-u}\,du.
}
\tag{1.6}
\]

Choose `1<c<2`.  Since the denominator in (1.4) gives exponential decay on vertical lines, the contour may be shifted between `Re(s)=c` and `Re(s)=-c-2`.  The poles crossed are exactly

\[
s=1,0,-1,-2,-3.
\]

Thus

\[
S(y)+y^2S(1/y)
=\sum_{s_0\in\{1,0,-1,-2,-3\}}
\operatorname*{Res}_{s=s_0}A(s)y^{-s}.
\tag{1.7}
\]

## 1.3 Residues and signs

The five residues are

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
s_0 & \operatorname*{Res}_{s=s_0}A(s)\\ \hline
1   & \pi^3/180\\
0   & -\zeta(3)/2\\
-1  & \pi^3/36\\
-2  & -\zeta(3)/2\\
-3  & \pi^3/180.
\end{array}
\tag{1.8}
\]

For example, the two `zeta(3)` residues are especially transparent from (1.4): at `s=0`, `zeta(1-s)` has residue `-1` as a function of `s`, while at `s=-2`, `zeta(s+3)` has residue `+1` and `2 cos(-pi)=-2`.  Both residues are `-zeta(3)/2`.

Multiplying by `y^{-s_0}` gives exactly

\[
S(y)+y^2S(1/y)
=-\frac{1+y^2}{2}\zeta(3)
+\frac{\pi^3}{180}(y^{-1}+5y+y^3),
\]

which proves (1.2), with every sign fixed.

# 2. Exact transformation of the Eichler combination `V`

Put

\[
V(y)=U(e^{-2\pi y})
=-36S(y)+6S(2y)-4S(3y)+6S(6y).
\tag{2.1}
\]

Let

\[
R(y):=-\frac{1+y^2}{2}\zeta(3)
+\frac{\pi^3}{180}(y^3+5y+y^{-1}).
\]

From (1.2), for every positive integer `a`,

\[
y^2S(1/(ay))
=\frac{R(ay)-S(ay)}{a^2}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Substitute `a=6,3,2,1` into

\[
y^2V(1/(6y))
=-36y^2S(1/(6y))
+6y^2S(1/(3y))
-4y^2S(1/(2y))
+6y^2S(1/y).
\]

The `S`-terms combine to

\[
S(6y)-\frac23S(3y)+S(2y)-6S(y)
=\frac{V(y)}6.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
y^2V(1/(6y))=\frac{V(y)}6+P(y),
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

where

\[
P(y)=-R(6y)+\frac23R(3y)-R(2y)+6R(y).
\tag{2.4}
\]

The algebra gives

\[
\boxed{
P(y)=
\left(14y^2-\frac73\right)\zeta(3)
+\pi^3\left(-\frac{10}{9}y^3+\frac5{162y}\right).
}
\tag{2.5}
\]

There is no surviving `pi^3 y` term because

\[
-6+\frac23\cdot3-2+6=0.
\]

Let

\[
y_0=\frac1{\sqrt6}.
\]

Then `1/(6y_0)=y_0`, and both parts of `P(y_0)` vanish:

\[
14y_0^2-\frac73=0,
\]

and

\[
-\frac{10}{9}y_0^3+\frac5{162y_0}=0.
\]

So

\[
\boxed{P(y_0)=0.}
\tag{2.6}
\]

Differentiate (2.3).  If `w(y)=1/(6y)`, then

\[
\frac d{dy}\left(y^2V(w(y))\right)
=2yV(w)-\frac16V'(w).
\]

At the fixed point `w(y_0)=y_0`,

\[
2y_0V(y_0)-\frac16V'(y_0)
=\frac16V'(y_0)+P'(y_0).
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
V(y_0)-\frac1{\sqrt6}V'(y_0)
=\frac{P'(y_0)}{2y_0}.
}
\tag{2.7}
\]

Now

\[
P'(y)
=28y\zeta(3)
+\pi^3\left(-\frac{10}{3}y^2-\frac5{162y^2}\right).
\]

At `y_0=1/sqrt6`, division by `2y_0` gives

\[
\boxed{
V(y_0)-\frac1{\sqrt6}V'(y_0)
=14\zeta(3)-\frac{20\pi^3}{9\sqrt6}.
}
\tag{2.8}
\]

Thus the proposed CM constant has the correct sign and normalization.

# 3. Fricke fixed point, `E_y/E`, and the branch value `rho`

Write, along the imaginary axis,

\[
q=e^{-2\pi y},
\]

\[
t(y)=
\left(
\frac{\eta(iy)\eta(6iy)}
     {\eta(2iy)\eta(3iy)}
\right)^{12},
\]

and

\[
E(y)=
\frac{\eta(2iy)^7\eta(3iy)^7}
     {\eta(iy)^5\eta(6iy)^5}.
\]

For `a>0`, Dedekind eta gives on the positive imaginary axis

\[
\eta\left(\frac{i}{ay}\right)=\sqrt{ay}\,\eta(iay),
\tag{3.1}
\]

with no phase ambiguity because both sides are positive real.

Applying (3.1) to the four eta factors gives the two exact Fricke transformations

\[
\boxed{t(1/(6y))=t(y),}
\tag{3.2}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{E(1/(6y))=6y^2E(y).}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Differentiate (3.3) at `y_0`.  Since

\[
\left.\frac d{dy}\frac1{6y}\right|_{y_0}=-1,
\]

we get

\[
-E_y(y_0)=12y_0E(y_0)+E_y(y_0).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\frac{E_y(y_0)}{E(y_0)}=-6y_0=-\sqrt6.
}
\tag{3.4}
\]

This is the sign needed in the amplitude ratio.

The same Fricke invariance gives `t_y(y_0)=0`.  To identify the branch value, use the exact level-6 identity already established in Q7621,

\[
H=q\frac{t_q}{t}=F(t)\sqrt{\Delta(t)}.
\tag{3.5}
\]

Since `q=e^{-2pi y}`,

\[
\frac{t_y}{t}=-2\pi H.
\tag{3.6}
\]

At the fixed point, `t_y=0`, while `F=E` is finite and nonzero; hence

\[
\Delta(t(y_0))=0.
\]

The two roots are

\[
\rho=17-12\sqrt2,
\qquad
\rho^{-1}=17+12\sqrt2.
\]

On the positive imaginary axis

\[
t(q)=\frac q{A(q)},
\qquad
A(q)=\prod_{3\nmid m}(1+q^m)^{12}>1,
\]

so `0<t(y_0)<q_0<1`.  Therefore the branch value is the small root:

\[
\boxed{t(y_0)=\rho.}
\tag{3.7}
\]

# 4. Local square-root coordinate and the coefficient `f_1`

Put

\[
x=1-\frac t\rho,
\qquad
u=\sqrt{x},
\tag{4.1}
\]

with `u>0` on the real branch `y>y_0`.

Since the roots of `Delta` are `rho` and `rho^{-1}`,

\[
\Delta(t)
=(1-t/\rho)(1-\rho t).
\]

With `t=rho(1-u^2)`, this is exactly

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=u^2(d_1+\rho^2u^2),
\qquad
d_1:=1-\rho^2.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

The quadratic equation for `rho` gives

\[
1+\rho^2=34\rho,
\]

hence

\[
\boxed{
d_1=1-\rho^2=2(1-17\rho).}
\tag{4.3}
\]

In particular `d_1>0` and `1-17rho>0`.

From (3.5)--(3.6),

\[
t_y=-2\pi tF\sqrt\Delta.
\]

But `t=rho(1-u^2)`, so `t_y=-2rho u u_y`.  Cancelling the common factor on the real branch gives

\[
\boxed{
 u_y
=\pi(1-u^2)
F(\rho(1-u^2))
\sqrt{d_1+\rho^2u^2}.
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

Let

\[
E_0:=E(y_0)=F(\rho)>0.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{u_y(y_0^+)=\pi E_0\sqrt{d_1}.}
\tag{4.5}
\]

This proves that the ramification is simple, not merely even-order.

Because the modular functions are analytic in the local `y`-coordinate and `t` is a simple quadratic quotient coordinate, `F` has a genuine Puiseux expansion with no logarithms:

\[
F(t)=f_0+f_1u+f_2u^2+f_3u^3+\cdots,
\qquad f_0=E_0.
\tag{4.6}
\]

Since `F(t(y))=E(y)`, differentiation at `y_0` gives

\[
E_y(y_0)=f_1u_y(y_0^+).
\]

Use (3.4) and (4.5):

\[
\boxed{
 f_1=-\frac{\sqrt6}{\pi\sqrt{d_1}}.
}
\tag{4.7}
\]

This exactly matches the coefficient stated in the question.

As a by-product, standard transfer already yields the leading Apéry asymptotic

\[
\boxed{
 b_n
\sim
\frac{\sqrt6}{2\pi^{3/2}\sqrt{d_1}}
\rho^{-n}n^{-3/2}.
}
\tag{4.8}
\]

Indeed `Gamma(-1/2)=-2sqrt(pi)` and `f_1<0`.

# 5. The limiting ratio `kappa_n/b_n`

The Eichler generating series is

\[
K(t)=E(q(t))U(q(t)).
\]

Along the imaginary axis,

\[
K(t(y))=E(y)V(y).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Because `E` and `V` are analytic at `y_0`, `K` has the same pure Puiseux structure

\[
K(t)=k_0+k_1u+k_2u^2+k_3u^3+\cdots.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Differentiating (5.1) at `y_0`,

\[
K_y=E_yV+EV'.
\]

Since `k_1=K_y/u_y` and `f_1=E_y/u_y`,

\[
\frac{k_1}{f_1}
=\frac{K_y}{E_y}
=V+\frac{E}{E_y}V'.
\]

By (3.4), `E/E_y=-1/sqrt6`.  Hence

\[
\boxed{
\frac{k_1}{f_1}
=V(y_0)-\frac1{\sqrt6}V'(y_0)
=C_*.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Combining with (2.8),

\[
\boxed{
C_*=14\zeta(3)-\frac{20\pi^3}{9\sqrt6}.
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

The modular parametrization supplies a dented `t`-domain at the unique dominant branch value `rho`: the level-6 `t`-map is degree two, and the other finite branch value is `rho^{-1}>1`.  The local `y`-uniformizer also proves that there are no logarithmic terms at `rho` for `F` or `K`.

Therefore the usual singularity-transfer theorem applies, and the leading nonanalytic amplitudes give

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\kappa_n}{b_n}\longrightarrow C_*.
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

This is a theorem, not a numerical extrapolation.

# 6. First correction: exact operator calculation

Set

\[
W(t):=K(t)-C_*F(t).
\tag{6.1}
\]

By construction of `C_*`, the `u=x^{1/2}` coefficient cancels:

\[
W=w_0+w_2x+w_3x^{3/2}+O(x^2).
\tag{6.2}
\]

There may be analytic integer powers; they do not affect the singular coefficient we are about to match.

Since `L F=0`, equation (0.4) gives

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal LW
=-5(\mathscr C-1)-t,
\qquad
\mathscr C=\frac1{F^2\sqrt\Delta}.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

From (4.2) and `F=E_0+O(x^{1/2})`,

\[
\mathscr C(t)
=\frac1{E_0^2\sqrt{d_1}}x^{-1/2}+O(1).
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal LW
=-\frac5{E_0^2\sqrt{d_1}}x^{-1/2}+O(1).
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

## 6.1 Indicial coefficient, with sign

Expand the operator:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L
=\Delta\theta^3
+3t(t-17)\theta^2
+3t(t-9)\theta
+t(t-5).
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

Since `t=rho(1-x)`,

\[
\theta=-(1-x)\frac d{dx}.
\]

Only the first two terms of (6.5) contribute to the most singular power `x^{lambda-2}`.  Also

\[
\Delta=d_1x+O(x^2),
\]

and

\[
3\rho(\rho-17)
=3(17\rho-1)
=-3(1-17\rho).
\]

Using `d_1=2(1-17rho)` gives

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L(x^\lambda)
=I(\lambda)x^{\lambda-2}+O(x^{\lambda-1}),
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

with

\[
\boxed{
I(\lambda)
=-\lambda(\lambda-1)(2\lambda-1)(1-17\rho).
}
\tag{6.7}
\]

Thus the local exponents are `0,1/2,1`, as expected.

At `lambda=3/2`,

\[
I(3/2)=-\frac32(1-17\rho).
\tag{6.8}
\]

The analytic terms in (6.2) cannot produce `x^{-1/2}`.  Matching (6.4) therefore gives

\[
I(3/2)w_3
=-\frac5{E_0^2\sqrt{d_1}}.

Hence

\[
\boxed{
 w_3=k_3-C_*f_3
=\frac{10}{3E_0^2\sqrt{d_1}(1-17\rho)}.
}
\tag{6.9}
\]

This is exactly the value stated in the question.

# 7. Transfer to the `1/n` correction

For fixed nonintegral `beta`,

\[
[t^n](1-t/\rho)^\beta
=\frac{\rho^{-n}n^{-\beta-1}}{\Gamma(-\beta)}
\left(1+\frac{\beta(\beta+1)}{2n}+O(n^{-2})\right).
\tag{7.1}
\]

In particular,

\[
\frac{[t^n]x^{3/2}}{[t^n]x^{1/2}}
=\frac{\Gamma(-1/2)}{\Gamma(-3/2)}\frac1n
+O(n^{-2}).
\]

Since

\[
\Gamma(-1/2)=-2\sqrt\pi,
\qquad
\Gamma(-3/2)=\frac{4\sqrt\pi}{3},
\]

we get

\[
\boxed{
\frac{[t^n]x^{3/2}}{[t^n]x^{1/2}}
=-\frac3{2n}+O(n^{-2}).
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Now

\[
\kappa_n-C_*b_n
=w_3[t^n]x^{3/2}+O(\rho^{-n}n^{-7/2}),
\]

while

\[
b_n=f_1[t^n]x^{1/2}
\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right).
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{\kappa_n}{b_n}
=C_*
-\frac32\frac{w_3}{f_1}\frac1n
+O(n^{-2}).
\tag{7.3}
\]

Substituting (4.7) and (6.9),

\[
-\frac32\frac{w_3}{f_1}
=\frac{5\pi}{\sqrt6\,E_0^2(1-17\rho)}.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\kappa_n}{b_n}
=C_*+
\frac{5\pi}{\sqrt6\,E_0^2(1-17\rho)}\frac1n
+O(n^{-2}).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Every factor in `c_1` is positive, so

\[
\boxed{c_1>0.}
\tag{7.5}
\]

Hence the rational ratios approach `C_*` from above for all sufficiently large `n`.

### Why the `O(n^-2)` is legitimate

This is not obtained merely by writing one Frobenius term.  The modular `y`-uniformizer gives convergent pure Puiseux series

\[
F=\sum_{j\ge0}f_ju^j,
\qquad
K=\sum_{j\ge0}k_ju^j,
\qquad u^2=x.
\]

After the `u` term of `K-C_*F` is killed, the next nonanalytic term is `u^3=x^{3/2}`, and the next one is `u^5=x^{5/2}`.  Integer powers of `x` are analytic across the local branch point and do not contribute at the same `rho^{-n}` algebraic scale.  The `x^{5/2}` term is two powers of `n` below the leading `x^{1/2}` term.  This is precisely what yields the remainder `O(n^-2)` in the ratio.

# 8. Denominator of `kappa_n`: a direct proof of `D_n | lcm(1,...,n)^3`

Write the reduced Eichler coefficient as

\[
\kappa_n=\frac{A_n}{D_n},
\qquad
\gcd(A_n,D_n)=1,
\qquad D_n>0.
\tag{8.1}
\]

The stronger denominator statement in the question is correct:

\[
\boxed{D_n\mid L_n^3,
\qquad L_n:=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n).}
\tag{8.2}
\]

There is a short proof directly from the Eichler `q`-series.

First,

\[
S(q):=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{q^m}{m^3(1-q^m)}
=\sum_{N\ge1}\sigma_{-3}(N)q^N.
\]

Since

\[
\sigma_{-3}(N)=\frac{\sigma_3(N)}{N^3},
\]

we have

\[
N^3[q^N]S(q)\in\mathbf Z.
\]

For `S(q^d)`, the coefficient of `q^N` is zero unless `d|N`, and otherwise is `sigma_{-3}(N/d)`, whose denominator divides `(N/d)^3`, hence divides `N^3`.

Therefore the coefficients of

\[
U(q)=-36S(q)+6S(q^2)-4S(q^3)+6S(q^6)
\]

satisfy

\[
\boxed{N^3[q^N]U(q)\in\mathbf Z.}
\tag{8.3}
\]

The level-6 period `E(q)` has integral `q`-coefficients.  Hence the coefficient of `q^N` in `E(q)U(q)` has denominator dividing

\[
\operatorname{lcm}_{1\le m\le N}m^3=L_N^3.
\tag{8.4}
\]

Finally,

\[
t(q)=\frac q{A(q)},
\qquad
A(q)\in1+q\mathbf Z[[q]],
\]

so

\[
t(q)\in q+q^2\mathbf Z[[q]].
\]

The compositional inverse of a power series over `Z` with linear coefficient `1` again lies in `t Z[[t]]`; this follows recursively, since each new inverse coefficient occurs with coefficient `1`.  Thus

\[
q(t)\in t\mathbf Z[[t]].
\tag{8.5}
\]

Composition with `q(t)` cannot introduce new denominators.  Therefore

\[
K(t)=E(q(t))U(q(t))
=\sum_{n\ge0}\kappa_nt^n
\]

has

\[
D_n\mid L_n^3.
\]

This proves (8.2) without any finite calculation.

# 9. Exact denominator defect and the large-prime target

Let

\[
g_n:=\gcd(A_n,b_n).
\tag{9.1}
\]

Because `A_n/D_n` is reduced,

\[
\gcd(A_n,D_n)=1.
\]

Hence

\[
\gcd(A_n,D_nb_n)=\gcd(A_n,b_n)=g_n.
\]

Therefore the reduced denominator of the rational ratio is exactly

\[
\boxed{
Q_n:=\operatorname{den}\left(\frac{\kappa_n}{b_n}\right)
=\frac{D_nb_n}{g_n}.
}
\tag{9.2}
\]

This is the formula stated in the question.

For a prime `p>n`, (8.2) gives `p not dividing D_n`, so

\[
\boxed{
 v_p(Q_n)
=v_p(b_n)-\min\{v_p(A_n),v_p(b_n)\}.
}
\tag{9.3}
\]

Thus the target prime condition has an exact denominator-defect interpretation:

\[
\boxed{
 p>n,\ p\mid g_n
\iff
v_p(Q_n)<v_p(b_n).
}
\tag{9.4}
\]

Equivalently, `R_n` is the radical of the primes larger than `n` at which the denominator of `kappa_n/b_n` has lost at least one copy of the corresponding factor of `b_n`.

This formulation is useful conceptually, but it also exposes why a generic irrationality measure is too coarse: an irrationality measure sees the **total size of `Q_n`**, while the target asks for a prime-restricted comparison between `Q_n` and `b_n`.  The factor `D_n`, supported entirely on primes at most `n`, can already contribute exponential denominator height without saying anything about `R_n`.

# 10. Fixed-order Richardson: exact cancellation and denominator bookkeeping

The local Puiseux expansion gives, for every fixed integer `H`, a full coefficient-ratio asymptotic

\[
\boxed{
 r_n:=\frac{\kappa_n}{b_n}
=C_*+\frac{c_1}{n}+\frac{c_2}{n^2}+\cdots
+\frac{c_H}{n^H}+O_H(n^{-H-1}).
}
\tag{10.1}
\]

Only fixed `H` is asserted here.  No uniformity in `H` is being smuggled in.

For an integer `h>=1` and `n>h`, use the **backward** Richardson operator

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_h(n)
:=\frac1{h!}\sum_{j=0}^h
(-1)^j\binom hj (n-j)^h r_{n-j}.
}
\tag{10.2}
\]

Backward indices are preferable for the present arithmetic target because all source indices are at most `n`, so every Eichler denominator divides the single small-prime number `L_n^3`.

The finite-difference identities are

\[
\frac1{h!}\sum_{j=0}^h
(-1)^j\binom hj(n-j)^h=1,
\tag{10.3}
\]

and, for `1<=k<=h`,

\[
\frac1{h!}\sum_{j=0}^h
(-1)^j\binom hj(n-j)^{h-k}=0.
\tag{10.4}
\]

Thus the first `h` inverse-power corrections cancel.

A proof-quality detail is worth recording: because the Richardson weights have size `O_h(n^h)`, an expansion only through `n^{-h}` is not enough to control the transformed remainder.  To prove the expected error one may use (10.1) through order `2h`:

\[
r_m=C_*+\sum_{k=1}^{2h}c_km^{-k}+O_h(m^{-2h-1}).
\]

Then the terms `k<=h` vanish by (10.4); the `h`-th finite difference of `m^{h-k}` for `k>=h+1` is `O_h(n^{-k})`; and the weighted final remainder is

\[
O_h(n^h n^{-2h-1})=O_h(n^{-h-1}).
\]

Therefore, for each **fixed** `h`,

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_h(n)=C_*+O_h(n^{-h-1}).
}
\tag{10.5}
\]

This is rigorous.

## 10.1 Target-safe common denominator

For `m<=n`, write

\[
r_m=\frac{P_m}{Q_m}
\]

in lowest terms.  By (9.2),

\[
Q_m=\frac{D_mb_m}{g_m},
\qquad
D_m\mid L_n^3.
\]

Set

\[
B_m:=\frac{b_m}{g_m}.
\]

Then

\[
Q_m\mid L_n^3 B_m.
\]

Consequently a valid common denominator for the unreduced Richardson combination is

\[
\boxed{
\widehat Q_{n,h}
:=h!\,L_n^3\prod_{j=0}^h B_{n-j}.
}
\tag{10.6}
\]

If `mathcal Q_{n,h}` denotes the **reduced** denominator of `mathcal R_h(n)`, then

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal Q_{n,h}\mid\widehat Q_{n,h}.
}
\tag{10.7}
\]

This product denominator preserves the target in the only safe sense available: it first removes `g_m` from every `b_m`, and its extra factor `h!L_n^3` is supported on primes at most `n`.  Thus no prime `p>n` is reintroduced by the known Eichler denominator or the Richardson weight denominator.

A prime `p>n` removed from one factor `B_m` can still occur in another factor `B_{m'}`.  Therefore one must **not** read (10.6) as a pointwise prime detector.  What is preserved exactly is the aggregate size identity

\[
\prod_{j=0}^h g_{n-j}
=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^h b_{n-j}}
       {\prod_{j=0}^h B_{n-j}}.
\]

Since (10.7) implies

\[
\prod_{j=0}^h B_{n-j}
\ge
\frac{\mathcal Q_{n,h}}{h!L_n^3},
\]

we obtain the exact target-safe inequality

\[
\boxed{
\prod_{j=0}^h g_{n-j}
\le
\frac{h!L_n^3\prod_{j=0}^h b_{n-j}}
     {\mathcal Q_{n,h}}.
}
\tag{10.8}
\]

In particular

\[
\boxed{
R_n\le g_n\le\prod_{j=0}^h g_{n-j},
}
\tag{10.9}
\]

so any genuinely strong lower bound on `mathcal Q_{n,h}` would transfer to the desired target.  The problem is quantitative size, not logical target loss.

# 11. Why a fixed-order irrationality measure gives no radical gain

Suppose, conditionally, that `C_*` were known irrational with a conventional irrationality measure: there exist fixed `mu<infinity` and `c>0` such that, for all relevant reduced rationals `P/Q`,

\[
\left|C_*-\frac PQ\right|\ge cQ^{-\mu}.
\tag{11.1}
\]

Apply this to `mathcal R_h(n)`.  For fixed `h`, (10.5) gives

\[
\left|C_*-\mathcal R_h(n)\right|
\le C_h n^{-h-1}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\log\mathcal Q_{n,h}
\ge \frac{h+1}{\mu}\log n+O_h(1).
}
\tag{11.2}
\]

This is only logarithmic in `n`.

On the other hand, let

\[
\alpha=\rho^{-1}=17+12\sqrt2.
\]

From (4.8),

\[
\log b_m=m\log\alpha+O(\log m).
\tag{11.3}
\]

Also

\[
\log L_n=\psi(n)=n+o(n).
\tag{11.4}
\]

Hence for fixed `h`, the logarithm of the numerator in (10.8) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\log h!+3\log L_n+
\sum_{j=0}^h\log b_{n-j}\\
&\qquad=
\left(3+(h+1)\log\alpha\right)n+o(n).
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.5}
\]

Subtracting the lower bound (11.2) changes this only by `O(log n)`.  Thus the generic irrationality-measure argument gives at best an exponential upper bound for the gcd product, not `exp(o(n))`.

Already at `h=0`, the phenomenon is visible in

\[
Q_n=\frac{D_nb_n}{g_n}.
\]

A polynomial lower bound for `Q_n` can be supplied entirely by the small-prime factor `D_n`; it need not force any large-prime factor to survive in `b_n/g_n`.

So fixed Richardson order does not cross the height scale needed for `R_n`.

# 12. Growing Richardson order: two independent barriers

It is tempting to take `h=h(n)` so that

\[
n^{-h}=\exp(-h\log n)
\]

becomes exponentially small.  There are two separate reasons this is not presently a theorem route.

## 12.1 Analytic barrier: no uniform high-order remainder has been proved

The singularity analysis proves (10.1) for every **fixed** `H`.  It does not, by itself, give a bound uniform for

\[
H=H(n)\to\infty.
\]

To justify (10.5) with growing `h`, one needs uniform control of the Puiseux coefficients and of the transfer remainder through at least order `2h`, because the Richardson weights grow like `n^h`.

The local Puiseux series is convergent in its local variable, but that fact alone is not a ready-made uniform Richardson theorem: high-order coefficient extraction also contains the large-order gamma-ratio expansion, and the constants in the transferred asymptotic have to be tracked.  No such uniform estimate is currently established in the same-project derivation.

Therefore an assertion such as

\[
\mathcal R_{h(n)}(n)-C_*=O(n^{-h(n)-1})
\]

would currently be an extra theorem, not a consequence already proved above.

## 12.2 Arithmetic barrier even if one grants ideal uniformity

Suppose generously that a uniform estimate of Richardson type is available and that (11.1) holds with fixed `mu`.

Then the best generic denominator lower bound has scale

\[
\log\mathcal Q_{n,h}
\gtrsim \frac{h}{\mu}\log n
\]

up to the logarithm of the uniform remainder constant.

But the target-safe carrier (10.8) has

\[
\sum_{j=0}^h\log b_{n-j}
=\log\alpha\left((h+1)n-\frac{h(h+1)}2\right)
+O(h\log n).
\tag{12.1}
\]

For `h=o(n)`, this is

\[
\Theta(hn).
\]

The weight denominator contributes

\[
\log h!=O(h\log h),
\]

and the shared Eichler denominator contributes only

\[
3\log L_n=3n+o(n),
\]

but the product of the `b`-factors is still of height `exp(Theta(hn))`.

Thus the comparison is

\[
\boxed{
\text{irrationality information: } O(h\log n),
\qquad
\text{target-safe denominator height: } \Theta(hn)+O(n).
}
\tag{12.2}
\]

The ratio of these scales is `O(log n/n)`.

For the commonly suggested choice

\[
h\asymp\frac n{\log n},
\]

an ideal Richardson error would be `exp(-Theta(n))`, and a power-law irrationality measure would give only

\[
\log\mathcal Q_{n,h}=\Omega(n),
\]

whereas the product carrier has

\[
\log\widehat Q_{n,h}=\Theta(n^2/\log n).
\]

For `h=Theta(n)`, the comparison becomes `Omega(n log n)` versus `Theta(n^2)`.

So growing the Richardson order does not repair the denominator-height mismatch in this architecture.

# 13. The transcendence input is not currently available anyway

The limiting constant is

\[
C_*=14\zeta(3)-\frac{20}{9\sqrt6}\pi^3.
\tag{13.1}
\]

Apéry's theorem gives `zeta(3)` irrational, and Lindemann--Weierstrass gives `pi` (hence `pi^3`) transcendental.  These two facts **do not imply** that the particular algebraic linear combination (13.1) is irrational.  They provide no linear independence theorem for `1`, `zeta(3)`, and `pi^3`.

Accordingly, this proof does not assume even irrationality of `C_*`, much less an irrationality measure.  Any use of (11.1) above is explicitly conditional and is included only to show that **even such a conventional measure would still be quantitatively too weak for the radical target through the standard Richardson/common-denominator route**.

This is the correct level of caution.  One should not replace it by the invalid inference

```text
zeta(3) irrational + pi transcendental
    => 14*zeta(3) - algebraic*pi^3 irrational.
```

No such implication exists.

# 14. Sharply scoped no-go theorem

The preceding estimates can be summarized as follows.

> **Theorem (Richardson/denominator no-go, scoped).**
> Let
> \[
> r_n=\kappa_n/b_n,
> \qquad
> \kappa_n=A_n/D_n
> \]
> in lowest terms, with `D_n | L_n^3`, and let
> \[
> g_n=\gcd(A_n,b_n).
> \]
> For every fixed `h`, the backward Richardson combination (10.2) satisfies
> \[
> \mathcal R_h(n)=C_*+O_h(n^{-h-1}),
> \]
> and its reduced denominator `mathcal Q_{n,h}` divides
> \[
> h!L_n^3\prod_{j=0}^h\frac{b_{n-j}}{g_{n-j}}.
> \]
> Therefore
> \[
> R_n
> \le
> \frac{h!L_n^3\prod_{j=0}^h b_{n-j}}
>      {\mathcal Q_{n,h}}.
> \]
> If one adds only a fixed power-law irrationality measure
> \[
> |C_*-P/Q|\gg Q^{-\mu},
> \]
> then for fixed `h` the resulting lower bound on `mathcal Q_{n,h}` is only polynomial in `n`, while the right-hand carrier has exponential height.  If `h` grows, even granting the unproved uniform Richardson remainder, the denominator information grows on the scale `O(h log n)` while the target-safe product carrier grows on the scale `Theta(hn)+O(n)`.  Hence these ingredients alone do not imply `log R_n=o(n)`.

The hypotheses of this no-go are deliberate.  It does **not** exclude a proof that adds, for example,

* a direct lower bound for the **prime-`>n` part** of the reduced denominator rather than for its total size;
* a special adjacent-index gcd theorem that replaces the product of `b_{n-j}` by a subexponential common carrier;
* a Padé/linear-form construction whose denominator is essentially `b_n/g_n` rather than a product over a Richardson window;
* a new arithmetic identity converting the denominator defect into a fixed-height resultant or norm;
* or genuinely new transcendence information adapted to the specific CM period combination.

What is ruled out is the generic slogan

```text
very good Richardson approximation to C_*
    + irrationality measure for C_*
    => small large-prime gcd.
```

That implication loses the relevant prime support and the relevant height scale.

# 15. Warning about `J` recurrences and source vanishing

The recent same-project Cartier/Eichler work makes one final logical distinction important.

A three-term recurrence or Green identity has the schematic form

\[
A_rJ_{r+1}+B_rJ_r+C_rJ_{r-1}=S_r.
\tag{15.1}
\]

From a single modular zero

\[
J_r\equiv0\pmod p
\]

one cannot infer

\[
S_r\equiv0\pmod p,
\]

because the neighboring terms remain.  Even if enough framed coordinates vanish at one row to force one specialized source residue to vanish modulo `p`, that is a statement at **one characteristic and one row**; it is not the characteristic-zero identity `S_r=0`, and certainly not the vanishing of the entire source sequence.

This matters particularly for the second-neighbor constructions in Q7654: `J_2` is a second Frobenius/Eichler extension coordinate, not a discrete row derivative of `J_1`.  A zero of one coordinate is not evidence that the accumulated source has disappeared.

Accordingly, none of the CM asymptotic arguments above uses a finite `J` zero, a finite scan, or a presumed source vanishing.
