# The Apéry Wronskian as an S-unit equation: exact consequences and the obstruction

## Verdict

The Wronskian is extremely useful for **local valuation control**, but neither the classical Schmidt Subspace Theorem, the Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt theory of S-unit equations, nor smooth-number estimates turn it into a proof that

\[
G_n=\exp(o(n)).
\]

There are three decisive corrections to the proposed route.

1. There is no “divisibility or propagation” dichotomy. In the correct integral normalization one always has the stronger unconditional divisibility
   \[
   \boxed{G_nG_{n+1}\mid \Omega_n},
   \qquad
   \Omega_n:=\frac{6L_n^3L_{n+1}^3}{(n+1)^3},
   \]
   where \(L_n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n)\). In particular \(G_n\mid\Omega_n\) regardless of whether \(G_n\) divides either coordinate at time \(n+1\).

2. The fact that \(\Omega_n\) is supported on primes \(\le n\) does **not** make the four Apéry coordinates S-units in a fixed finite-rank multiplicative group. Their large prime factors are unrestricted, and the permitted set of small primes itself grows with \(n\).

3. Smooth-number theorems count smooth integers; they do not bound the size of an individual smooth integer. Indeed \(L_n^3=\exp(3n+o(n))\) and \(\Omega_n=\exp(6n+o(n))\) are themselves \(n\)-smooth. Even squarefree \(n\)-smooth integers can have logarithm \(n+o(n)\).

Most importantly, the exact Wronskian right-hand side contains essentially **every prime in the hard range** \((n/2,n)\), normally to the sixth power. Thus the Wronskian supplies the campaign’s valuation cap but no prime-support sparsity. Prime-support sparsity is precisely the missing Apéry-zero/cross-prime input.

\tag{0.2}
\]

## 1. The exact divisibility theorem

### Proposition 1

For every \(n\ge1\),

\[
\boxed{G_nG_{n+1}\mid \Omega_n.}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Consequently

\[
G_n\mid \gcd(\Omega_{n-1},\Omega_n)
\qquad(n\ge2).
\tag{1.2}
\]

### Proof

Write

\[
U_n=G_nx_n,\qquad V_n=G_ny_n,\qquad \gcd(x_n,y_n)=1,
\]

and similarly at \(n+1\). Then (0.2) becomes

\[
G_nG_{n+1}
\bigl(x_ny_{n+1}-x_{n+1}y_n\bigr)
=-\Omega_n.
\tag{1.3}
\]

The expression in parentheses is an integer, proving (1.1). Applying (1.1) at \(n-1\) and at \(n\) proves (1.2). ∎

Define the **primitive adjacent determinant**

\[
\delta_n:=x_ny_{n+1}-x_{n+1}y_n
=-\frac{\Omega_n}{G_nG_{n+1}}\in\mathbf Z\setminus\{0\}.
\tag{1.4}
\]

Thus the Wronskian gives exactly

\[
G_nG_{n+1}=\frac{\Omega_n}{|\delta_n|}.
\tag{1.5}
\]

The elementary lower bound \(|\delta_n|\ge1\) gives (1.1), and nothing stronger follows without a new lower bound for \(|\delta_n|\).

### The proposed propagation alternative is false

There is no exceptional case in which one must instead propagate \(G_n\) to \(G_{n+1}\). The two terms

\[
U_nV_{n+1},\qquad U_{n+1}V_n
\]

are both divisible by \(G_n\), simply because \(G_n\mid U_n,V_n\). Hence \(G_n\mid\Omega_n\) always.

Moreover, even the extra condition \(G_n\mid V_{n+1}\) does not imply \(G_n\mid G_{n+1}\): propagation would require divisibility of **both** \(U_{n+1}\) and \(V_{n+1}\).

### Tiny primitive determinants are arithmetically ordinary

A small \(\delta_n\) is not exceptional from the viewpoint of rational approximation. For example,

\[
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
 m&m-1\\
 m+1&m
\end{pmatrix}=1
\]

for every \(m\), although both rows are primitive and all four entries tend to infinity. Scaling the first row by \(g\) and the second by \(h\) produces determinant \(gh\). Hence a determinant identity alone is perfectly compatible with very large row gcds.

In continued-fraction language, consecutive reduced rationals frequently have cross-determinant \(1\). Therefore “the reduced Apéry vectors would have a small determinant” is not by itself a contradiction; it is the normal extremal geometry of rational approximation.

## 2. Exact size and prime factorization of the right-hand side

Let

\[
\psi(x):=\sum_{p^k\le x}\log p.
\]

Then \(\log L_n=\psi(n)\), so

\[
\boxed{
\log \Omega_n
=\log 6+3\psi(n)+3\psi(n+1)-3\log(n+1).
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

By the prime number theorem, \(\psi(x)=x+o(x)\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{\log\Omega_n=6n+o(n),\qquad \Omega_n=\exp(6n+o(n)).}
\tag{2.2}
\]

The factor \((n+1)^{-3}\) removes only \(O(\log n)\) from the logarithm. It does not alter the leading constant \(6\).

### Exact local valuation

For every prime \(p\),

\[
v_p(L_n)=\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
v_p(\Omega_n)
=v_p(6)
+3\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor
+3\lfloor\log_p(n+1)\rfloor
-3v_p(n+1).
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

This is the exact prime-factor constraint supplied by the Wronskian.

Also,

\[
\frac{L_{n+1}}{L_n}=
\begin{cases}
p,&n+1=p^k\text{ is a prime power},\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Hence

\[
\Omega_n=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle \frac{6L_n^6}{(n+1)^3},
&n+1\text{ is not a prime power},\\[1.1em]
\displaystyle \frac{6L_n^6}{p^{3(k-1)}},
&n+1=p^k.
\end{cases}
\tag{2.4}
\]

Apart from the fixed primes \(2,3\), every prime factor of \(\Omega_n\), and therefore of \(G_n\), is at most \(n\). If \(n+1\) itself is prime, its contribution from \(L_{n+1}^3\) is canceled exactly by the denominator \((n+1)^3\).

### The hard range is not removed

Let \(p\) be prime with

\[
\frac{n+1}{2}<p<n.
\]

Then \(p\nmid n(n+1)\), and all relevant base-\(p\) logarithmic floors equal \(1\). Thus

\[
\boxed{v_p(\Omega_{n-1})=v_p(\Omega_n)=6.}
\tag{2.5}
\]

Therefore even the stronger neighboring bound

\[
G_n\mid\gcd(\Omega_{n-1},\Omega_n)
\]

still permits **every** prime in the top window, to exponent \(6\). This is exactly consistent with the current campaign’s banked bound

\[
v_p(G_n)\le6\qquad(\sqrt n<p\le n).
\]

The logarithmic mass of the fully permitted top-window product is

\[
\log\prod_{n/2<p<n}p^6
=6\bigl(\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(n/2)\bigr)
=3n+o(n).
\tag{2.6}
\]

Even allowing each such prime only once gives

\[
\log\prod_{n/2<p<n}p
=\frac n2+o(n),
\tag{2.7}
\]

which is still far larger than \(o(n)\). Thus bounded valuations are not the missing theorem; one must prove that only a zero-density weighted subset of the eligible primes actually occurs.

For most \(n\) with no prime-power transition at \(n\) or \(n+1\), one can also see directly that

\[
\gcd(\Omega_{n-1},\Omega_n)
\asymp_{\{2,3\}}
\frac{6L_n^6}{n^3(n+1)^3},
\]

whose logarithm is still \(6n+o(n)\). Using both adjacent Wronskians therefore does not change the exponential scale.

## 3. Question 1: can the Schmidt Subspace Theorem be applied?

### Bilinearity is not the principal obstruction

The determinant is bilinear in the four coordinates, but one can introduce Segre monomials

\[
X_n:=U_nV_{n+1},\qquad Y_n:=U_{n+1}V_n.
\]

Then the Wronskian is the linear equation

\[
X_n-Y_n=-\Omega_n.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Equivalently, after division by \(\Omega_n\),

\[
\frac{X_n}{\Omega_n}-\frac{Y_n}{\Omega_n}=-1.
\tag{3.2}
\]

So the degree-two appearance can be linearized. The failure occurs after this step.

### Why the classical theorem gives no conclusion

There are four separate obstructions.

#### 1. The variables are not S-units

Let

\[
S_n:=\{p:p\le n\}\cup\{2,3\}.
\]

The number \(\Omega_n\) is an \(S_n\)-unit, but generally

\[
X_n/\Omega_n,\quad Y_n/\Omega_n
\]

are not. The Apéry values \(U_n,V_n,U_{n+1},V_{n+1}\) can contain arbitrary primes outside \(S_n\). Knowing that the **difference** of two integers is smooth does not make either integer smooth.

This is the most immediate hypothesis failure.

#### 2. The set of places is not fixed

Classical S-unit/Subspace-Theorem arguments work with a fixed finite set \(S\), or equivalently a fixed finite-rank multiplicative group. Here \(S_n\) expands with \(n\), with rank about

\[
|S_n|=\pi(n)+O(1)\sim\frac n{\log n}.
\]

Declaring every prime occurring in the Apéry coordinates to belong to \(S\) would make the assertion tautological, and the resulting group would vary with \(n\) and have uncontrolled rank.

#### 3. The points already lie in the exhibited hyperplane

In the monomial coordinates \((X_n,Y_n,\Omega_n)\), every point lies in the fixed hyperplane

\[
X-Y+\Omega=0.
\]

A Subspace-Theorem conclusion that the points lie in finitely many proper linear subspaces is therefore already satisfied by the defining relation itself. To obtain more, one would need a separate multiplicative nondegeneracy statement that excludes this hyperplane as the sole explanation. No such statement follows from the Wronskian.

#### 4. Common scalar factors are projectively invisible

The usual height geometry of the Subspace Theorem is projective. Replacing

\[
(U_n,V_n)\mapsto(cU_n,cV_n)
\]

leaves the projective point \([U_n:V_n]\) unchanged. But \(G_n\) is exactly such a common scalar factor. Thus a theorem applied only to the rational point \([a_n:b_n]\) cannot detect \(G_n\) at all.

The adjacent determinant reintroduces the scale, but its right-hand side already has size \(\exp(6n+o(n))\), exactly large enough to accommodate row factors of exponential size.

### Conclusion for Question 1

The bilinear form can be linearized, but the resulting points are not in a fixed S-unit group, the set of places varies, the displayed hyperplane is tautological, and projective height loses the gcd scalar. Therefore the classical Schmidt Subspace Theorem does not yield \(G_n=\exp(o(n))\) from (0.2).

## 4. Question 2: Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt and polynomial S-unit equations

The Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt theorem concerns nondegenerate solutions of

\[
a_1x_1+\cdots+a_mx_m=1
\]

with \((x_1,\dots,x_m)\) lying in a **fixed multiplicative subgroup of finite rank**. The number of solutions is bounded in terms of \(m\) and the group rank.

If \(X_n/\Omega_n\) and \(Y_n/\Omega_n\) really belonged to one fixed finite-rank group, then (3.2) would indeed be a two-variable S-unit equation. But they do not. The theorem does not say that two arbitrary integers whose difference is smooth must themselves have restricted arithmetic.

Allowing \(S=S_n\) does not repair the argument:

- the group changes at every \(n\);
- the Apéry coordinates still contain primes outside \(S_n\);
- the theorem counts solutions in a fixed group rather than bounding the height of one designated solution;
- there is only one Apéry tuple at each \(n\), so a finiteness bound gives no contradiction.

Generalizations to Laurent-polynomial or polynomial equations in S-units have the same foundational requirement: the variables must range in a fixed finite-rank torus, and toric degeneracies must be excluded. The determinant polynomial

\[
f(X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4)=X_1X_4-X_2X_3
\]

is syntactically eligible for such a discussion, but the Apéry tuple is not known to lie in the required torus. Moreover, the determinant has large positive-dimensional factorization fibers: even controlling the two products \(U_nV_{n+1}\) and \(U_{n+1}V_n\) would not recover the four factors or their row gcds.

### Why the Bugeaud–Corvaja–Zannier analogy stops here

For

\[
\gcd(a^n-1,b^n-1),
\]

the moving point \((a^n,b^n)\) lies in the fixed rank-two multiplicative subgroup generated by \((a,1)\) and \((1,b)\). Multiplicative independence supplies the needed nondegeneracy. That fixed-torus structure is what makes the Subspace Theorem effective.

The Apéry recurrence

\[
(n+1)^3u_{n+1}=P(n)u_n-n^3u_{n-1}
\]

is non-autonomous: its coefficients vary polynomially with \(n\). The values are not a finite power sum in fixed algebraic bases and are not known to lie in any fixed finitely generated multiplicative group. Results for constant-coefficient linear recurrences, such as Fuchs’s extensions of the Bugeaud–Corvaja–Zannier method, therefore do not apply merely because the Apéry sequence is holonomic.

### Conclusion for Question 2

The Wronskian is a polynomial equation, but it is **not an S-unit equation in the arithmetic sense required by Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt**. The missing fixed-rank multiplicative parametrization is not a technical detail; it is the central input of the classical method.

## 5. What the Wronskian does imply directly

The strongest immediate consequences are:

\[
G_nG_{n+1}\mid\Omega_n,
\tag{5.1}
\]

\[
G_n\mid\gcd(\Omega_{n-1},\Omega_n),
\tag{5.2}
\]

and the exact local cap

\[
v_p(G_n)\le
\min\{v_p(\Omega_{n-1}),v_p(\Omega_n)\}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

These are valuable. Combined with the Cartier–Frobenius block law and no-consecutive-zero information, they help identify the exact top-window valuations. But they do not determine **which primes** occur.

### Denominator-defect interpretation

Write the rational number \(a_n\) in lowest terms as

\[
a_n=\frac{A_n}{E_n},\qquad \gcd(A_n,E_n)=1,\qquad E_n\mid D_n,
\]

and put

\[
R_n:=\frac{D_n}{E_n}.
\]

Then exactly

\[
U_n=R_nA_n,\qquad V_n=R_nE_nb_n,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{G_n=R_n\gcd(A_n,b_n).}
\tag{5.4}
\]

In particular,

\[
R_n\mid G_n.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Thus an \(n\)-smooth factor is built into the problem before any accidental common prime between \(A_n\) and \(b_n\) is considered. The repository’s denominator-defect audit studies precisely this factor \(R_n\), and in the tested range reports \(\gcd(A_n,b_n)=1\).

This explains why smoothness cannot solve the problem: proving \(G_n=\exp(o(n))\) is substantially a theorem that the exact denominator \(E_n\) almost exhausts the universal clearing denominator \(D_n\). The Wronskian right-hand side is constructed from the same universal clearing denominators, so it naturally has enough smooth divisibility to absorb a large defect.

### The exact missing determinant statement

From (1.5), a Wronskian-only route would have to prove

\[
\log|\delta_n|=6n-o(n).
\tag{5.6}
\]

Then \(\log G_n+\log G_{n+1}=o(n)\) would follow. But \(\delta_n\) is the cross-determinant of the **reduced** adjacent Apéry fractions, and no general Diophantine principle forces such a determinant to be large. It may equal \(1\) for perfectly ordinary neighboring reduced fractions.

Therefore (5.6) is not a consequence of integrality, smoothness, recurrence order, or the usual Subspace Theorem. It would be a genuinely new Apéry-specific arithmetic theorem, essentially another formulation of the desired denominator-defect control.

## 6. Question 4: why smooth-number estimates do not help

### The Canfield–Erdős–Pomerance theorem was misapplied

The smooth-number function

\[
\Psi(x,y):=\#\{m\le x:P^+(m)\le y\}
\]

counts \(y\)-smooth integers up to \(x\). Canfield–Erdős–Pomerance obtain estimates, including a lower bound, for this **counting function**. Such estimates do not bound the largest \(y\)-smooth integer below \(x\).

Indeed, for every \(y\ge2\),

\[
2^{\lfloor\log_2x\rfloor}
\]

is \(y\)-smooth and lies in \((x/2,x]\). Hence the largest \(y\)-smooth integer below \(x\) is always at least \(x/2\). No power saving in the size of an individual smooth integer is possible from smoothness alone.

The expression \(x^{1-u+o(1)}\) in the question is therefore not a valid upper bound for the largest smooth integer. The Dickman–de Bruijn/CEP theory describes the density or number of smooth integers in suitable regimes, not a maximum-size obstruction.

### Explicit counterexamples at the exact Apéry scale

The two most relevant examples are already present:

\[
L_n^3=\exp(3n+o(n))
\]

is \(n\)-smooth, and

\[
\Omega_n=\exp(6n+o(n))
\]

is \(n\)-smooth. Thus “\(n\)-smooth and exponentially bounded” is completely consistent with exponential size \(e^{cn}\).

### Even bounded valuations are insufficient

Suppose hypothetically that

\[
v_p(G_n)\le C
\]

for every prime \(p\le n\), with a fixed constant \(C\). Then only

\[
\log G_n
\le C\sum_{p\le n}\log p
=C\vartheta(n)
=Cn+o(n)
\tag{6.1}
\]

follows. This remains linear in \(n\).

Even the squarefree number

\[
\operatorname{rad}(L_n)=\prod_{p\le n}p
\]

satisfies

\[
\log\operatorname{rad}(L_n)=\vartheta(n)=n+o(n).
\tag{6.2}
\]

Therefore the desired conclusion requires not merely bounded valuations but

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\mid G_n}\log p=o(n)
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

up to the already controlled valuation weights. This is a weighted prime-support theorem.

In the Apéry program, (6.3) is exactly the hard assertion that only a negligible weighted subset of the eligible primes produces simultaneous numerator/denominator cancellation. The Wronskian identifies the eligible primes but does not select among them.

### A subexponential number of divisors is not a size bound

One can estimate

\[
\log \tau(\Omega_n)=O\!\left(\frac n{\log n}\right).
\tag{6.4}
\]

For \(p>\sqrt n\), the exponent in \(\Omega_n\) is bounded, and there are \(O(n/\log n)\) such primes; smaller primes contribute less. Thus \(\Omega_n\) has only \(\exp(o(n))\) divisors.

But this does not imply that a particular divisor is small: \(\Omega_n\) itself is one of those divisors. Nor does it force repetition as \(n\) varies—the available divisor set changes with \(n\), and even \(\exp(o(n))\) possibilities vastly exceed the one value selected at a given index.

### Conclusion for Question 4

Smoothness, even together with an \(O(1)\) valuation cap and a subexponential divisor count, does not imply \(G_n=\exp(o(n))\). A support-sparsity theorem is indispensable.

## 7. A Diophantine-approximation cross-check

One might try to combine the reduced vectors with the exceptional quality of the Apéry approximants. This also does not close the problem.

Let

\[
\alpha=17+12\sqrt2,
\qquad \lambda=\log\alpha.
\]

Standard Apéry asymptotics give, at logarithmic precision,

\[
b_n=\exp(\lambda n+o(n)),
\qquad
\left|\zeta(3)-\frac{a_n}{b_n}\right|
=\exp(-2\lambda n+o(n)).
\tag{7.1}
\]

After reduction by \(G_n\), the denominator is

\[
q_n=\frac{D_nb_n}{G_n}.
\]

If \(\log G_n=g_nn\), then

\[
\log q_n=(3+\lambda-g_n)n+o(n).
\tag{7.2}
\]

Suppose one had an irrationality-measure lower bound

\[
\left|\zeta(3)-\frac pq\right|\ge q^{-\mu-o(1)}.
\]

Combining it with (7.1)–(7.2) gives only

\[
g_n\le 3+\lambda-\frac{2\lambda}{\mu}+o(1).
\tag{7.3}
\]

Even the hypothetical optimal exponent \(\mu=2\) yields merely

\[
\log G_n\le3n+o(n),
\]

not \(o(n)\). Since every irrational has irrationality exponent at least \(2\), an ordinary irrationality-measure argument cannot reach the target. This confirms, from a different direction, that the common scaling factor is too projectively invisible for standard Diophantine approximation to control.

## 8. Precise answers to the five questions

### 1. Schmidt Subspace Theorem

**No direct application proves the conjecture.** Bilinearity can be removed by passing to the two product coordinates, so bilinearity is not the real problem. The real failures are:

- the product coordinates are not S-units;
- the set of primes varies with \(n\);
- the points already lie in the displayed hyperplane;
- projective height does not see the common row factor \(G_n\).

### 2. Evertse–Schlickewei and polynomial S-unit equations

The determinant qualifies only **formally** as a polynomial/Laurent-polynomial relation. It does not qualify arithmetically for the finite-rank S-unit theorems because the Apéry values do not lie in a fixed finitely generated multiplicative group. The fixed-torus mechanism behind the Bugeaud–Corvaja–Zannier theorem is absent.

### 3. Direct use of the Wronskian

The exact conclusion is stronger and cleaner than the proposed dichotomy:

\[
\boxed{G_nG_{n+1}\mid\Omega_n.}
\]

There is no propagation alternative. This gives exact support and valuation caps, but

\[
\log\Omega_n=6n+o(n),
\]

and \(\Omega_n\) contains all hard-window primes to bounded positive exponent. Hence it does not imply a sublinear logarithm for \(G_n\).

### 4. Smooth-number bounds

They do not help pointwise. CEP-type results count smooth integers. An individual \(n\)-smooth integer can be essentially as large as any prescribed bound, and squarefree \(n\)-smooth integers already attain logarithmic size \(n+o(n)\). Bounded valuations still give only \(O(n)\), not \(o(n)\).

### 5. Exact size of the right-hand side

With \(L_n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n)\),

\[
\boxed{
\Omega_n=\frac{6L_n^3L_{n+1}^3}{(n+1)^3},
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\log\Omega_n
=\log6+3\psi(n)+3\psi(n+1)-3\log(n+1)
=6n+o(n),
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
v_p(\Omega_n)
=v_p(6)+3\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor
+3\lfloor\log_p(n+1)\rfloor-3v_p(n+1).}
\]

This is an exponential-size, small-prime-supported integer—not a subexponential one.

## 9. What a successful Wronskian enhancement would have to add

The Wronskian route could become decisive only after importing one of the following genuinely new inputs:

1. **Weighted support sparsity**
   \[
   \sum_{p\mid G_n}\log p=o(n);
   \]

2. **A large primitive-determinant theorem**
   \[
   \log|\delta_n|=6n-o(n);
   \]

3. **A fixed finite-rank multiplicative parametrization** of the Apéry vectors, together with a nondegeneracy theorem strong enough for a Corvaja–Zannier gcd-height estimate;

4. **An Apéry-specific cross-prime theorem** showing that the zero conditions selecting primes in the top and middle windows cannot align at positive weighted density.

Items 1, 2, and 4 are essentially reformulations of the same missing selectivity. The current Wronskian supplies the local exponent bound once a prime is selected; it does not prove that few primes are selected. In the language of the present campaign, the remaining issue is still the moving-index/cross-prime anti-concentration problem captured by LGTQ-3 and its variants.

## Final assessment

The Wronskian identity is not a hidden complete S-unit proof. It is already operating at its natural strength:

- it forbids primes outside the denominator-clearing support;
- it gives sharp or near-sharp local valuation caps;
- together with Apéry-specific congruences, it identifies the contribution of a selected prime.

But its right-hand side has logarithm \(6n+o(n)\) and retains the full dangerous prime interval. Standard S-unit and smooth-number theory cannot convert that information into \(\log G_n=o(n)\). A new theorem must control **prime selection/correlation**, not merely prime size or exponent.
