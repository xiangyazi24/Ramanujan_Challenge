## 2. The triangular condition has one folded root

Write

\[
\tau(u)=u(u+1).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\tau(n)-\tau(s)=(n-s)(n+s+1).}
\tag{2.1}
\]

For an odd prime `p`,

\[
\tau(n)\equiv\tau(s)\pmod p
\iff
s\equiv n\pmod p
\quad\text{or}\quad
s\equiv-1-n\pmod p.
\tag{2.2}
\]

If additionally `2s<p`, there is one representative in the folded interval. If `r=n mod p`, it is

\[
\boxed{\rho_p(n)=\min(r,p-1-r).}
\tag{2.3}
\]

The two roots of `X(X+1)=tau(n)` are `r` and `p-1-r`; they sum to `p-1`.

### Lemma 2.1 — one prime, one folded row

Let `p` be odd and let `s,t>=0` satisfy `2s<p` and `2t<p`. If

\[
p\mid\tau(n)-\tau(s),\qquad p\mid\tau(n)-\tau(t),
\]

then `s=t`.

**Proof.** Subtract:

\[
p\mid\tau(s)-\tau(t)=(s-t)(s+t+1).
\]

Now `|s-t|<p`, while `s+t+1<=p`; equality in the latter can occur only at `s=t=(p-1)/2`. Thus a factor can vanish modulo `p` only when `s=t`. ∎

This already rules out a same-characteristic two-row collision obtained solely by preserving the physical `n`.

### Quotient form

Equation (2.1) gives the exact alternatives

\[
n-s=qp\qquad\text{or}\qquad n+s+1=qp,
\tag{2.4}
\]

with `q>=1`. Since `p^3>n^2`, one has `p>n^(2/3)`, so these quotient digits are on the cube-root scale. That bookkeeping is useful, but it contributes no second Apéry equation.

## 3. The integral Racah/Newton row

Put

\[
\lambda_j=j(j+1)=\tau(j),\qquad
U_{j,k}=\binom jk\binom{j+k}{k}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Define

\[
\phi_k(Y)=
\frac{\prod_{a=0}^{k-1}(Y-\lambda_a)}{(k!)^2},
\qquad \phi_0=1,
\tag{3.2}
\]

and

\[
R_s(Y)=\sum_{k=0}^s U_{s,k}\phi_k(Y).
\tag{3.3}
\]

At `Y=lambda_j`,

\[
\prod_{a=0}^{k-1}(\lambda_j-\lambda_a)
=\prod_{a=0}^{k-1}(j-a)(j+a+1),
\]

hence

\[
\phi_k(\lambda_j)=
\begin{cases}
U_{j,k},&k\le j,\\
0,&k>j.
\end{cases}
\tag{3.4}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
R_s(\lambda_j)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(s,j)}U_{s,k}U_{j,k}
=R_j(\lambda_s).
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

In particular,

\[
\boxed{R_s(\lambda_s)=\sum_{k=0}^sU_{s,k}^2=b_s.}
\tag{3.6}
\]

For an integer certificate clear the basis denominators by defining

\[
\widehat R_s(Y)=(s!)^2R_s(Y).
\tag{3.7}
\]

Explicitly,

\[
\boxed{
\widehat R_s(Y)
=\sum_{k=0}^s
U_{s,k}\left(\frac{s!}{k!}\right)^2
\prod_{a=0}^{k-1}(Y-\lambda_a)
\in\mathbf Z[Y],
}
\tag{3.8}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{\widehat R_s(\lambda_s)=(s!)^2b_s.}
\tag{3.9}
\]

This is the canonical integral object for the common-physical-`n` resultant attack.

## 4. Main obstruction theorem: physical transport collapses exactly

### Theorem 4.1 — integral transport/remainder identity

For every `s>=0` there is `Qhat_s(Y) in Z[Y]` such that for every integer `n`,

\[
\boxed{
\widehat R_s(\lambda_n)
=(s!)^2b_s
 +(\lambda_n-\lambda_s)\widehat Q_s(\lambda_n).
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\gcd\!\left(\widehat R_s(\lambda_n),\lambda_n-\lambda_s\right)
=
\gcd\!\left((s!)^2b_s,\lambda_n-\lambda_s\right).
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

For every prime `p>s`, if `p|lambda_n-lambda_s`, then

\[
\boxed{
p\mid\widehat R_s(\lambda_n)\iff p\mid b_s,}
\tag{4.3}
\]

and, more strongly, multiplying `b_s` by `(s!)^2` does not alter any `p`-adic valuation in the common gcd.

**Proof.** Since `Rhat_s` is an integer polynomial, polynomial division by the monic linear factor `Y-lambda_s` gives

\[
\widehat R_s(Y)-\widehat R_s(\lambda_s)
=(Y-\lambda_s)\widehat Q_s(Y)
\]

with `Qhat_s in Z[Y]`. Insert (3.9), then set `Y=lambda_n`. Equation (4.2) is the elementary identity `gcd(A+BC,B)=gcd(A,B)`. If `p>s`, then `p∤s!`. ∎

### Corollary 4.2 — exact resultant collapse

With the convention `Res_Y(Y-a,F(Y))=F(a)`, one has

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_Y(Y-\lambda_s,\widehat R_s(Y))=(s!)^2b_s,
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_Y(Y-\lambda_n,\widehat R_s(Y))=\widehat R_s(\lambda_n),
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_Y(Y-\lambda_n,\widehat R_s)
-
\operatorname{Res}_Y(Y-\lambda_s,\widehat R_s)
=(\lambda_n-\lambda_s)\widehat Q_s(\lambda_n).
}
\tag{4.6}
\]

Thus the resultant that retains the physical `n` reduces, modulo the same triangular equation used to transport the point, to the original Apéry coefficient.

### Corollary 4.3 — application to `T_n`

Every prime counted in `T_n` satisfies `p>2s>s`. Therefore

\[
(p\mid b_s,\ p\mid\tau(n)-\tau(s))
\]

is equivalent prime-by-prime to

\[
(p\mid\widehat R_s(\tau(n)),\ p\mid\tau(n)-\tau(s)),
\]

including the `p`-adic multiplicity in the common gcd. A height bound for the latter object is therefore not a new certificate unless it uses additional information beyond these two equations.

## 5. The adjacent row is transverse at every `T_n` prime

The Racah rows satisfy

\[
(j+1)^3R_{j+1}(Y)
=\left(j^3+(j+1)^3+2(2j+1)Y\right)R_j(Y)
-j^3R_{j-1}(Y).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Let `P_j` denote the monic normalization. Comparing leading coefficients gives

\[
\boxed{
P_{j+1}(Y)
=
\left(Y+\frac{j^3+(j+1)^3}{2(2j+1)}\right)P_j(Y)
-
\beta_jP_{j-1}(Y),
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

where

\[
\boxed{\beta_j=\frac{j^6}{4(4j^2-1)}.}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Modulo `P_j`, if `alpha` is a root of `P_j`,

\[
P_{j+1}(\alpha)=-\beta_jP_{j-1}(\alpha).
\]

Taking products over all roots yields the adjacent-resultant recursion and hence

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}(P_s,P_{s-1})
=(-1)^{s(s-1)/2}
\prod_{j=1}^{s-1}
\left(\frac{j^6}{4(4j^2-1)}\right)^j.
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

Every prime in the numerator or denominator is at most `2s-1`. Therefore for an odd prime `p>2s`, the resultant is a `p`-adic unit.

### Theorem 5.1 — no adjacent companion

If

\[
p>2s,\qquad p\mid b_s,\qquad p\mid\tau(n)-\tau(s),
\]

then

\[
\boxed{p\mid R_s(\tau(n)),\qquad p\nmid R_{s-1}(\tau(n)).}
\tag{5.5}
\]

**Proof.** The first divisibility is Theorem 4.1. If the second also held, `tau(n)=tau(s)` would be a common root of `P_s` and `P_{s-1}` modulo `p`, contradicting the p-unit resultant (5.4). ∎

A two-row Wronskian, adjacent subresultant, or Christoffel-Darboux numerator whose target mechanism requires both rows to vanish therefore cannot collect the `T_n` prime.

## 6. Why fixed-depth cross-row resultants do not collect the triangular primes

There are two natural variants.

### 6.1 Same prime, two folded labels

Lemma 2.1 forbids this. Once a `T_n` prime is attached to `s`, the triangular equation cannot force the same prime onto another `t` with `2t<p`.

Thus `Res(R_s,R_t)` for `s!=t` receives no **forced** target divisibility from the triangular data. Such a divisibility would be an extra Apéry zero, not a consequence of the normal form.

### 6.2 A local stencil around the selected row

The first adjacent row is already a unit by Theorem 5.1. Hence the simplest fixed local determinant does not supply a second target equation.

Together, these facts give a theorem-level version of the one-primary obstruction: preserving the physical `n` has not converted the single zero in characteristic `p` into two algebraically dependent zeros that a low-height resultant can exploit.

## 7. The reflection midpoint can be discarded at logarithmic cost

This is a small unconditional pruning, not the main obstruction.

If `p=2s+1`, then

\[
4\tau(s)+1=(2s+1)^2\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

If also `p|tau(n)-tau(s)`, then

\[
(2n+1)^2=4\tau(n)+1\equiv0\pmod p,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{p\mid2n+1.}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Because a `T_n` prime also has `p>n^(2/3)`, for sufficiently large `n` there can be at most one such midpoint prime: the product of two would exceed `2n+1`. Its total contribution is `O(log n)`. Hence the midpoint degeneracy can be removed without affecting the target `o(n)` question.

## 10. What remains after the obstruction

The theorem above removes a class of candidate proofs; it does not itself bound `T_n`.

A successful theorem has to inject information absent from the one-characteristic pair

\[
(p\mid b_s,\ p\mid\tau(n)-\tau(s)).
\]

The surviving mechanisms are therefore genuinely new ones, for example:

1. a cross-prime estimate coupling several distinct `T_n` primes at the same physical `n`;
2. a first-primary invariant not forced by the mod-p Racah row, with aggregate logarithmic height below the target prime mass;
3. a bilinear/large-sieve estimate for the actual defining-characteristic coefficient-zero packets, uniform in the moving characteristic and quotient branch.

I do not prove any of these here, and I do not cite fixed-modulus, density-one, auxiliary-ell, or fixed-S theorems as substitutes for the required pointwise quantifier.

### Comparison with the current top-strip four-prime rectangle gateway

The present theorem is **not** a smaller sufficient theorem than the four-prime rectangle gateway. It is a negative theorem explaining why a plausible shortcut to avoid that gateway cannot work.

The size comparison is structural:

- the failed common-`n` resultant uses one characteristic and one folded label, optionally a fixed local adjacent stencil;
- Lemma 2.1 says one characteristic gives only one folded `T_n` row;
- Theorem 5.1 says the first adjacent row is a p-unit;
- a four-prime rectangle brings in several distinct characteristics and is not subject to same-prime folded-label uniqueness.

Thus the rectangle is genuinely beyond this obstruction. If proved, it would add cross-prime information that (A), (B), and (C) do not contain. Replacing it by `Rhat_s(tau(n))`, an adjacent Wronskian, or a level-6 evaluation resultant does not.

So the correct comparison is: **this obstruction is earlier than the rectangle, not stronger than it as a closing theorem.**
