# Kloosterman Mellin transforms, Stickelberger slopes, and what they do — and do not — imply for Apéry zeros

## Executive conclusion

There is no contradiction between

\[
\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}\chi(t)\,\operatorname{Kl}_2(1,t;p)=g(\chi)^2\ne0
\quad\text{in characteristic }0
\]

and the reduction of that algebraic integer being zero modulo a prime above \(p\). The crucial point is that a nontrivial Gauss sum is **not a \(p\)-adic unit**. Its complex absolute value is \(\sqrt p\), but Stickelberger/Gross--Koblitz gives a positive, generally fractional, valuation at primes above \(p\).

The precise answers are:

1. For the raw classical Kloosterman sum and the unnormalized multiplicative Mellin transform, the answer is exactly
   \[
   \boxed{M(\operatorname{Kl}_2)(\chi)=g(\chi)^2.}
   \]
   There is no factor \(p\) or \(1/p\). Signs, \(p^{-1/2}\), or \(1/(p-1)\) occur only when one changes the sheaf, weight, convolution, or Fourier-transform normalization.

2. One must distinguish three inequivalent meanings of “mod \(p\)”:

   \[
   \begin{array}{c|c}
   \text{reduction being tested}&\#\{\chi\ne1:g(\chi)^2\text{ vanishes}\}\\ \hline
   \bmod\mathfrak P\text{ (one maximal ideal / uniformizer)}&p-2\\
   \bmod p\text{ in one chosen local field }K_{\mathfrak P}&(p-1)/2\\
   \bmod p\mathcal O_K\text{ globally}&1
   \end{array}
   \]

   for every odd prime \(p\). The unique global zero is the quadratic character.

3. Consequently, an observed count near \(1.00\) agrees with the Gauss-square formula only if the computation tests **global divisibility by \(p\mathcal O_K\)** (or an equivalent coefficientwise cyclotomic reduction). It then predicts not merely an average but exactly one zero for every odd \(p\), at
   \[
   r=(p-1)/2.
   \]
   If the observed zero indices vary, then the computed object, character lift, or reduction ring is not the raw global Mellin transform above.

4. Gross--Koblitz can be applied termwise to the Apéry finite-field hypergeometric expression, but the Apéry coefficient is an **outer sum of \(p-1\) Gauss/Jacobi products**, not one product of four Gamma values. Stickelberger therefore gives the slope packets; the zero event is cancellation among equal-slope units. The exact project computation in `problems/3.2/apery_mellin_carry_polygon.tex` and `problems/3.2/ORACLE_COMM/chatgpt_q32_padic_horizontal_mellin.md` confirms this obstruction.

Thus Kloosterman is the model case where Mellin diagonalization leaves one product. The current Apéry formula does not collapse in that way.

## 1. Conventions

Let \(p\) be odd, let

\[
\psi(x)=\zeta_p^x,
\]

and define the raw two-variable Kloosterman sum by

\[
K(t)=\operatorname{Kl}_2(1,t;p)
:=\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p^\times}\psi\!\left(x+\frac{t}{x}\right).
\tag{1.1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
K(t)=\sum_{xy=t}\psi(x)\psi(y).
\tag{1.2}
\]

For a multiplicative character \(\chi:\mathbf F_p^\times\to\overline{\mathbf Q}^{\times}\), put

\[
g(\chi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p^\times}\chi(x)\psi(x).
\tag{1.3}
\]

The trivial character is denoted \(\mathbf1\). With this convention,

\[
g(\mathbf1)=-1.
\tag{1.4}
\]

For nontrivial \(\chi\),

\[
|g(\chi)|=\sqrt p,
\qquad
g(\chi)g(\chi^{-1})=\chi(-1)p.
\tag{1.5}
\]

The second identity is already a warning: it is \(g(\chi)g(\chi^{-1})\), not generally \(g(\chi)^2\), that is visibly \(p\) times a root of unity.

# (A) Exact Mellin normalization

## 2. The raw identity is \(g(\chi)^2\)

Starting from (1.2),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{t\ne0}\chi(t)K(t)
&=\sum_{t\ne0}\chi(t)\sum_{xy=t}\psi(x)\psi(y)\\
&=\sum_{x,y\ne0}\chi(xy)\psi(x)\psi(y)\\
&=\left(\sum_{x\ne0}\chi(x)\psi(x)\right)
  \left(\sum_{y\ne0}\chi(y)\psi(y)\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}\chi(t)\operatorname{Kl}_2(1,t;p)
=g(\chi)^2.}
\tag{2.1}
\]

There is no factor \(p\), \(1/p\), or \(p-1\) in (2.1).

For \(\chi=\mathbf1\), equation (2.1) gives

\[
\sum_{t\ne0}K(t)=g(\mathbf1)^2=1.
\tag{2.2}
\]

## 3. Where other normalizations come from

The common variants are as follows.

| Convention | Mellin value |
|---|---:|
| Raw \(K(t)\), transform \(\sum_t\chi(t)K(t)\) | \(g(\chi)^2\) |
| Transform using \(\chi^{-1}\) | \(g(\chi^{-1})^2\) |
| Averaged transform \((p-1)^{-1}\sum_t\chi(t)K(t)\) | \(g(\chi)^2/(p-1)\) |
| Katz rank-two Kloosterman sheaf with trace \(-K(t)\) | \(-g(\chi)^2\) |
| Weight-zero analytic normalization \(p^{-1/2}K(t)\) | \(p^{-1/2}g(\chi)^2\) |
| Both averaged and weight normalized | \(g(\chi)^2/((p-1)\sqrt p)\) |

The sheaf-theoretic sign comes from the cohomological shift used in the usual definition of the Kloosterman sheaf. A Tate/weight normalization contributes \(p^{-1/2}\). A harmonic-analysis normalization contributes \(1/(p-1)\). None of these is intrinsic to the raw sum (1.1).

A factor \(1/p\) appears only if one explicitly divides the raw Kloosterman trace or convolution by \(p\); it is not supplied by the convolution theorem itself.

## 4. The notation \(t^r\) must be interpreted correctly

Choose a generator \(g_0\) of \(\mathbf F_p^\times\). The complex multiplicative character indexed by \(r\) is

\[
\chi_r(g_0^m)=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i rm}{p-1}\right).
\tag{2.3}
\]

This is not the ordinary integer \([g_0^m]^r\). Thus the exact characteristic-zero identity is

\[
\sum_t\chi_r(t)K(t)=g(\chi_r)^2,
\tag{2.4}
\]

not, literally,

\[
\sum_t [t]^rK(t)=g(\chi_r)^2.
\]

If \(w_r(t)=[t]^r\) denotes the ordinary integer representative raised to the \(r\)-th power, exact character Fourier inversion instead gives

\[
\sum_t w_r(t)K(t)
=\frac1{p-1}\sum_{\chi}
\widehat w_r(\chi)\,g(\chi)^2,
\tag{2.5}
\]

where

\[
\widehat w_r(\chi)=\sum_{u\ne0}w_r(u)\chi(u)^{-1}.
\]

So a literal integer-power weight gives a linear combination of Gauss squares.

In the \(p\)-adic formulation, one uses the Teichmuller character

\[
\omega:\mathbf F_p^\times\longrightarrow\mu_{p-1}\subset\mathbf Z_p^\times,
\qquad \omega(t)\equiv t\pmod p.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Then \(\omega(t)^r\) reduces to \(t^r\in\mathbf F_p\) at the chosen \(p\)-adic prime. This is a **local specialization** of the character value; it is not the same as testing global divisibility in the full cyclotomic ring. Confusing those two operations is exactly capable of changing the zero count from \(1\) to \((p-1)/2\).

# (B) Stickelberger/Gross--Koblitz and the three meanings of “zero mod \(p\)”

## 5. The one-prime Gross--Koblitz formula

Fix a \(p\)-adic embedding and let \(\omega\) be the corresponding Teichmuller character. Choose \(\pi\) satisfying

\[
\pi^{p-1}=-p,
\qquad v_p(p)=1,
\qquad v_p(\pi)=\frac1{p-1}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

For \(0\le j\le p-2\), Gross--Koblitz gives

\[
\boxed{
g(\omega^{-j})=-\pi^j\Gamma_p\!\left(\frac{j}{p-1}\right).}
\tag{3.2}
\]

Morita's \(\Gamma_p\) is a \(p\)-adic unit on \(\mathbf Z_p\). Hence

\[
\boxed{
v_p(g(\omega^{-j}))=\frac{j}{p-1}.}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Equivalently, for \(1\le r\le p-2\),

\[
\omega^r=\omega^{-(p-1-r)}
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
v_p(g(\omega^r))=1-\frac{r}{p-1}.}
\tag{3.4}
\]

The trivial character is exceptional only in the sense that \(g(\mathbf1)=-1\) has valuation zero.

Thus the statement “a nontrivial Gauss sum is always a \(p\)-adic unit” is false. In fact, at the chosen prime every nontrivial Gauss sum has positive valuation.

## 6. Correction to the fractional-parts formula in the question

The scalar

\[
\sum_{a=1}^{d-1}\left\{\frac ad\right\}
=\frac{d-1}{2}
\tag{3.5}
\]

is not the valuation of one Gauss sum.

Suppose \(\chi\) has exact order \(d\), and at a chosen prime it is represented as

\[
\chi=\omega^{-(p-1)c/d},
\qquad (c,d)=1,\quad 1\le c<d.
\]

Then the valuation of this one Gauss sum is

\[
\boxed{v_p(g(\chi))=\frac cd.}
\tag{3.6}
\]

As the prime above \(p\) is varied through the primes arising from the character-value field, the slopes form the multiset

\[
\left\{\left\langle\frac{ac}{d}\right\rangle:
 a\in(\mathbf Z/d\mathbf Z)^\times\right\}
=
\left\{\frac ad:a\in(\mathbf Z/d\mathbf Z)^\times\right\}.
\tag{3.7}
\]

Their sum is \(arphi(d)/2\) for \(d>2\). If one instead sums over every \(a=1,\ldots,d-1\), including nonprimitive powers, one obtains (3.5). These sums describe an aggregate of valuations of conjugates or powers. Stickelberger's theorem is naturally a **group-ring/ideal factorization with one coefficient for each prime**, not one scalar valuation obtained by adding all coefficients.

## 7. Reduction modulo a maximal ideal: all nontrivial characters vanish

Let \(\mathfrak P\mid p\) be the selected maximal ideal, with uniformizer \(\pi\). From (3.2), for every nontrivial character \(j\ge1\), so

\[
g(\omega^{-j})\in(\pi).
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{g(\chi)^2\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak P}}
\tag{3.8}
\]

for all \(p-2\) nontrivial characters.

This requires only positive valuation. It is **not** equivalent to \(v_p(g(\chi))\ge1/2\).

## 8. Reduction modulo the rational prime in one local field: half the characters vanish

Inside the chosen local field,

\[
p\mathcal O_{K_{\mathfrak P}}=(\pi^{p-1}).
\]

Therefore

\[
g(\omega^{-j})^2\in p\mathcal O_{K_{\mathfrak P}}
\iff 2j\ge p-1.
\tag{3.9}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{v_p(g(\chi))\ge\frac12.}
\tag{3.10}
\]

For the indexing \(\chi=\omega^{-j}\), the vanishing indices are

\[
\frac{p-1}{2}\le j\le p-2.
\]

There are exactly

\[
\boxed{\frac{p-1}{2}}
\tag{3.11}
\]

such characters.

For the indexing \(\chi_r=\omega^r\), equation (3.4) reverses the interval:

\[
1\le r\le\frac{p-1}{2}.
\tag{3.12}
\]

This is the meaning corresponding to the threshold in part (B) of the question. The correct equivalence is

\[
v_p(g(\chi))\ge1/2
\iff g(\chi)^2\equiv0\pmod{p\mathcal O_{K_{\mathfrak P}}},
\]

not merely modulo the uniformizer.

Order by order, the count is also transparent. Among characters of exact order \(d>2\), exactly \(arphi(d)/2\) have selected-prime slope at least \(1/2\); the exponents \(c\) pair as \(c\leftrightarrow d-c\). The quadratic character contributes one. Summing over \(d\mid p-1\) gives

\[
1+\frac12\sum_{\substack{d\mid p-1\\d>2}}\varphi(d)
=\frac{p-1}{2}.
\tag{3.13}
\]

## 9. Global divisibility by \(p\mathcal O_K\): only the quadratic character

Let \(d=\operatorname{ord}(\chi)\), and let

\[
K=\mathbf Q(\zeta_p,\zeta_d).
\]

Because \(d\mid p-1\), \(p\) splits completely in \(\mathbf Q(\zeta_d)\), while it is totally ramified in the \(\zeta_p\)-direction. At the primes \(\mathfrak P_a\mid p\), the normalized \(p\)-adic slopes of \(g(\chi)\) are the numbers in (3.7).

Global divisibility means the local inequality at **every** prime above \(p\):

\[
g(\chi)^2\in p\mathcal O_K
\iff
2\left\langle\frac{ac}{d}\right\rangle\ge1
\quad\text{for every }a\in(\mathbf Z/d\mathbf Z)^\times.
\tag{3.14}
\]

Since multiplication by \(c\) permutes the units modulo \(d\), the minimum slope is

\[
\min_a\left\langle\frac{ac}{d}\right\rangle=\frac1d.
\tag{3.15}
\]

Thus (3.14) holds precisely when

\[
\frac2d\ge1.
\]

The trivial character has \(g(\mathbf1)^2=1\), so the only nontrivial possibility is \(d=2\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{
g(\chi)^2\equiv0\pmod{p\mathcal O_K}
\iff \chi^2=\mathbf1\text{ and }\chi\ne\mathbf1.}
\tag{3.16}
\]

There is exactly one such character for every odd \(p\), namely the quadratic character \(arphi\). Indeed the classical identity gives directly

\[
\boxed{g(\varphi)^2=\varphi(-1)p.}
\tag{3.17}
\]

For every character of order \(d>2\), at least one prime over \(p\) has slope \(1/d<1/2\), so the square fails to be globally divisible by \(p\).

This also explains the apparent paradox algebraically. In \(\mathcal O_K/p\mathcal O_K\), a nontrivial Gauss sum is generally a nonzero nilpotent: it lies in every maximal ideal over \(p\), but not necessarily in the full ideal \(p\mathcal O_K\). Squaring kills it globally only in the quadratic case.

The complex estimate \(|g(\chi)|=\sqrt p\) proves characteristic-zero nonvanishing. It says nothing about whether the image in a nonreduced characteristic-\(p\) quotient is zero.

### Bonus: an \(m\)-fold Kloosterman convolution

The same argument gives a useful general rule. If an \(m\)-fold multiplicative Artin--Schreier convolution has Mellin transform \(g(\chi)^m\), then

\[
g(\chi)^m\in p\mathcal O_K
\iff \operatorname{ord}(\chi)\le m,
\tag{3.18}
\]

apart from the trivial character. Hence its number of global Mellin zeros is

\[
\sum_{\substack{d\mid p-1\\2\le d\le m}}\varphi(d)
\le\sum_{d=2}^m\varphi(d),
\tag{3.19}
\]

an absolute bound depending only on the convolution length. For \(m=2\), this is exactly the singleton quadratic zero.

## 10. Exact comparison with the reported computation

The three predicted patterns are unmistakable:

1. **Reduction modulo a residue field \(\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P\):** every nontrivial \(r\) vanishes.
2. **Reduction modulo \(p\) after choosing one \(p\)-adic embedding:** one contiguous half of the \(r\)-range vanishes; the half is reversed if the Mellin convention uses \(\chi^{-1}\).
3. **Global coefficientwise reduction modulo \(p\mathcal O_K\):** exactly
   \[
   r=(p-1)/2
   \]
   vanishes.

Therefore the reported average near \(1.00\) is naturally explained by the third interpretation. It should then strengthen to the deterministic assertion

\[
\boxed{Z_p^{\mathrm{Kl}}=\{(p-1)/2\}}
\tag{4.3}
\]

for every odd \(p\).

If the actual data sometimes has zero or two zeros, or if its singleton is not the quadratic exponent, then at least one of the following is true:

* \(t^r\) was implemented as an ordinary representative rather than a roots-of-unity character;
* the transform uses a different trace function from the raw \(K(t)\);
* a normalization or additive correction term was inserted;
* zero was tested after one specialization of the character-value field rather than in the full cyclotomic ring;
* the computed Kloosterman object is a rational/integer proxy, not the classical cyclotomic Kloosterman sum.

A decisive unit test is equation (3.17): for the quadratic index, the computed Mellin value must be exactly \(arphi(-1)p\), up to the one global sign dictated by the sheaf convention.

# (D) What survives for the Apéry hypergeometric problem

## 11. The decisive structural distinction

Kloosterman works because multiplicative convolution and Mellin transform diagonalize each other:

\[
\operatorname{AS}_\psi*\operatorname{AS}_\psi
\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{Mellin}\ }
g(\chi)^2.
\]

After Mellin transformation there is one product and no outer summation. Its reduction is controlled completely by the prime-ideal factorization of that product.

A finite-field hypergeometric **value at a fixed point**, by contrast, is itself an inverse Mellin sum. Schematically, Greene's definition has the form

\[
F_p(z)=C_p\sum_{\eta}A_p(\eta)\eta(z),
\tag{5.1}
\]

where \(A_p(\eta)\) is a bounded product/ratio of Gauss or Jacobi sums. Mellin transformation in the geometric variable \(z\) selects one term:

\[
\sum_{z\ne0}\chi(z)^{-1}F_p(z)
=(p-1)C_p A_p(\chi).
\tag{5.2}
\]

But evaluating at a fixed value such as \(z=1/16\) retains the entire sum

\[
F_p(1/16)=C_p\sum_{\eta}A_p(\eta)\eta(1/16).
\tag{5.3}
\]

This is the exact place where “a product of four Gamma values” can be misleading. Each **summand** may become a product of Gamma values under Gross--Koblitz; the hypergeometric value remains a sum of those products.

## 12. The classical Apéry coefficient is a terminating, \(r\)-dependent hypergeometric value

For the coefficient itself, the direct identity is

\[
\boxed{
b_r={}_4F_3\!\left(
\begin{matrix}-r,-r,r+1,r+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};1
\right).}
\tag{5.4}
\]

A half-parameter expression involving

\[
(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2;1,1,1)
\]

and a point such as \(1/16\) may describe a transformed period or a related finite-field hypergeometric family, but it is not automatically the same as the Mellin coefficient \(b_r\). Finite-field transformations also carry normalization and exceptional-character terms. The required identity must be proved at the level of the **function in the geometric Mellin variable**, not inferred from the existence of a classical hypergeometric representation of each coefficient.

This is a common interchange error:

\[
\text{“the coefficient is a hypergeometric value”}
\not\Longrightarrow
\text{“the coefficient is one Mellin-selected Gauss product.”}
\tag{5.5}
\]

and let \(\eta\) run over all \(N\) multiplicative characters. Then, for \(1\le r\le p-2\), the project records the exact form

There are exactly \(p-1\) summands in (5.6). Applying Gross--Koblitz to each Jacobi sum gives explicit fractional-part/carry exponents, but it does not remove the outer \(\eta\)-sum.

For every interior \(1\le r\le p-2\), this packet has at least two terms. Thus there is never a unique lowest-valuation term whose unit residue would force nonvanishing.

## 14. Exact Morita-Gamma criterion for \(b_r\bmod p\)

The same obstruction is especially transparent directly from the Apéry sum. For \(0\le k\le r\le p-1\), define the \(p\)-adic unit

\[
U_{p,r,k}
=\frac{\Gamma_p(r+k+1)}
{\Gamma_p(k+1)^2\Gamma_p(r-k+1)}.
\tag{5.8}
\]

Up to a sign that disappears after squaring, the exact factorial identity is

\[
\binom rk\binom{r+k}{k}
=p^{\mathbf1_{r+k\ge p}}U_{p,r,k}.
\tag{5.9}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
b_r=\sum_{k=0}^r
p^{2\mathbf1_{r+k\ge p}}U_{p,r,k}^2.}
\tag{5.10}
\]

Put

\[
\rho=\min(r,p-1-r).
\]

Reducing (5.10) modulo \(p\) gives the exact zero criterion

\[
\boxed{
p\mid b_r
\iff
\Theta_{p,r}:=
\sum_{k=0}^{\rho}\overline{U}_{p,r,k}^{\,2}=0
\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_p.}
\tag{5.11}
\]

Gross--Koblitz/Stickelberger has completely determined which terms survive. It has not determined whether their unit residues cancel. Equation (5.11) is explicit, but it is not a bounded-complexity location formula for the zeros.

The project gives a sharp elementary witness. Since

\[
b_5=819005,
\]

one has

\[
b_5\equiv0\pmod{11},
\qquad
b_5\equiv5\pmod{13}.
\tag{5.12}
\]

For both \(p=11\) and \(p=13\), all six \(k=0,\ldots,5\) summands in (5.10) have valuation zero. The slope vector is identical, while the unit sum cancels for \(p=11\) and does not cancel for \(p=13\). Hence the carry polygon alone cannot decide the zero event.

The exhaustive project audit through \(p\le200\) finds at most four Apéry zeros, with four occurring at \(p=181\) and \(p=193\). This is useful evidence for small average zero count, but it is not explained by the slope polygon.

## 15. Conditional positive result: what a genuine single-product formula would give

There is nevertheless a precise route worth preserving.

Suppose one could prove, for the actual AFP Mellin coefficient, an identity of the form

\[
M_r
=u_r p^{-e_r}
\prod_{i=1}^{A}g(A_i\chi_r)
\prod_{j=1}^{B}g(B_j\chi_r^{-1}),
\tag{5.13}
\]

with no remaining character sum, where \(A,B\) and the fixed characters \(A_i,B_j\) are independent of \(p\). Gross--Koblitz would then give at every prime above \(p\) a finite fractional-part expression

\[
v_p(M_r)
=-e_r+
\sum_i\left\langle\alpha_i+x_r\right\rangle
+
\sum_j\left\langle\beta_j-x_r\right\rangle,
\tag{5.14}
\]

up to the chosen inverse-character convention. Global divisibility would become a finite system of piecewise-linear inequalities over all Galois conjugates. This can indeed lead to an explicit and uniformly bounded zero set, exactly as it does for \(g(\chi)^2\).

So the proposed strategy is mathematically sound **conditional on the single-product identity**.

The present Apéry formulas do not provide that identity. They provide (5.6), an outer sum over all characters, or (5.10), a sum of \(r+1\) terms. Establishing a transformation that collapses either sum to (5.13) would be a genuine breakthrough and must be checked against the exact Apéry data. Gross--Koblitz by itself does not perform this collapse.

## 16. What Gross--Koblitz can still contribute to AFP

A rigorous Gross--Koblitz program for Apéry should proceed in the following order:

1. Fix one exact finite-field hypergeometric convention: Greene, Katz, or McCarthy. Record every factor of \(p\), \(p-1\), every sign, and every exceptional trivial-character term.
2. Prove that the function being transformed is the actual Apéry K3/Hasse trace function in the geometric variable used by the project.
3. Take the Mellin transform in that geometric variable. Do not confuse this with evaluating a hypergeometric function at a fixed point.
4. Apply Gross--Koblitz termwise and compute the complete Landau/carry function.
5. Remove all positive-slope terms modulo \(p\).
6. Analyze the surviving unit sum. This is the unresolved anti-cancellation step.

For the formulas currently in the repository, steps 1--5 lead exactly to (5.11). Step 6 is AFP.

Therefore the final verdict is

\[
\boxed{
\begin{minipage}{0.86\linewidth}
Kloosterman Mellin zeros are explicitly controlled by Stickelberger because the Mellin transform is one Gauss-square product. The reported one-zero average corresponds to global \(p\)-divisibility and, under the raw conventions, is exactly the quadratic character for every odd prime. For Apéry, Gross--Koblitz gives an exact slope-and-unit formula, but the known expression retains a large outer sum. The zeros are cancellations among minimum-slope units, so there is currently no valuation-only explicit formula for AFP zeros.
\end{minipage}}

## 17. Minimal code-level diagnostic

To identify exactly which Kloosterman reduction the computation used, check these four items.

1. **Character lift.** Is `chi_r(t)` represented as a \((p-1)\)-st root of unity indexed by the discrete logarithm of \(t\), or merely as the integer/field element \(t^r\)?
2. **Target ring.** Is zero tested in a residue field \(\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P\), in the local Artin ring \(\mathcal O_{K_{\mathfrak P}}/p\), or in the global quotient \(\mathcal O_K/p\mathcal O_K\)?
3. **Trace convention.** Is the value the classical \(K(t)\), the sheaf trace \(-K(t)\), or a normalized trace?
4. **Quadratic test.** Does the index \(r=(p-1)/2\) produce exactly \(arphi(-1)p\), up to the declared global sign?

Those checks determine whether the expected count is \(p-2\), \((p-1)/2\), or \(1\) without any numerical experimentation.
