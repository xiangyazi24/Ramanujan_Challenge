# M(p-1)=swap matrix: proof status and consequences

## Main conclusion

The computational identity

\[
M(p-1)=T(p-2)\cdots T(1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\]

is very plausible and is the matrix form of the Apéry reflection symmetry, but the implication from the known congruence

\[
b_r\equiv b_{p-1-r}\pmod p
\]

requires one extra ingredient: a statement about the full rank-2 solution space, not only the distinguished Apéry solution.

The reflection law determines the action on one solution vector. To determine a 2x2 transfer matrix completely, we need its action on a basis of solutions.

---

## (A) Proof of the swap identity

Write the recurrence as a first-order system

\[
V_{r+1}=T(r)V_r,
\]

where

\[
V_r=\binom{b_r}{b_{r-1}}.
\]

Then

\[
M(p-1)V_1=V_{p-1}.
\]

Since

\[
V_1=\binom{5}{1},
\]

and the reflection law gives

\[
b_{p-1}=b_0=1,
\qquad b_{p-2}=b_1=5,
\]

we get

\[
M(p-1)\binom51=\binom15.
\]

This proves only that

\[
M(p-1)
\]

maps one vector to the swapped vector.

To prove the entire matrix equals swap, introduce a second independent solution c_r. If one proves the same reflection law

\[
c_r=c_{p-1-r},
\]

then

\[
M(p-1)\binom{c_1}{c_0}=\binom{c_0}{c_1}.
\]

The two vectors

\[
\binom51,\quad \binom{c_1}{c_0}
\]

form a basis, hence

\[
M(p-1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Therefore the missing theorem is not the Apéry reflection itself, but the extension of reflection to the entire local system.

A sheaf-theoretic interpretation is that the Frobenius action at p-1 has a canonical involution exchanging the two local solutions.

---

## (B) Effect on AFP and orbit distribution

The swap identity gives a global constraint:

\[
M(p-1)^2=I.
\]

The orbit is therefore not an arbitrary random walk on P^1(F_p). It is a finite segment with a forced endpoint symmetry:

\[
[5:1]\longrightarrow [1:5].
\]

However this does not imply concentration near the fixed points.

The swap fixes exactly

\[
[1:1],\qquad [1:-1].
\]

A generic orbit under a product of varying matrices does not spend extra time at these points. Hitting a fixed point would require

\[
M(r)[5:1]=[\pm1:1],
\]

which is a codimension-one condition.

The reflection symmetry explains the observed evenness of zero counts, because zeros occur in paired positions

\[
r\leftrightarrow p-1-r.
\]

But the Poisson(1/2) statistics come from the stronger mixing property of the transfer cocycle, not from the swap alone.

---

## (C) Half-orbit matrix

Let

\[
H=M((p-1)/2).
\]

Splitting the product gives

\[
M(p-1)=H_{\rm right}H_{\rm left}=S,
\]

where

\[
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Hence

\[
H_{\rm right}=SH_{\rm left}^{-1}.
\]

This is a genuine constraint, but not a usual inverse symmetry because the transfer matrices are not constant.

The useful interpretation is:

The second half of the orbit is determined by the first half through the involution S.

For any point x,

\[
M(p-1-r)[5:1]
\]

is related to the image of the first half under S.

This gives a time-reversal symmetry of the cocycle.

A stronger statement such as

\[
H^{-1}=SHS
\]

would require an additional reciprocity identity for every transfer matrix. It does not follow from the endpoint swap alone.

## (D) Wilson theorem analogy

The analogy is very good, but with an important distinction.

Wilson says:

\[
(p-1)!\equiv -1\pmod p.
\]

It is the product identity of the multiplicative group F_p^*.

The Apéry identity is a noncommutative second-order analogue:

\[
T(p-2)\cdots T(1)=S.
\]

Instead of multiplying all elements of a finite group, we multiply a sequence of transfer operators in GL_2(F_p).

The common principle is:

- Wilson: global product over a complete finite orbit gives an involution.
- Apéry: global transfer over a complete finite recurrence cycle gives an involution.

So it is reasonable to call it a "matrix Wilson theorem" for the Apéry recurrence.

The deeper reason is the same phenomenon: a hidden symmetry forces a global cancellation/product identity.

---

## Consequence for AFP

The swap identity is important but is not by itself the AFP theorem.

It explains:

1. why zeros occur in reflection pairs;
2. why the orbit is not a generic open trajectory;
3. why the total orbit has a time-reversal symmetry.

AFP additionally needs:

\[
\text{near-uniform distribution of the intermediate orbit points in }P^1(F_p).
\]

The observed L2 discrepancy \(p^{0.03}\) and chi-squared convergence indicate that the cocycle has strong mixing despite the exact endpoint constraint.

The likely sheaf-theoretic picture is:

\[
\text{Sp}_2=SL_2\text{ monodromy}
+\text{reflection involution}
\Rightarrow
\text{uniform projective orbit with paired endpoints}.
\]

The swap identity is therefore analogous to Wilson's theorem: a structural identity controlling the boundary of the orbit, while AFP is the statement that the interior behaves randomly.
