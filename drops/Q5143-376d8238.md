# Creative-telescoping search for a P2.7 summand

## Executive verdict

The structural clues do not determine a unique terminating hypergeometric summand. In particular, the Pochhammer quotient

\[
\delta_n=2^{-20n}\frac{(3)_n^4(4)_n^6}
{(\frac52)_n^4(\frac72)_n^3(\frac92)_n^3}
\]

is a determinant-line/Casoratian datum of the rank-three recurrence. It constrains products of normalizations of three independent modes; it is **not** by itself the scalar prefactor of the marked denominator \(q_n\). Multiplying a candidate denominator by \(\delta_n\) would multiply every Poincaré root by \(2^{-20}\), so it must not be imposed without a compensating exponential gauge.

There are nevertheless two concrete searches.

1. **Finite Whipple search.** Search the slope-balanced terminating \( {}_4F_3(1) \) and \( {}_5F_4(1) \) families written below. Their saddle equations are cubic, as required for a four-term recurrence. The half-integer intercepts are searched on the lattice generated by
   
   \[
   \frac12,\quad \frac32,\quad \frac52,\quad \frac72,\quad \frac92,
   \]
   
   while the outer gamma quotient is searched separately. This is an exact finite linear/algebraic search once slope and intercept bounds are fixed.

2. **Level-11 quadratic trace.** The identity
   
   \[
   16P_{27}\!\left(\frac{(t-2)^2}{4}\right)
   =-C_{11}(t)C_{11}(4-t)
   \]
   
   gives an exact nonlocal transform of exponential modes:
   
   \[
   t^m\longmapsto
   \frac1{256^n}\sum_{m=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{m}(-2)^{2n-m}t^m
   =\left(\frac{(t-2)^2}{256}\right)^n.
   \]
   
   Hence the most promising candidate is not presently a bare one-fold \( {}_4F_3 \), but an even shifted-binomial trace of the Cooper level-11 sequence, followed by a low-degree change of cyclic vector. This candidate has the P2.7 Poincaré roots **by construction**.

The recommended order of work is therefore:

- first test the level-11 trace with a polynomial post-operator of degree at most two;
- in parallel run the bounded Whipple enumeration;
- reject candidates from 8–12 exact terms before invoking full creative telescoping;
- for a survivor, compute a WZ certificate and then construct the parameter derivative or partial-fraction companion producing the mixed kernel.

The current repository file `problems/2.7/proof.tex` still says that the printed recurrence is identified with AESZ #209. Given the exact dead ends listed in the question, that sentence should be read as a spectral/modular fingerprint claim until an actual Ore, integral, or geometric map is supplied.

# A. The terminating hypergeometric search

## A.1 The complete \( {}_4F_3(1) \) template

A terminating proper-hypergeometric single sum with a cubic saddle equation can be put in the form

\[
\boxed{
Q_n=N_n\,{}_4F_3\!\left(
\begin{matrix}
-Mn-\nu,\ A_1n+a_1,\ A_2n+a_2,\ A_3n+a_3\\
B_1n+b_1,\ B_2n+b_2,\ B_3n+b_3
\end{matrix};1\right),}
\tag{A.1}
\]

where

- \(M\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\) and \(\nu\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), so the support is \(0\le k\le Mn+\nu\);
- all slopes are rational, normally integral after a common rescaling;
- the intercepts lie in \(\frac12\mathbb Z\);
- no lower parameter is a nonpositive integer on the support;
- \(N_n\) is a rational gamma quotient of zero factorial degree.

The summand is

\[
T(n,k)=N_n
\frac{(-Mn-\nu)_k\prod_{i=1}^3(A_i n+a_i)_k}
{k!\prod_{j=1}^3(B_j n+b_j)_k}.
\tag{A.2}
\]

For \(k=\tau n+O(1)\), its consecutive-\(k\) ratio tends to

\[
\frac{T(n,k+1)}{T(n,k)}
\longrightarrow
\frac{(\tau-M)\prod_{i=1}^3(\tau+A_i)}
{\tau\prod_{j=1}^3(\tau+B_j)}.
\tag{A.3}
\]

The saddle equation is therefore

\[
\boxed{
(\tau-M)\prod_{i=1}^3(\tau+A_i)
-\tau\prod_{j=1}^3(\tau+B_j)=0.}
\tag{A.4}
\]

The quartic terms cancel automatically, so (A.4) is a cubic unless its leading cubic coefficient also vanishes. This is the basic reason a balanced \( {}_4F_3(1) \) is the correct one-fold class to search for an order-three Poincaré module.

Writing

\[
A^{(1)}=\sum_iA_i,\quad
A^{(2)}=\sum_{i<j}A_iA_j,\quad
A^{(3)}=A_1A_2A_3,
\]

and similarly \(B^{(r)}\), the cubic is

\[
\begin{aligned}
S_4(\tau)={}&
(A^{(1)}-M-B^{(1)})\tau^3\\
&+(A^{(2)}-MA^{(1)}-B^{(2)})\tau^2\\
&+(A^{(3)}-MA^{(2)}-B^{(3)})\tau
-MA^{(3)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{A.5}
\]

This formula gives a fast algebraic screen. It is reasonable to require either

\[
S_4(u\tau+v)=c\,C_{11}(\tau)
\tag{A.6}
\]

for rational \(u,v,c\ne0\), or at least equality of discriminant and splitting field. Condition (A.6) is **not sufficient**, because recurrence multipliers are saddle phase values rather than saddle locations, but it is a cheap necessary modularity screen.

The exponential phase attached to a saddle is, up to the outer exponential part of \(N_n\),

\[
\Phi_4(\tau)=
(-1)^\tau
\frac{M^M}{(M-\tau)^{M-\tau}\tau^\tau}
\prod_{i=1}^3\frac{(A_i+\tau)^{A_i+\tau}}{A_i^{A_i}}
\prod_{j=1}^3\frac{B_j^{B_j}}{(B_j+\tau)^{B_j+\tau}}.
\tag{A.7}
\]

The branch convention is fixed algebraically by exponentiating logarithmic derivatives. For a candidate, eliminate \(\tau\) between \(S_4(\tau)=0\) and \(x-C\Phi_4(\tau)=0\). The resulting cubic in \(x\) must equal

\[
\chi_{27}(x)=x^3-\frac{55}{64}x^2+\frac1{2048}x-\frac1{2^{20}}
\tag{A.8}
\]

up to a scalar.

## A.2 The Whipple-type \( {}_5F_4(1) \) template

The natural duplication-compatible family is

\[
\boxed{
Q_n=N_n\,{}_5F_4\!\left(
\begin{matrix}
-Mn-\nu,\ -Mn-\nu',\ Fn+f,
\frac{Gn+g}{2},\frac{Gn+g+1}{2}\\
H_1n+h_1,\ H_2n+h_2,\ H_3n+h_3,
\frac{H_4n+h_4}{2}
\end{matrix};1\right).}
\tag{A.9}
\]

This includes the structural pattern used by Dauguet and Zudilin. Their successful simultaneous-approximation example has two negative upper slopes, a duplication pair in the numerator, and a half-integer lower parameter; see [Dauguet–Zudilin, arXiv:1401.5322](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.5322).

A generic \( {}_5F_4(1) \) has a quartic saddle polynomial. To obtain only three exponential characters, impose the slope-balance equation

\[
\boxed{
F+G-2M=H_1+H_2+H_3+\frac{H_4}{2}.}
\tag{A.10}
\]

Indeed, after the leading degree-five terms cancel, (A.10) cancels the degree-four term. The remaining saddle equation is cubic.

A minimal half-integral seed, useful only as the first test point and **not** asserted to be the answer, is

\[
\boxed{
{}_5F_4\!\left(
\begin{matrix}
-2n,-2n,4n+1,\frac{3n+1}{2},\frac{3n+2}{2}\\
n+1,n+1,1,n+\frac12
\end{matrix};1\right).}
\tag{A.11}
\]

Its slopes satisfy (A.10), its support is \(0\le k\le2n\), and it has precisely the duplication/half-integer architecture suggested by the endpoint factors. It should be treated as the origin of a contiguous family: shift each intercept by an element of

\[
\{-2,-\tfrac32,-1,-\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2\}
\tag{A.12}
\]

and search the resulting finite box. A candidate is retained only if its exact first terms can be normalized to \(q_0,q_1,q_2\) and its held-out recurrence residuals vanish.

## A.3 Outer prefixes and the correct use of \(\delta_n\)

Use

\[
N_n=C^n
\frac{(3)_n^{u_3}(4)_n^{u_4}}
{(\frac52)_n^{v_5}(\frac72)_n^{v_7}(\frac92)_n^{v_9}}
\frac{\prod_r(\alpha_r)_n}{\prod_s(\beta_s)_n},
\tag{A.13}
\]

with

\[
u_3+u_4+\#\{r\}=v_5+v_7+v_9+\#\{s\}
\tag{A.14}
\]

to prevent factorial-scale growth. Search small integer exponents, for example

\[
0\le u_3,u_4,v_5,v_7,v_9\le6.
\tag{A.15}
\]

The determinant datum singles out the lattice point

\[
(u_3,u_4;v_5,v_7,v_9)=(4,6;4,3,3),
\tag{A.16}
\]

but it does **not** say that the denominator itself uses (A.16). If (A.16) is tested, \(C\) must remain free because that quotient has exponential factor \(2^{-20n}\) and polynomial factor \(n^2\).

A standard normalized \(pF_{p-1}\) saddle sum with affine parameters often has formal power \(n^0\). Thus using the whole factor (A.16) would normally shift the formal power by \(+2\). This is another reason to search the half-integer data as **intercepts inside the hypergeometric function**, rather than automatically multiplying by the full Casoratian quotient.

## A.4 Exact finite search protocol

For each candidate:

1. Generate \(Q_0,\ldots,Q_{12}\) exactly from the term ratio; do not call floating-point `hypergeometric`.
2. Normalize \(Q_0=q_0\). If an exponential gauge \(C^n\) is free, solve for \(C\) using \(Q_1/q_1\), then require exact agreement at \(n=2\).
3. Evaluate the P2.7 residual for \(n=2,\ldots,9\). One nonzero residual rejects the candidate.
4. Reduce the same calculation modulo two good primes, such as \(101\) and \(103\), before doing expensive rational arithmetic.
5. Only for a survivor, invoke Chyzak/Zeilberger creative telescoping and compare the primitive telescoper with the stored P2.7 operator.
6. Require a certificate identity
   
   \[
   \sum_{j=0}^3P_j(n)T(n+j,k)
   =G(n,k+1)-G(n,k)
   \tag{A.17}
   \]
   
   and verify the boundary terms at \(k=0\) and the terminating endpoint.

A recurrence guessed from terms is not the proof; (A.17) is.

# B. What Calabi–Yau operations can and cannot do

## B.1 Constant pullback

If \(A(z)=\sum a_nz^n\), then

\[
A(cz)=\sum c^na_nz^n.
\]

This multiplies every Poincaré root by \(c\). Therefore the exact factor \(1/4\) between two characteristic cubics can always be produced by a constant pullback. It preserves differential order and does not explain a change from a rank-four Picard–Fuchs module to a rank-three recurrence module.

## B.2 Symmetric powers and Clausen transformations

For a rank-two differential module \(E\),

\[
\operatorname{Sym}^2(E)\text{ has rank }3,
\qquad
\operatorname{Sym}^3(E)\text{ has rank }4.
\]

This is the only standard representation-theoretic mechanism in the question that naturally gives ranks three and four from the same modular rank-two object. Cooper’s review explains that classical Apéry-like rank-three systems arise from a Clausen-type square of weight-one modular systems; see [Cooper, arXiv:2302.00757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00757), especially the discussion around the nonlinear equation and the resulting \((k+1)\)-term recurrence.

However, one must not infer that AESZ #209 is a symmetric cube merely from modularity. A generic fourth-order Calabi–Yau operator has symplectic monodromy in \(\mathrm{Sp}_4\); the subgroup \(\operatorname{Sym}^3\mathrm{SL}_2\) is special. An exact test requires:

- local exponents compatible with a symmetric cube at every singularity;
- the appropriate rational invariant in a symmetric/exterior power;
- an exact `symmetric_power` or differential-Galois certificate.

If such a rank-two \(E_{11}\) exists, then the candidate relation would be

\[
L_{209}\simeq \operatorname{Sym}^3(E_{11}),
\qquad
L_{27}\simeq \operatorname{Sym}^2(E_{11})\otimes\chi,
\tag{B.1}
\]

where \(\chi\) is a rank-one coordinate/Tate gauge producing the factor \(1/4\). Equation (B.1) is a precise computational hypothesis, not a result currently established in the repository.

## B.3 Yifan Yang pullback

Yifan Yang’s pullback starts from certain fifth-order equations related to exterior squares and produces fourth-order Calabi–Yau equations, often reducing polynomial degree. It does not generally turn an arbitrary fourth-order Picard–Fuchs equation into a third-order recurrence. See [Almkvist, arXiv:math/0612215](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0612215) and [Almkvist, arXiv:math/0703261](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0703261).

Thus “Yifan Yang pullback” is not by itself an explanation of P2.7.

## B.4 Hadamard product

Hadamard product acts coefficientwise:

\[
\left(\sum a_nz^n\right)\star
\left(\sum b_nz^n\right)=\sum a_nb_nz^n.
\]

It multiplies exponential characters. Hadamard multiplication by a geometric sequence \(c^n\) is just the constant pullback above. A genuine Hadamard square root is not canonical, and even if it exists it does not automatically preserve the marked regulator period \(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\).

## B.5 Middle convolution / Euler transform

Middle convolution and Euler transforms can change rank and local exponents while preserving geometric/global-nilpotent structure. They are legitimate search operations, but they do not preserve a connection constant without an explicit transformed cycle. See Dettweiler–Reiter, [arXiv:math/0305311](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0305311).

## B.6 The operation actually indicated by the cubic identity

The identity in the question points instead to a quadratic descent under the involution

\[
t\longmapsto4-t,
\qquad
x=\frac{(t-2)^2}{4}.
\tag{B.2}
\]

This is an invariant/even projection on a degree-two cover. On sequences it is realized by a shifted binomial transform and even section, not by a symmetric power or a scalar gauge.

That is the operation to test first.

# C. The exact level-11 trace candidate

## C.1 Cooper’s level-11 sequence

Let \(T_m\) be the self-starting level-11 sequence

\[
\begin{aligned}
(m+1)^3T_{m+1}={}&
2(2m+1)(5m^2+5m+2)T_m\\
&-8m(7m^2+1)T_{m-1}
+22m(2m-1)(m-1)T_{m-2},
\end{aligned}
\tag{C.1}
\]

with \(T_0=1\). It begins

\[
1,4,28,268,3004,36784,476476,\ldots
\]

and is discussed in [Cooper, arXiv:2302.00757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00757). No one-fold binomial formula is supplied there for this level-11 sequence; this is why the modular candidate below is initially a nonlocal transform rather than a single \( {}_pF_q \).

## C.2 The quadratic trace

Define

\[
\boxed{
W_n=256^{-n}\sum_{m=0}^{2n}
\binom{2n}{m}(-2)^{2n-m}T_m.}
\tag{C.2}
\]

For a pure exponential \(T_m=t^m\),

\[
W_n=\left(\frac{(t-2)^2}{256}\right)^n.
\tag{C.3}
\]

If \(t\) is a root of \(C_{11}\), then the spectral identity implies

\[
\mu=\frac{(t-2)^2}{4}
\quad\text{is a root of }P_{27},
\]

and hence

\[
\lambda=\frac\mu{64}=\frac{(t-2)^2}{256}
\tag{C.4}
\]

is an actual P2.7 recurrence multiplier. Thus (C.2) has the desired three exponential characters exactly.

This is much stronger than matching a numerical dominant root.

## C.3 Required change of cyclic vector

Matching the characteristic roots does not fix a recurrence or its marked solution. The smallest useful ansatz is

\[
\boxed{
q_n=R_0(n)W_n+R_1(n)W_{n+1}+R_2(n)W_{n+2},}
\tag{C.5}
\]

where

\[
R_i(n)=\frac{P_i(n)}{D(n)},
\qquad
\deg P_i\le d,\quad \deg D\le d,
\tag{C.6}
\]

and \(d=0,1,2,3,\ldots\) is increased gradually. Start with \(d=2\).

An equivalent single outer sum is

\[
\boxed{
q_n=\frac{256^{-n}}{D(n)}
\sum_{m=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{m}(-2)^{2n-m}
P(n,m)T_m,}
\tag{C.7}
\]

where

\[
P(n,m)=c_{00}+c_{10}n+c_{01}m+c_{20}n^2+c_{11}nm+c_{02}m^2.
\tag{C.8}
\]

Equations (C.7)–(C.8) are the **most promising concrete candidate parameters** from the supplied structural information:

- upper limit: \(2n\);
- binomial upper parameter: \(-2n\);
- shift parameter: \(-2\);
- even-trace scaling: \(256^{-n}\);
- post-operator degree: at most two initially;
- six rational unknowns plus an overall normalization.

The coefficients are determined from \(q_0,\ldots,q_6\), then tested on held-out terms and on the full recurrence. If degree two fails, increase the polynomial degree or use the three-shift form (C.5) with rational functions.

The half-integer factors in the P2.7 determinant should be used to choose the denominator \(D(n)\), for example from products of

\[
n+\frac52,\qquad n+\frac72,\qquad n+\frac92,
\tag{C.9}
\]

but they should not be imposed as the entire factor \(\delta_n\).

## C.4 Why this is not yet a hypergeometric single sum

The kernel

\[
\binom{2n}{m}(-2)^{2n-m}
\]

is proper hypergeometric in \((n,m)\). The obstruction is \(T_m\): the level-11 sequence is recurrence-defined and has no currently known one-fold binomial representation. If a hypergeometric or diagonal formula for \(T_m\) is found, substituting it into (C.7) gives a proper double sum. Creative telescoping may then collapse it to a one-fold \( {}_5F_4 \), but that collapse should be discovered, not assumed.

This explains why a blind \( {}_4F_3 \) search may miss the answer even though the modular trace is correct.

# D. Companion construction for \(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\)

A denominator formula alone is insufficient. The same kernel must support a mixed companion.

The elementary identity is

\[
\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)
=\int_0^1\!\int_0^1
\frac{1-\frac12\log(xy)}{1-xy}\,dx\,dy.
\tag{D.1}
\]

For a terminating summand \(T(n,k;s)\) depending on a parameter \(s\), the ideal construction is

\[
Q_n(s)=\sum_kT(n,k;s),
\tag{D.2}
\]

with

\[
q_n=Q_n(0),
\qquad
p_n=\text{rational part of }
\left(Q_n(0)-\frac12\partial_sQ_n(s)|_{s=0}\right).
\tag{D.3}
\]

Differentiating Pochhammer factors produces digamma differences and hence harmonic numbers. The decoration must be derived from the parameterized WZ identity, not guessed as merely \(\alpha H_k^{(2)}+\beta H_k^{(3)}\).

For the modular trace candidate, one should first construct companions \(T_m^{(2)},T_m^{(3)}\) for the level-11 system, then apply exactly the same trace and cyclic-vector operator:

\[
p_n^{(r)}=
R_0(n)W_n^{(r)}+R_1(n)W_{n+1}^{(r)}+R_2(n)W_{n+2}^{(r)},
\qquad r=2,3,
\tag{D.4}
\]

and set

\[
p_n=p_n^{(2)}+p_n^{(3)}.
\tag{D.5}
\]

The pure denominator transform does not automatically transport the regulator/normal-function extension. Equation (D.4) is a separate exact test.

# E. Sage search skeleton

The following code performs the cheap exact screens. It deliberately separates candidate generation from the final WZ proof.

from sage.all import *

QQh = QQ

# P2.7 coefficients and target terms
# ------------------------------------------------------------
def A(n):
    n = QQ(n)
    return 1024*(2*n+5)^4*(2*n+7)^3*(2*n+9)^3 \
        *(946*n^2+6407*n+10860)

def B(n):
    n = QQ(n)
    return 128*(2*n+7)^3*(2*n+9)^3*(
        104060*n^6+1745370*n^5+12145238*n^4
        +44886481*n^3+92943995*n^2+102256019*n
        +46709052)

    n = QQ(n)
    return 16*(n+3)^4*(2*n+9)^3*(
        3784*n^5+57792*n^4+351019*n^3
        +1059230*n^2+1587211*n+944620)

    n = QQ(n)
    return (n+3)^4*(n+4)^6*(946*n^2+4515*n+5399)

    u = list(map(QQ, init))
    for n in range(2, N-1):
        u.append(B(n)/A(n)*u[n]
                 -C(n-1)/A(n-1)*u[n-1]
                 +D(n-2)/A(n-2)*u[n-2])
    return u

    -215040420000,
    -QQ(167282265043404)/905,
    -QQ(964185327658080)/6071,
], 40)

    out = QQ(1)
    for j in range(k):
        out *= a+j
    return out

def hyper_sum_4f3(n, M, nu, As, aa, Bs, bb):
    K = M*n + nu
    total = QQ(0)
    term = QQ(1)
    for k in range(K+1):
        if k:
            km1 = k-1
            num = (-M*n-nu+km1)
            for Ai, ai in zip(As, aa):
                num *= Ai*n+ai+km1
            den = k
            for Bj, bj in zip(Bs, bb):
                den *= Bj*n+bj+km1
            if den == 0:
                raise ZeroDivisionError
            term *= num/den
        total += term
    return total

def hyper_sum_5f4(n, M, nu, nu2, F, f, G, g,
                   H, h, H4, h4):
    K = min(M*n+nu, M*n+nu2)
    total = QQ(0)
    term = QQ(1)
    for k in range(K+1):
        if k:
            j = k-1
            num = (-M*n-nu+j)*(-M*n-nu2+j)*(F*n+f+j)
            num *= (QQ(G*n+g)/2+j)*(QQ(G*n+g+1)/2+j)
            den = k
            for Hi, hi in zip(H, h):
                den *= Hi*n+hi+j
            den *= (QQ(H4*n+h4)/2+j)
            if den == 0:
                raise ZeroDivisionError
            term *= num/den
        total += term
    return total

def residual(u, n):
    return (u[n+1]-B(n)/A(n)*u[n]
            +C(n-1)/A(n-1)*u[n-1]
            -D(n-2)/A(n-2)*u[n-2])

# Seed (A.11). It is a search origin, not a claimed formula.
seed = [hyper_sum_5f4(n,

                      F=4, f=1, G=3, g=1,
                      H=[1,1,0], h=[1,1,1],
                      H4=2, h4=1)
        for n in range(13)]

# Any candidate must be normalized and then have zero residuals.
print('seed first terms:', seed[:5])

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Cooper level-11 trace
# ------------------------------------------------------------
def cooper_terms(N):
    T = [QQ(1)]
    # n=0 gives T1=4
    for n in range(0, N-1):
        tm1 = T[n-1] if n >= 1 else QQ(0)
        tm2 = T[n-2] if n >= 2 else QQ(0)
        nxt = (2*(2*n+1)*(5*n^2+5*n+2)*T[n]
               -8*n*(7*n^2+1)*tm1
               +22*n*(2*n-1)*(n-1)*tm2)/(n+1)^3

T11 = cooper_terms(100)

def trace_W(n):

print('level-11 trace:', W[:8])

# R_i(n)=P_i(n)/D(n), with a common polynomial denominator.

def fit_polynomial_postoperator(d, nfit):
    cols = []

        for j in range(d+1):

               lambda r,c: cols[c][r])
    b = vector(QQ, q_target[:nfit])

# F. Answers to the three questions

## (A)

The most general useful one-fold class is (A.1), and the most general duplication-compatible Whipple class is (A.9) with the cubic-saddle condition (A.10). The factor \(\delta_n\) supplies a finite intercept/prefactor search lattice but does not uniquely determine \(N_n\). The concrete seed (A.11) is the first contiguous point to test; it is not claimed to satisfy P2.7.

## (B)

A constant pullback gives the factor \(1/4\) but cannot lower rank. Symmetric square gives rank three and symmetric cube gives rank four, so a common rank-two modular source is a mathematically precise possibility, but it requires exact operator invariants. Yifan Yang pullback is a fifth-to-fourth-order construction, not the desired fourth-to-third transformation. Hadamard and middle-convolution operations require explicit cycle data to preserve the zeta target.

The operation directly encoded by the spectral identity is the quadratic invariant/even trace (C.2).

## (C)

The P2.7 denominator may come from the same level-11 modular data in a different representation, but the evidence points to a quadratic descent under \(t\mapsto4-t\), not automatically to another symmetric power. The exact candidate is (C.7) with parameters

\[
2n,\quad -2,\quad 256^{-n},\quad \deg P\le2.
\]

This candidate has the correct exponential characters identically. The remaining questions are finite:

1. does a low-degree \(P(n,m)/D(n)\) reproduce the P2.7 recurrence and initial vector;
2. can the corresponding modular/diagonal kernel be expressed as a proper multisum;
3. can a parameter derivative or regulator companion be transported through the same trace to produce \(p_n\) and the mixed period.

That is a substantially narrower and better-motivated search than an unrestricted \( {}_5F_4 \) scan.
