# Verdict

The Gershgorin calculation is **correct as a characteristic-zero/real statement**, and it admits a stronger formulation:

\[
\boxed{B_{r,h}(T)\text{ is positive definite for every real }T\ge 0.}
\]

Consequently,

\[
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)>0,
\qquad
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_s)>0
\]

as ordinary integers, and every real root of the Dirichlet continuant
\(N_h^{(r)}(T)\) is strictly negative.

However, this does **not** prove the finite-field statement needed in P3.2. The desired conclusion is

\[
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)\not\equiv0\pmod p,
\]

whereas Gershgorin proves only

\[
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)\ne0\quad\text{in }\mathbf Z.
\]

A positive integer can be divisible by a large good prime. This failure occurs already in the smallest examples. For instance,

\[
r=1,\quad h=3,\quad p=727
\]

gives

\[
N_3^{(1)}(\lambda_1)=5816=8\cdot727,
\]

even though the corresponding real symmetric matrix is strictly diagonally dominant and positive definite.

Thus the proposed spectral argument proves an archimedean theorem, not the required modulo-\(p\) unit theorem. It does **not** close the nonmate two-zero problem.

The sharp remaining statement is still

\[
\boxed{
 b_r\equiv b_s\equiv0\pmod p
 \Longrightarrow
 p\nmid\gcd_{\mathbf Z}
 \bigl(N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r),N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_s)\bigr).}
\]

That is an arithmetic two-value exclusion, not a real spectral exclusion.

# 1. The exact Dirichlet matrix

Put

\[
s=r+h,
\qquad
I=\{r+1,r+2,\ldots,s-1\}.
\]

The Dirichlet matrix is the \((h-1)\times(h-1)\) tridiagonal matrix

\[
B_{r,h}(T)=\bigl(B_{m,n}(T)\bigr)_{m,n\in I},
\]

with

\[
B_{n,n}(T)=\gamma_n(T)
=n^3+(n+1)^3+2(2n+1)T,
\]

and

\[
B_{n,n+1}(T)=B_{n+1,n}(T)=-(n+1)^3.
\]

Therefore the matrix in the cubic normalization is already symmetric; no diagonal similarity is needed. If one instead starts from the monic Racah recurrence, one obtains the equivalent Jacobi matrix after the usual positive diagonal similarity.

The continuant is

\[
\boxed{N_h^{(r)}(T)=\det B_{r,h}(T).}
\]

The conventions are

\[
N_1^{(r)}(T)=1
\]

for the empty block, and

\[
N_2^{(r)}(T)=\gamma_{r+1}(T)
\]

for the one-vertex block.

# 2. The exact energy identity

Let

\[
x=(x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{s-1})^{\mathsf T},
\]

and extend it by Dirichlet boundary values

\[
x_r=x_s=0.
\]

Then direct expansion gives

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
x^{\mathsf T}B_{r,h}(T)x
&=\sum_{n=r}^{s-1}(n+1)^3(x_{n+1}-x_n)^2\\
&\quad+2T\sum_{n=r+1}^{s-1}(2n+1)x_n^2.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{1}
\]

The first sum is strictly positive for every nonzero Dirichlet vector \(x\). Indeed, if every difference vanished, then the path would be constant; the two zero endpoints would force that constant to be zero.

Hence

\[
B_{r,h}(0)>0
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{B_{r,h}(T)>0\quad\text{for every real }T\ge0.}
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, since

\[
\lambda_r=r(r+1)\ge0,
\qquad
\lambda_s=s(s+1)>0,
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)>0,
\qquad
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_s)>0
}
\tag{3}
\]

over \(\mathbf R\), and in fact over \(\mathbf Z\).

For \(r\ge1\), both parameters are strictly positive. For the edge case \(r=0\), the first term in (1) still proves positive definiteness at \(T=0\).

This is the cleanest structural proof. It is stronger than Gershgorin because it also handles \(T=0\) without any issue about weak diagonal dominance in interior rows.

# 3. The proposed Gershgorin argument is valid over the reals

For a row indexed by \(n\in I\), the actual Gershgorin radius is

\[
R_n=
\mathbf 1_{n>r+1}\,n^3
+
\mathbf 1_{n<s-1}\,(n+1)^3.
\]

Thus

\[
R_n\le n^3+(n+1)^3.
\]

The diagonal entry is

\[
d_n(T)=n^3+(n+1)^3+2(2n+1)T,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
d_n(T)-R_n\ge2(2n+1)T.}
\tag{4}
\]

At \(T=\lambda_r\), with \(r\ge1\), this gives

\[
d_n(\lambda_r)-R_n
\ge2(2n+1)r(r+1)>0.
\]

Therefore every Gershgorin interval lies strictly in \((0,\infty)\), and zero is not an eigenvalue. Since the matrix is symmetric, one obtains the quantitative lower bound

\[
\lambda_{\min}\bigl(B_{r,h}(\lambda_r)\bigr)
\ge
2(2r+3)r(r+1)>0.
\tag{5}
\]

The same statement holds with \(\lambda_s\) in place of \(\lambda_r\).

So the proposed calculation

\[
\gamma_n(\lambda_r)-n^3-(n+1)^3
=2(2n+1)r(r+1)>0
\]

is correct. At the two boundary rows the actual radius is smaller, making the estimate even stronger.

The error occurs only in the final implication from real invertibility to invertibility modulo \(p\).

# 4. The exact generalized eigenvalue interpretation

Write

\[
B_{r,h}(T)=L_{r,h}+T D_{r,h},
\]

where

\[
L_{r,h}=B_{r,h}(0)
\]

and

\[
D_{r,h}=\operatorname{diag}_{n=r+1}^{s-1}\bigl(2(2n+1)\bigr).
\]

Both matrices are real symmetric positive definite. Hence

\[
A_{r,h}
:=D_{r,h}^{-1/2}L_{r,h}D_{r,h}^{-1/2}
\]

is a positive-definite irreducible Jacobi matrix, and

\[
B_{r,h}(T)
=D_{r,h}^{1/2}(T I+A_{r,h})D_{r,h}^{1/2}.
\]

Taking determinants gives

\[
\boxed{
N_h^{(r)}(T)
=\det(D_{r,h})\det(TI+A_{r,h}).}
\tag{6}
\]

If

\[
0<\theta_1<\theta_2<\cdots<\theta_{h-1}
\]

are the eigenvalues of \(A_{r,h}\), then

\[
\boxed{
N_h^{(r)}(T)
=\left(\prod_{n=r+1}^{s-1}2(2n+1)\right)
 \prod_{j=1}^{h-1}(T+\theta_j).}
\tag{7}
\]

Consequences:

1. every root of \(N_h^{(r)}\) is real and strictly negative;
2. every root is simple, because the Jacobi matrix has nonzero off-diagonal entries;
3. all coefficients of \(N_h^{(r)}(T)\) are positive;
4. the leading coefficient is exactly
   \[
   \boxed{
   \operatorname{lc}_T N_h^{(r)}
   =\prod_{n=r+1}^{s-1}2(2n+1);}
   \tag{8}
   \]
5. for fixed \(r\), the roots interlace as \(h\) increases, by Cauchy interlacing for leading principal Jacobi submatrices.

This also corrects the spectral sign convention. The positive eigenvalues of the related Jacobi operator are the \(\theta_j\); the roots in the variable \(T\) are

\[
T_j=-\theta_j<0.
\]

Thus \(\lambda_r\) is not excluded because its index lies outside the interior interval. It is excluded over \(\mathbf R\) because \(\lambda_r\ge0\) while the entire Dirichlet \(T\)-spectrum lies in \(( -\infty,0)\).

Indeed, the same real argument excludes **every** nonnegative value of \(T\), including \(\lambda_n\) for an index \(n\) inside the block.

# 5. Small-gap computations

## Gap \(h=1\)

The block is empty:

\[
N_1^{(r)}(T)=1.
\]

There are no eigenvalues.

## Gap \(h=2\)

There is one interior index \(n=r+1\), so

\[
\boxed{
N_2^{(r)}(T)
=(r+1)^3+(r+2)^3+2(2r+3)T.}
\tag{9}
\]

Its unique root is

\[
\boxed{
T=-\frac{(r+1)^3+(r+2)^3}{2(2r+3)}<0.}
\tag{10}
\]

Examples:

| \(r\) | \(s=r+2\) | root of \(N_2^{(r)}\) | \(\lambda_r\) | \(\lambda_s\) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3 | \(-7/2=-3.5\) | 2 | 12 |
| 2 | 4 | \(-13/2=-6.5\) | 6 | 20 |

The gap-two finite-field exclusion is not really a positivity argument. If both special values vanished modulo a good prime, subtracting them would give

\[
2(2r+3)(\lambda_s-\lambda_r)=0.
\]

In the physical lower-half range, both factors are units, a contradiction. This is the exact linear-polynomial proof.

# 6. Why the real theorem does not imply the modulo-\(p\) theorem

The logical distinction is

```text
Gershgorin / positive definiteness:
    N_h^(r)(lambda_r) is a positive nonzero integer.

P3.2 requires:
    p does not divide that positive integer.
```

There is no implication from the first statement to the second unless one also proves, for example,

\[
0<N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)<p,
\]

or proves an explicit factorization whose factors are all known \(p\)-units. Neither is true here. The determinant grows far faster than \(p\) in the moving range.

Sign, order, positive definiteness, Gershgorin discs, and Cauchy interlacing are archimedean notions. After reduction to \(\mathbf F_p\):

- there is no positive half-line;
- a negative real root can reduce to any residue class;
- a positive determinant can become zero;
- real diagonal dominance does not imply \(p\)-adic diagonal dominance.

A nonarchimedean Gershgorin theorem would require a strict valuation inequality. Here the relevant diagonal and off-diagonal entries are generally all \(p\)-units, so cancellation modulo \(p\) is possible.

# 7. Exact good-characteristic failures of the proposed inference

## 7.1 The one-dimensional example

Take

\[
r=1,\qquad h=2,\qquad s=3.
\]

Then

\[
N_2^{(1)}(T)=35+10T.
\]

Over the reals its root is

\[
-\frac72<0.
\]

But modulo \(p=11\),

\[
-\frac72\equiv2=\lambda_1\pmod{11}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
N_2^{(1)}(\lambda_1)
=N_2^{(1)}(2)=55\equiv0\pmod{11}.}
\tag{12}
\]

This is good characteristic:

\[
11>2s+1=7.
\]

Thus the real statement “the only root is negative” does not prevent the reduction of that root from being the positive integer residue \(\lambda_1\).

At the other endpoint,

\[
N_2^{(1)}(\lambda_3)=N_2^{(1)}(12)=155\equiv1\pmod{11},
\]

so the proven gap-two two-root exclusion remains intact.

## 7.2 A strictly diagonally dominant \(2\times2\) example

Take

\[
r=1,\qquad h=3,\qquad s=4,\qquad T=\lambda_1=2.
\]

Then

\[
B_{1,3}(2)
=\begin{pmatrix}
55&-27\\
-27&119
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its Gershgorin intervals are

\[
[28,82],
\qquad
[92,146],
\]

so the matrix is visibly positive definite over \(\mathbf R\). Nevertheless,

\[
\det B_{1,3}(2)
=55\cdot119-27^2
=5816
=8\cdot727.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
N_3^{(1)}(\lambda_1)\equiv0\pmod{727},}
\tag{13}
\]

with

\[
727>2s+1=9.
\]

Modulo \(727\), the cancellation is explicit:

\[
55\cdot119\equiv2\equiv27^2\pmod{727}.
\]

This is an exact counterexample to the inference

\[
\text{strict real diagonal dominance}
\Longrightarrow
\text{nonzero determinant mod }p.
\]

## 7.3 The existing project fixture with one Apéry endpoint zero

The repository already records the strict lower-half fixture

\[
(p,r,s)=(41,10,13),
\]

for which

\[
b_{10}\equiv0,
\qquad
K(10,13)\equiv0,
\qquad
b_{13}\not\equiv0
\pmod{41}.
\]

Here \(h=3\), and

\[
N_3^{(10)}(T)
=\gamma_{11}(T)\gamma_{12}(T)-12^6.
\]

Modulo \(41\),

\[
\gamma_{11}(T)\equiv25+5T,
\qquad
\gamma_{12}(T)\equiv30+9T,
\qquad
12^6\equiv36.
\]

Since

\[
\lambda_{10}=110\equiv28\pmod{41},
\]

we get

\[
\gamma_{11}(\lambda_{10})\equiv1,
\qquad
\gamma_{12}(\lambda_{10})\equiv36,
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
N_3^{(10)}(\lambda_{10})
\equiv1\cdot36-36
\equiv0\pmod{41}.}
\tag{14}
\]

Over \(\mathbf R\), the same determinant is strictly positive. Thus even the extra condition \(b_r=0\) at the left endpoint does not turn archimedean positivity into a modulo-\(p\) unit statement.

This fixture is not a counterexample to the desired two-zero theorem, because

\[
b_{13}\not\equiv0\pmod{41}.
\]

Indeed,

\[
N_3^{(10)}(\lambda_{13})\equiv27\not\equiv0\pmod{41}.
\]

It nevertheless pinpoints exactly why Gershgorin cannot close the second endpoint.

# 8. Relation to the Q2327 equivalence

Suppose first only that

\[
b_r=K(r,r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Let \(z_n\) be the Dirichlet solution normalized by

\[
z_r=0,
\qquad
z_{r+1}=1.
\]

Continuant propagation gives

\[
\boxed{
z_s=
\frac{N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)}
{\displaystyle\prod_{m=r+2}^{s}m^3}.}
\tag{15}
\]

The sequence \(K(r,n)\) satisfies the same recurrence, so modulo \(p\)

\[
K(r,s)
=K(r,r+1)z_s.
\]

Q2288 proved that \(K(r,r+1)\) is a \(p\)-unit at an Apéry zero, and every denominator in (15) is a unit in the lower-half range. Hence

\[
\boxed{
K(r,s)\equiv0
\iff
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)\equiv0
\pmod p.}
\tag{16}
\]

Under the additional endpoint condition

\[
b_s\equiv0\pmod p,
\]

symmetry and the reverse Dirichlet propagation give the second evaluation in Q2327. Thus, on the two-zero locus,

\[
K(r,s)\equiv0
\]

forces

\[
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)\equiv
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_s)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

The real spectral theorem proves only that the two corresponding integers are positive. It supplies no contradiction to their simultaneous divisibility by \(p\).

In fact, equation (16) shows why the proposed argument cannot secretly be stronger: proving that \(N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)\) is a \(p\)-unit on the Apéry-zero locus is already equivalent to proving the desired Racah value is nonzero.

# 9. What interlacing and Gershgorin do—and do not—add

They do add useful exact information:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{all characteristic-zero Dirichlet roots are simple and negative},\\
N_h^{(r)}(T)>0\text{ for }T\ge0,\\
N_h^{(r)}(T)\text{ is strictly increasing for }T\ge0,\\
\text{and the roots interlace as the interval is enlarged.}
\end{gathered}}
\]

For example, from (7),

\[
N_h^{(r)}(T)
=c\prod_j(T+\theta_j)
\]

with \(c>0\) and \(\theta_j>0\), so monotonicity on \([0,\infty)\) is immediate.

But none of these statements controls prime divisors of the special integer values

\[
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r),
\qquad
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_s).
\]

Cauchy interlacing is not preserved as an ordering statement in \(\mathbf F_p\). Gershgorin discs use an archimedean absolute value. Positivity of coefficients does not prevent modular cancellation.

The only way these real bounds could finish the proof would be through a new arithmetic estimate, for example:

1. an upper bound showing the determinant lies strictly between \(0\) and \(p\), which is false in the moving range;
2. an explicit factorization into known \(p\)-units;
3. a Bézout identity
   \[
   U_{r,h}N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)
   +V_{r,h}N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_s)
   =E_{r,h}
   \]
   with \(E_{r,h}\) a known \(p\)-unit;
4. a Smith-normal-form or resultant theorem proving that every common prime divisor belongs to an excluded small set;
5. a genuinely \(p\)-adic diagonal-dominance theorem using valuations supplied by the Apéry endpoint conditions.

No such input follows from the real Jacobi spectrum alone.

# Final conclusion

The proposed Gershgorin computation should be retained as a correct lemma:

\[
\boxed{
B_{r,h}(T)>0\text{ for all real }T\ge0,
\quad
\operatorname{Roots}_{\mathbf R}N_h^{(r)}\subset(-\infty,0).}
\]

It gives a complete answer to parts (a)--(d) over characteristic zero:

- the small-gap roots are all negative;
- \(\lambda_r\) and \(\lambda_s\) are not real Dirichlet eigenvalues;
- the leading coefficient is the expected product
  \(\prod 2(2n+1)\);
- Gershgorin and Cauchy interlacing rigorously separate the real roots from every nonnegative \(\lambda_n\).

But the P3.2 target is a congruence statement. The implication

\[
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)>0
\Longrightarrow
N_h^{(r)}(\lambda_r)\not\equiv0\pmod p
\]

is false, as shown explicitly by

\[
N_3^{(1)}(\lambda_1)=8\cdot727.
\]

Therefore the answer to the proposed decisive step is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The Gershgorin argument is correct over }\mathbf R,
\text{ but it does not prove finite-field nonvanishing.}}
\]

The nonmate two-Apéry-zero theorem remains the arithmetic assertion that the physical prime cannot divide both special positive determinant values. That is precisely the unresolved two-root exclusion, not a consequence of real spectral separation.
