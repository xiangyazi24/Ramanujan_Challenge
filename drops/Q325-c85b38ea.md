## 1.1 Why the result controls all complex roots

The proof in `meso_result.tex` uses the \((d-1)\times(d-1)\) symmetric tridiagonal matrix

\[
D_d(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(x+1)&(x+2)^3&&\\
(x+2)^3&P(x+2)&(x+3)^3&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&& (x+d-1)^3&P(x+d-1)
\end{pmatrix},
\]

for which

\[
\det D_d(x)=N_d(x).
\]

For \(\Re x\ge-1\), put

\[
z_i=x+i+\frac12.
\]

Then

\[
P(x+i)=34z_i^3+\frac32z_i.
\]

Since \(|z_i|\ge 1/2\), the diagonal entry satisfies

\[
|P(x+i)|
\ge |z_i|\left(34|z_i|^2-\frac32\right)
\ge 28|z_i|^3,
\]

whereas the sum of the two possible off-diagonal magnitudes in row \(i\) is at most

\[
|z_i-1/2|^3+|z_i+1/2|^3
\le 16|z_i|^3.
\]

Hence \(D_d(x)\) is strictly row-diagonally dominant and therefore invertible in the entire half-plane \(\Re x\ge-1\).

The reversal identity

\[
D_d(-x-d-1)=-J D_d(x)J
\]

then excludes the opposite half-plane \(\Re x\le-d\). Consequently every complex root \(\alpha\) of \(N_d\) satisfies

\[
\boxed{-d<\Re\alpha<-1.}
\tag{3}
\]

Now compare the two shifted factors in Q325. A root of \(N_j(x)\) lies in

\[
-j<\Re x<-1,
\]

while a root of \(N_{k-j}(x+j)\) satisfies

\[
-(k-j)<\Re(x+j)<-1,
\]

or equivalently

\[
-k<\Re x<-j-1.
\]

These are disjoint vertical strips:

\[
\underbrace{-k<\Re x<-j-1}_{N_{k-j}(x+j)=0}
\qquad\big|\qquad
\underbrace{-j<\Re x<-1}_{N_j(x)=0}.
\]

There is even a unit-width gap between them. Thus no approximation by half-integers and no separate treatment of real versus nonreal roots is needed. The tridiagonal argument already separates **all complex roots**.

# 2. The new congruence now gives pairwise coprimality immediately

The renewal identity, with \(d=k-j\), is

\[
N_k(x)
=N_{j+1}(x)N_{k-j}(x+j)
 -(x+j+1)^6N_j(x)N_{k-j-1}(x+j+1).
\tag{4}
\]

Therefore

\[
N_k(x)\equiv N_{j+1}(x)N_{k-j}(x+j)\pmod{N_j(x)}.
\]

Since the repository proves

\[
\gcd(N_j,N_{j+1})=1,
\]

Euclid’s lemma in \(\mathbf Q[x]\) gives, up to multiplication by a nonzero rational constant,

\[
\gcd(N_j,N_k)
=\gcd\bigl(N_j,N_{k-j}(x+j)\bigr).
\]

The right-hand side is \(1\) by the separated strips above. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\gcd_{\mathbf Q[x]}(N_j,N_k)=1
\qquad(1\le j<k).}
\tag{5}
\]

Since \(N_h=-\widetilde W_h\), the identical conclusion holds for the gap polynomials:

\[
\boxed{
\gcd_{\mathbf Q[x]}(\widetilde W_j,\widetilde W_k)=1
\qquad(j\ne k).}
\tag{6}
\]

Thus polynomial irreducibility of the centered factors \(f_k\) or \(h_{2m}\) is not needed for this step.

# 3. What the standard tridiagonal simplicity theorem actually says

Let \(T\) be a **fixed real symmetric** or **fixed Hermitian** tridiagonal matrix whose off-diagonal entries are all nonzero. Then its eigenvalues are indeed simple.

The proof is short. For a fixed eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the first row of

\[
(T-\lambda I)v=0
\]

determines \(v_2\) from \(v_1\), the second row determines \(v_3\), and so on. If \(v_1=0\), all components vanish. Hence the eigenspace has dimension at most one. Since a real symmetric or Hermitian matrix is diagonalizable, algebraic and geometric multiplicities agree, so every eigenvalue is simple.

Two qualifications are essential.

## 3.1 Complex symmetric is not Hermitian

At a nonreal root \(x_0\), the specialized Apéry matrix \(D_k(x_0)\) is complex symmetric in the transpose sense,

\[
D_k(x_0)^T=D_k(x_0),
\]

but generally not Hermitian:

\[
D_k(x_0)^*\ne D_k(x_0).
\]

Complex symmetric matrices need not be diagonalizable. For example,

\[
S=
\begin{pmatrix}
 i&1\\
 1&-i
\end{pmatrix}
\]

is symmetric, tridiagonal, and has nonzero off-diagonal entry, but

\[
S^2=0,
\qquad S\ne0.
\]

Its characteristic polynomial is \(\lambda^2\), so the sole eigenvalue \(0\) is repeated and defective.

Thus the theorem “unreduced symmetric tridiagonal implies simple spectrum” is valid over the real/Hermitian setting, not for arbitrary complex-symmetric specializations. This already prevents it from treating the \(2(k-1)\) nonreal roots of the Apéry determinant.

## 3.2 Unreducedness gives geometric multiplicity one, not automatically algebraic multiplicity one

For an arbitrary unreduced tridiagonal matrix over \(\mathbf C\), the same recurrence argument still shows that every eigenspace has dimension at most one. Equivalently, the matrix is nonderogatory. But without normality or Hermitian symmetry, an eigenvalue may have one-dimensional eigenspace and a nontrivial Jordan block. The matrix \(S\) above is the \(2\times2\) example.

# 4. Simple eigenvalue does not imply a prescribed cofactor is nonzero

This is the decisive flaw in the proposed adjugate argument.

Suppose \(A\) is symmetric and has corank one at an eigenvalue. If \(v\) spans its kernel, then

\[
\operatorname{adj}(A)=\gamma vv^T
\tag{7}
\]

for some nonzero scalar \(\gamma\). Therefore

\[
C_{jj}(A)=\gamma v_j^2.
\tag{8}
\]

A simple eigenvalue guarantees that the adjugate matrix is nonzero, but it does **not** guarantee that every entry of the adjugate is nonzero. A diagonal cofactor vanishes exactly when the corresponding eigenvector component vanishes.

Interior components of eigenvectors of unreduced real symmetric tridiagonal matrices can vanish. Consider the standard Jacobi pencil

\[
F(\lambda)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda&-1&0\\
-1&\lambda&-1\\
0&-1&\lambda
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det F(\lambda)=\lambda(\lambda^2-2),
\]

so all three eigenvalues are simple. At \(\lambda=0\), however,

\[
v=(1,0,-1)^T
\]

spans the kernel. Consequently

\[
C_{22}(\lambda)=\lambda^2,
\qquad
C_{22}(0)=0.
\]

Indeed,

\[
\gcd\bigl(\det F,C_{22}\bigr)=\lambda
\]

even though the full matrix has simple spectrum and every off-diagonal entry is nonzero.

What irreducibility does force is that the **first and last** components of every eigenvector are nonzero. It does not rule out isolated zeros in the interior. Correspondingly, endpoint principal minors strictly interlace the full characteristic polynomial, but an interior principal cofactor can share an eigenvalue with the full matrix.

There is also an important wording distinction:

- simple spectrum implies that \(\det(T-\lambda I)\) and **all entries of the adjugate collectively** have no common zero;
- it does not imply that \(\det(T-\lambda I)\) is coprime to each prescribed cofactor \(C_{jj}\).

Q325 needs the second, stronger statement.

# 5. The spectral parameter \(x\) is nonlinear, so matrix-eigenvalue simplicity and polynomial-root simplicity are different

The roots of \(N_k(x)=\det D_k(x)\) are nonlinear eigenvalues of the matrix polynomial \(D_k(x)\): they are parameter values at which \(0\) enters the spectrum of the specialized matrix. This is not the ordinary characteristic-polynomial situation \(D(x)=T-xI\).

Even if \(0\) is a simple eigenvalue of \(D(x_0)\), the determinant can have a multiple zero as a function of \(x\). For example,

\[
G(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&1+x^2
\end{pmatrix}
\]

is real symmetric and unreduced for every real \(x\). At \(x=0\), its eigenvalues are \(0\) and \(2\), so the zero eigenvalue of the specialized matrix is simple. Nevertheless,

\[
\det G(x)=x^2,
\]

and \(x=0\) is a double root of the determinant polynomial.

More generally, if \(D(x_0)\) is symmetric of corank one and \(v\) spans its kernel, then from (7)

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\det D(x)\Big|_{x=x_0}
=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\operatorname{adj}(D(x_0))D'(x_0)\bigr)
=\gamma\,v^T D'(x_0)v.
\tag{9}
\]

Thus:

- simplicity of \(x_0\) as a determinant root requires the **crossing condition**
  \[
  v^T D'(x_0)v\ne0;
  \]
- nonvanishing of the \(j\)-th cofactor requires the independent **support condition**
  \[
  v_j\ne0.
  \]

Neither condition implies the other.

Therefore the chain

\[
\text{simple eigenvalue of }D_k(x_0)
\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{disc}_x N_k\ne0
\Longrightarrow
C_{jj}(x_0)\ne0
\]

is invalid at both arrows.

# 6. What is true for the Apéry cofactor

Although the generic spectral shortcut fails, the desired cofactor statement is true for the Apéry matrix. It follows cleanly after the shifted-coprimality theorem.

## 6.1 No off-diagonal entry vanishes at a root of \(N_k\)

The off-diagonal entries of \(D_k(x)\) vanish only at

\[
x=-m,\qquad 2\le m\le k-1.
\]

At such a point the matrix splits into two blocks:

\[
D_k(-m)=D_m(-m)\oplus D_{k-m+1}(-1).
\]

Hence

\[
N_k(-m)=N_m(-m)N_{k-m+1}(-1).
\]

Using the repository’s dictionary \(N_h(r)=U_{h-1}(r+1)\), reflection, and

\[
U_n(0)=(n!)^3b_n,
\]

where \(b_n\) is the \(n\)-th Apéry number, one gets

\[
N_m(-m)=(-1)^{m-1}((m-1)!)^3b_{m-1},
\]

and

\[
N_{k-m+1}(-1)=((k-m)!)^3b_{k-m}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
N_k(-m)
=(-1)^{m-1}igl((m-1)!(k-m)!\bigr)^3
b_{m-1}b_{k-m}\ne0.}
\tag{10}
\]

Thus every singular specialization \(D_k(x_0)\) is genuinely unreduced. Its kernel is one-dimensional and its adjugate is nonzero of rank one.

Notice that “the off-diagonal entries are nonzero polynomials” alone would not suffice; what is needed is nonvanishing **after specialization at every determinant root**. Formula (10) supplies that missing check for this family.

## 6.2 Kernel coordinates are the leading continuants

Let \(x_0\) be a root of \(N_k\), and normalize the unique kernel vector \(v\) by \(v_1=1\). Forward substitution in the tridiagonal recurrence gives

\[
\boxed{
 v_j
 =(-1)^{j-1}
 \frac{N_j(x_0)}{\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{j-1}(x_0+r+1)^3}}
\qquad(1\le j\le k-1).}
\tag{11}
\]

All denominator factors are nonzero by (10). Hence

\[
v_j=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
N_j(x_0)=0.
\tag{12}
\]

This gives the exact matrix interpretation of pairwise coprimality:

\[
\gcd(N_j,N_k)=1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every kernel vector of every }D_k(x_0)	ext{ has }v_j\ne0.
\]

Deleting row and column \(j\) disconnects the path, so

\[
C_{jj}(x)
=N_j(x)N_{k-j}(x+j).
\tag{13}
\]

At a singular specialization, (7), (11), and (13) yield

\[
C_{jj}(x_0)=\gamma v_j^2.
\]

Now apply the already-proved pairwise coprimality (5): \(N_j(x_0)\ne0\). Therefore \(v_j\ne0\), and hence

\[
\boxed{
C_{jj}(x_0)\ne0
\qquad
\text{for every root }x_0	ext{ of }N_k
\text{ and every }1\le j\le k-1.}
\tag{14}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\gcd_{\mathbf Q[x]}\bigl(N_k(x),C_{jj}(x)\bigr)=1.}
\tag{15}
\]

So the Apéry singular vectors have **full support**. This is a special theorem of this family, obtained from renewal plus separated root strips—not from generic Jacobi-matrix simplicity.

# 7. Direct answers to the four questions

## Question 1

**Yes.** The shifted coprimality is proved by the continuant/tridiagonal structure. The repository’s proposition

\[
\operatorname{Res}_x(N_d(x),N_r(x+d))>0
\]

is exactly the required statement with \(d=j\), \(r=k-j\). Its proof is diagonal dominance plus reversal symmetry, giving disjoint complex root strips. Combining it with the renewal congruence and adjacent coprimality proves \(\gcd(N_j,N_k)=1\).

## Question 2

For a **fixed real symmetric or Hermitian** unreduced tridiagonal matrix, yes, the eigenvalues are simple. For a **complex symmetric** unreduced tridiagonal matrix, no: such a matrix can be defective. In either case, simple spectrum does not imply that every prescribed diagonal cofactor is nonzero at every eigenvalue.

## Question 3

For a general unreduced symmetric tridiagonal matrix, **no**. At a simple eigenvalue,

\[
C_{jj}=\gamma v_j^2,
\]

and an interior component \(v_j\) may vanish. The \(3\times3\) path matrix gives an explicit counterexample. For the Apéry matrix, however, the answer is **yes**, because shifted/pairwise coprimality proves that all coordinates in (11) are nonzero.

## Question 4

**No, not immediately.** “All off-diagonal entries are nonzero as polynomials” is weaker than “all off-diagonal entries remain nonzero at every determinant root”; formula (10) supplies the latter. Even after that check, unreducedness gives only a one-dimensional kernel, not full support of the kernel vector. The missing full-support assertion is precisely the coprimality theorem one is trying to prove.

Thus the valid dependency chain is

\[
\boxed{
\text{tridiagonal diagonal dominance + reflection}
\Longrightarrow
\text{shifted resultant }\mathcal R_{j,k-j}>0
\Longrightarrow
\text{renewal + adjacent gcd}
\Longrightarrow
\gcd(N_j,N_k)=1
\Longrightarrow
C_{jj}(x_0)\ne0.}
\]

The invalid chain is

\[
\text{unreduced tridiagonal}
\Longrightarrow
\text{simple spectrum}
\Longrightarrow
\text{all cofactors nonzero}.
\]

# 8. Drop-in proof for the paper

```latex
\begin{proposition}[Shifted block coprimality]
Let
\[
N_0=0,\qquad N_1=1,\qquad
N_{h+1}(x)=P(x+h)N_h(x)-(x+h)^6N_{h-1}(x),
\]
where $P(t)=(2t+1)(17t^2+17t+5)$.  Then for all integers
$d,r\geq 1$,
\[
\gcd_{\mathbf Q[x]}\bigl(N_d(x),N_r(x+d)\bigr)=1.
\]
For $d,r\geq2$ the corresponding resultant is positive.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The cases $d=1$ or $r=1$ are immediate from $N_1=1$.
For $h\geq2$, every complex root of $N_h$ lies in the strict strip
\[
-h<\Re x<-1.
\]
Indeed, the tridiagonal continuant matrix is strictly diagonally
 dominant for $\Re x\geq-1$, while reversal gives
$D_h(-x-h-1)=-JD_h(x)J$ and excludes $\Re x\leq-h$.
Thus the roots of $N_d(x)$ lie in
\[
-d<\Re x<-1,
\]
whereas the roots of $N_r(x+d)$ lie in
\[
-d-r<\Re x<-d-1.
\]
The strips are disjoint, proving coprimality.  The root-product formula,
positive leading coefficients, and conjugate pairing show that the
resultant is positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Pairwise coprimality of the Ap\'ery gap continuants]
For all distinct positive integers $j,k$,
\[
\gcd_{\mathbf Q[x]}(N_j,N_k)=1.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\gcd_{\mathbf Q[x]}(\widetilde W_j,\widetilde W_k)=1.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume $1\leq j<k$ and put $d=k-j$.  The renewal identity is
\[
N_k(x)=N_{j+1}(x)N_d(x+j)
 -(x+j+1)^6N_j(x)N_{d-1}(x+j+1).
\]
Modulo $N_j(x)$ this becomes
\[
N_k(x)\equiv N_{j+1}(x)N_d(x+j).
\]
Since $\gcd(N_j,N_{j+1})=1$, any common irreducible divisor of
$N_j$ and $N_k$ would divide $N_d(x+j)$.  This contradicts shifted
block coprimality.  Hence $\gcd(N_j,N_k)=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Full support of singular Ap\'ery continuant vectors]
Let $x_0$ be a root of $N_k$.  Then every off-diagonal entry of
$D_k(x_0)$ is nonzero, $D_k(x_0)$ has one-dimensional kernel, and every
nonzero vector $v\in\ker D_k(x_0)$ has all coordinates nonzero.
Consequently every diagonal cofactor satisfies
\[
C_{jj}(x_0)\ne0.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
An off-diagonal zero would have $x_0=-m$ with $2\leq m\leq k-1$.
At that point the matrix splits into two blocks and
\[
N_k(-m)=(-1)^{m-1}
\bigl((m-1)!(k-m)!\bigr)^3b_{m-1}b_{k-m}\ne0,
\]
a contradiction.  Normalize a kernel vector by $v_1=1$.  Forward
substitution gives
\[
v_j=(-1)^{j-1}
\frac{N_j(x_0)}{\prod_{r=1}^{j-1}(x_0+r+1)^3}.
\]
Pairwise coprimality implies $N_j(x_0)\ne0$ for $j<k$, so every
coordinate is nonzero.  Since the adjugate of a symmetric corank-one
matrix is a nonzero scalar multiple of $vv^T$, all diagonal cofactors
are nonzero.
\end{proof}
```
