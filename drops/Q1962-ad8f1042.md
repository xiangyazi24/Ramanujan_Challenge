## Hasse factor correction

For a reciprocal polynomial

\[
B(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b_jX^j,\qquad B(-1-X)=B(X),
\]

one may write a centered factorization after the involution change of variables \(Z=X(X+1)\). A square factor appearing for small primes has the form

\[
B(X)=\Delta(X)C(Z)^2,
\]

but the factor \(\Delta\) and the degree of \(C\) depend on the characteristic. The p=19 factorization is therefore an observed Hasse decomposition, not a universal theorem without an additional supersingularity/Hasse invariant input.

## Recurrence constraint

Writing the Apéry recurrence as a polynomial identity gives, for coefficients of B, a differential operator constraint. The coefficient recurrence translates to a third-order relation of the schematic form

\[
\mathcal D_5(B)=0\quad\text{mod }(X^p-X),
\]

with correction terms supported at the defect polynomial \((X^p-X)Q_2(X)\). The defect records exactly the failure of the recurrence at the two characteristic endpoints; it does not by itself impose a residue identity for

\[
\sum_{j\le m-2}\frac{(m-j)^2-1}{b_j}.
\]

## Elimination consequence

Assume the gap conditions

\[
b_{m-1}=b_{m+1}=0
\]

and impose \(\Sigma=0\). Clearing denominators gives a polynomial condition

\[
F(b_0,\ldots,b_m)=3-6b_m\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}((m-j)^2-1)\prod_{i\ne j}b_i=0.
\]

The recurrence equations and reciprocity equations define an algebraic ideal. A proof would require showing that the saturation of this ideal by the non-gap coefficients contains 1 after adjoining F. The p=19 row elimination indicates this happens in that finite case, but does not provide a uniform identity.

1. a universal differential resultant proving that \(F\) cannot vanish in the saturated recurrence ideal;

The current Hasse square structure alone is insufficient, and the p=19 elimination data should be treated as evidence rather than a proof.
