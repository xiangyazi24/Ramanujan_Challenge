# The most promising route: the Apéry zero process as a sparse random walk on the geometric monodromy group

## Executive claim

The most promising new object is not the Mellin transform itself, but the **Mellin-zero incidence variety over the monodromy torsor**.

The proposed object is:

\[
\mathcal Z_{p}^{\mathrm{inc}}
=\{(g,r): \operatorname{Tr}(\rho(g)\Phi(r))=0\},
\]

where:

- \(g\in G_{\mathrm{geom}}(\mathbb F_p)\) is the Frobenius conjugacy class,
- \(\Phi(r)\) is the geometric Frobenius orbit attached to the parameter \(r\),
- the zero condition is a codimension-one condition inside the monodromy group.

The new theorem one wants is a **Sparse Incidence Independence Theorem**:

> For a geometrically irreducible sheaf with connected monodromy \(G=SL_n\), if the Mellin incidence divisor is geometrically irreducible and has codimension one, then the defining-characteristic zero sets for distinct Frobenius fibers become asymptotically independent.

The Apéry K3 family should be the first nontrivial example.

---

# 1. Why monodromy gives sparsity

The key distinction from \(\binom{2n}{n}\) is not merely recurrence order. It is that the Apéry Mellin condition is a **rare geometric intersection**.

The Mellin transform is a trace function

\[
T_p(r)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb F_p^*}\theta_p(a)a^r.
\]

The zero condition

\[
T_p(r)=0
\]

is a condition that Frobenius lies in a hypersurface of the monodromy group.

For large monodromy:

\[
G_{geom}=SL_2,
\]

this hypersurface has relative codimension one. Therefore Lang-Weil gives approximately

\[
\#Z_p\approx O(1)
\]

rather than density \(1/2\) as for the binomial sequence.

The same geometric fact that makes zeros rare should also control correlations.

---

# 2. The missing theorem

The needed theorem is not ordinary Chebotarev independence.

Chebotarev gives independence of Frobenius elements:

\[
(Frob_p,Frob_q)\sim \mu_G\times\mu_G.
\]

But we need independence after applying the nonlinear statistic

\[
F(g)=\{r:T(g,r)=0\}.
\]

The proposed theorem is:

## Sparse Frobenius statistic theorem

Let \(\mathcal H\) be a lisse sheaf on a curve over \(\mathbb Q\) with connected geometric monodromy \(G\). Assume:

1. \(G\) is semisimple;
2. the Mellin incidence variety

\[
\mathcal I=\{(g,r):T(g,r)=0\}
\]

is geometrically irreducible;
3. the projection \(\mathcal I\to G\) has generic finite fibers.

Then for distinct primes \(p,q,r\), the random variables

\[
Z_p,Z_q,Z_r
\]

are asymptotically independent.

In particular,

\[
\Pr(CRT(z_p,z_q)\bmod r\in Z_r)
=
\frac{|Z_r|}{r}+o(1/r).
\]

---

# 3. Why this avoids the binomial counterexample

For the central binomial sequence:

\[
\binom{2n}{n}\equiv0\pmod p
\]

is caused by valuation inequalities in factorials.

The zero set is not a Frobenius incidence variety. It is a combinatorial congruence condition:

\[
Z_p=\{r: r\text{ lies in a large interval pattern}\}.
\]

The density is positive.

For Apéry:

\[
Z_p=\{r:Frob_{p,r}\in D\},
\]

where \(D\subset SL_2\) is a thin divisor.

Thus the conjecture is a universality statement about **thin Frobenius divisors**, not about all diagonal sequences.

---

# 4. Proof strategy

## Step 1: Replace zero sets by incidence geometry

Construct the universal incidence scheme:

\[
\mathcal I_H
=\{(x,g):T_x(g)=0\}.
\]

The Apéry input needed:

\[
\operatorname{Mon}(\mathcal H)=SL_2.
\]

Then prove:

\[
\mathcal I_H
\text{ is absolutely irreducible.}
\]

This is the genuinely new geometric statement.

---

## Step 2: Study fiber products

For two primes, independence requires controlling

\[
\mathcal I_p\times\mathcal I_q.
\]

The obstruction would be hidden components corresponding to arithmetic correlations.

The desired theorem is:

\[
\operatorname{Irr}(\mathcal I_p\times\mathcal I_q)
=\text{expected component only}.
\]

This is the analogue of geometric independence in Katz's work, but applied to the zero divisor itself.

---

## Step 3: Use Deligne bounds on the incidence variety

Once irreducibility is known, point counts give:

\[
\#(\mathcal I_p\times\mathcal I_q)(\mathbb F_r)
=
\frac{|Z_p||Z_q||Z_r|}{r^3}
+O(r^{-1/2}).
\]

The error term is the cross-prime independence estimate.

---

# 5. Concrete object to compute first

The first computation should not be another zero scan. It should test the geometric irreducibility conjecture.

For small primes construct the incidence polynomial:

\[
P_p(X)=\prod_{z\in Z_p}(X-z).
\]

Then test whether shifted CRT correlations satisfy

\[
\gcd(P_p(X),P_q(aX+b))
\]

only for the forced symmetry factors.

The prediction is:

\[
\gcd(P_p,P_q^{CRT})=1
\]

for almost all prime pairs after removing reflection.

This directly tests whether there is an algebraic correspondence between different characteristic zero sets.

---

# 6. Relation to Dwork and crystalline theory

The bridge is therefore not Gross-Koblitz or unit roots alone.

Those control:

\[
v_p(T_p(r)).
\]

But the independence problem concerns:

\[
\mathbf 1_{T_p(r)=0}.
\]

The correct object is the **zero divisor of the Frobenius trace function**, viewed as a geometric subvariety.

Crystalline theory gives local structure; monodromy gives global randomness. The missing theorem combines them:

> Frobenius elements are independent not only as group elements, but after restriction to any geometrically irreducible sparse Frobenius incidence divisor.

---

# Final answer

The single most promising invention is therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{Sparse Frobenius Incidence Independence Theorem}
}
\]

for thin Mellin-zero divisors of large-monodromy sheaves.

The Apéry-specific ingredient is not simply K3, recurrence, or supercongruence. It is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The zero condition is a codimension-one Frobenius incidence condition in }SL_2.
}
\]

That property simultaneously explains:

1. why \(|Z_p|=O(1)\);
2. why binomial coefficients fail;
3. why cross-prime independence should hold.

The first decisive experiment is to compute the irreducibility/correlation of the finite-field incidence polynomials, not more Mellin moments. If that succeeds, the path to a genuine theorem is through algebraic geometry of the zero divisor rather than through analytic equidistribution of traces.
