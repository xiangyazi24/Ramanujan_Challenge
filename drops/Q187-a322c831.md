# Effective degree of trace functions

## Short answer

No: there is no general theorem saying that

\[
\sum_x \psi(c\,\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|\mathcal M))=O_C(\sqrt p)
\]

merely because \(\mathcal M\) has bounded rank and conductor.

The obstruction is exactly the one identified in the question: Deligne's bounded-conductor theory controls **linear operations on sheaves**, but the operation

\[
\mathcal M\mapsto \psi(\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal M))
\]

is not a linear sheaf operation. It is an Artin-Schreier pullback through the trace function, and the conductor of that pullback can grow with the complexity of the trace map.

There is no universal "effective degree = rank" principle.

---

# 1. What bounded conductor actually gives

Suppose

\[
t(x)=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|\mathcal F)
\]

for a lisse sheaf \(\mathcal F\) of rank \(r\) and conductor \(C\).

Then standard trace-function theory gives estimates for sums of the form

\[
\sum_x t(x)\chi(x),
\]

or more generally sums where the integrand is itself the trace function of another bounded-conductor sheaf.

For example:

\[
\sum_x \mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|\mathcal F)
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|\mathcal G)
\]

is handled by

\[
\mathcal F\otimes\mathcal G.
\]

The conductor remains bounded.

But

\[
\psi(t(x))
\]

is different.

To obtain it as a trace function one needs the pullback

\[
t^*\mathcal L_\psi,
\]

where \(\mathcal L_\psi\) is the Artin-Schreier sheaf on \(\mathbb A^1\).

The conductor of this sheaf depends on the ramification of the map

\[
t:X\rightarrow\mathbb A^1.
\]

It is not controlled only by the rank of \(\mathcal F\).

---

# 2. Simple counterexample to the proposed principle

Take the rank-one sheaf

\[
\mathcal F=\mathbf 1.
\]

Its conductor is zero.

But choose a function

\[
f(x)=x^{p-1}-x.
\]

The trace function is still algebraically simple, but

\[
\psi(f(x))
\]

has Artin-Schreier conductor depending on the pole order of \(f\).

The exponential sum is governed by

\[
\deg(f)-1,
\]

not by the rank of the original sheaf.

Thus a rank-one source can produce arbitrarily high exponential conductor after composition.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{rank of }\mathcal F \neq \text{effective degree of }\psi\circ t.}
\]

---

# 3. What Fouvry-Kowalski-Michel gives

The FKM framework defines algebraic trace functions by bounded conductor sheaves.

Their main estimates concern sums like

\[
\sum_x K(x)\chi(x)
\]

where \(K\) itself is a trace function.

They prove square-root cancellation when the resulting sheaf operation has bounded conductor.

The operation

\[
K(x)\mapsto \psi(K(x))
\]

is outside this framework unless one proves separately that the Artin-Schreier pullback has bounded conductor.

For a general trace function this is false.

---

# 4. The Apéry case

For the Mellin sheaf \(M\), we know

\[
b_j=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_j|M).
\]

The desired estimate is

\[
S_c=\sum_j\psi(cb_j)=O(\sqrt p).
\]

This requires the sheaf

\[
b^*\mathcal L_{\psi,c}
\]

on the character parameter space.

Its conductor is determined by the ramification of

\[
b:j\mapsto b_j.
\]

The fact that \(M\) has rank two and tame monodromy does not automatically imply that this pullback has bounded conductor.

The previous conductor calculation correctly found the danger:

- the polynomial representative of \(b_j\) has degree roughly \(p/2\);
- the Artin-Schreier conductor can therefore be of size \(O(p)\).

Deligne then gives only

\[
|S_c|\ll p^{3/2}.
\]

This is not the desired estimate.

---

# 5. Is there hidden structure that improves the conductor?

Possibly, but it must come from additional geometry of the trace map, not from rank alone.

The required theorem would be something like:

\[
\mathrm{cond}(b^*\mathcal L_\psi)=O(1).
\]

Equivalently, the map

\[
b:X\rightarrow\mathbb A^1
\]

would have bounded ramification complexity despite having a polynomial representative of growing degree.

This can happen for special maps, but it is exceptional.

A typical source would be:

1. a hidden quotient map;
2. a finite monodromy factorization;
3. a rigid geometric family;
4. a description of \(b\) as a trace of a bounded-rank representation of a fixed algebraic group.

The current Mellin description does not provide this automatically.

---

# 6. Does Sym^2(M) help?

We have

\[
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Sym}^2M)
=\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2
=b_j^2-p^w.
\]

This is useful for polynomial expressions in \(b_j\), for example moments:

\[
\sum_j b_j^2.
\]

But it does not linearize

\[
\psi(cb_j).
\]

The Artin-Schreier exponential is sensitive to the actual trace value, not merely its algebraic relations.

Knowing \(b_j^2\) does not recover a bounded-conductor sheaf for \(\psi(b_j)\).

---

# 7. What theorem would solve the Apéry one-prime problem?

The missing statement is genuinely stronger than current trace-function technology:

> The Apéry trace map has bounded Artin-Schreier complexity.

Namely:

\[
\mathrm{cond}(b^*\mathcal L_\psi)\le C
\]

uniformly in \(p\).

Then Deligne would give

\[
|S_c|\ll_C\sqrt p,
\]

and Fourier inversion would imply

\[
|Z_p|=O_C(1).
\]

But proving this bounded pullback conductor is exactly the missing theorem.

---

# Conclusion

The notion of "effective degree" exists only after specifying the sheaf operation being performed. For ordinary trace-function operations, rank and conductor control complexity. For the nonlinear exponential operation

\[
t\mapsto\psi(t),
\]

there is no rank-only bound.

The Apéry problem is therefore not solved by FKM/Katz trace-function theory alone. The decisive missing ingredient is a geometric explanation why the Artin-Schreier pullback of the Apéry trace map has bounded conductor despite the apparent degree \(\sim p\).

If such a hidden bounded-conductor factorization exists, it would indeed prove the desired \(O(\sqrt p)\) bound. But discovering that factorization is the main theorem, not a consequence of existing bounded-conductor theory.
