# Verdict

No: the 2295 successful evaluations cannot be converted by Schwartz--Zippel or black-box polynomial identity testing into the all-gap theorem.

There is a narrow literal statement that is true but much weaker:

> For one fixed prime `p`, one exact value
> \(K_p(a,b)\ne 0\) already proves, deterministically and without any probability estimate, that the formal polynomial \(K_p(T,U)\) is not the zero polynomial.

That is not the required statement. The target is zero-freeness on every admissible zero pair, for every prime:

\[
\forall p\;\forall(r,s)\in Z_p^{\mathrm{nm}},
\qquad K_p(\lambda_r,\lambda_s)\ne0,
\]

where

\[
Z_p^{\mathrm{nm}}
 =\{(r,s):A_p(\lambda_r)=A_p(\lambda_s)=0,
             \ (r,s)\text{ nonmate}\}.
\]

PIT proves an existential statement,

\[
K_p\not\equiv0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists(t,u)\;K_p(t,u)\ne0,
\]

whereas the all-gap use requires the universal statement above. A nonzero polynomial can still vanish at one, many, or almost all points of the constrained locus.

Taking the scan report as exact, it proves the finite theorem for the tested range \(p\le200000\). It does not supply the missing uniform argument for larger primes.

# 1. `F(p)` is not a polynomial to which PIT applies

The notation

\[
F(p)=K_p(\lambda_r,\lambda_s)
\]

hides three changes.

First, there can be several zero pairs for one prime, so the honest object is the partial function

\[
\mathcal F(p,r,s)=K_p(\lambda_r,\lambda_s),
\qquad (r,s)\in Z_p^{\mathrm{nm}}.
\]

Second,

\[
K_p\in\mathbf F_p[T,U],
\]

so values belonging to different primes live in different fields. They are not evaluations of one polynomial over one fixed coefficient field.

Third, the polynomial itself changes with `p`: its truncation index, coefficients, and degree all depend on `p`. A bound

\[
\deg_T K_p,\deg_U K_p\le p-1
\]

is a moving bound on a family of polynomials. It is not a degree bound for the scalar map \(p\mapsto\mathcal F(p,r,s)\). In particular, 2295 values at different characteristics cannot be pooled as 2295 interpolation points for one degree-\(O(p)\) polynomial.

To make cross-prime interpolation meaningful one would first need a single characteristic-zero polynomial or rational function, for example

\[
\mathscr K(P,R,S)\in\mathbf Z[P,R,S],
\]

with degree bounded independently of `p`, together with a theorem that every scanned value is the reduction of
\(\mathscr K(p,r,s)\). No such object follows from the degree of \(K_p\) in `T,U`; the varying upper Newton index is precisely part of the obstruction.

Even if such a universal polynomial existed, one nonzero evaluation would prove only
\(\mathscr K\not\equiv0\). It would still not prove that \(\mathscr K\) has no zero on every admissible triple.

# 2. The fixed-`p` Schwartz--Zippel bound is vacuous in the stated field

From the stated separate-degree bounds,

\[
\deg_T K_p\le p-1,
\qquad
\deg_U K_p\le p-1,
\]

the total degree satisfies

\[
D_p:=\deg K_p\le2(p-1).
\]

For a nonzero polynomial of total degree \(D_p\), Schwartz--Zippel over a sampling set \(S\) gives

\[
\Pr_{(t,u)\in S^2}[K_p(t,u)=0]
 \le \frac{D_p}{|S|}.
\]

With the natural choice \(S=\mathbf F_p\), this becomes

\[
\frac{D_p}{p}\le\frac{2p-2}{p}>1
\]

for every odd `p`, so after truncating a probability bound at `1` it says nothing. Even under the more optimistic hypothetical total-degree bound \(D_p=p-1\), it would give only

\[
\Pr[K_p(t,u)=0]\le1-\frac1p,
\]

which is almost vacuous for large `p`.

This is not merely a weakness of the standard estimate. At the allowed bidegree there is an exact interpolation obstruction. For any \((a,b)\in\mathbf F_p^2\), define

\[
\delta_{a,b}(T,U)
 =\bigl(1-(T-a)^{p-1}\bigr)
  \bigl(1-(U-b)^{p-1}\bigr).
\]

Fermat's theorem gives

\[
\delta_{a,b}(t,u)=
\begin{cases}
1,&(t,u)=(a,b),\\
0,&(t,u)\ne(a,b).
\end{cases}
\]

Thus a nonzero polynomial of bidegree exactly \((p-1,p-1)\) can vanish at \(p^2-1\) of the \(p^2\) field points. Equivalently, polynomials with separate degrees at most \(p-1\) realize all functions on \(\mathbf F_p^2\).

Consequently, no proper subset of \(\mathbf F_p^2\) is a hitting set for the full stated degree class. If an untested point \((a,b)\) remains, then

\[
\widetilde K_p(T,U)
 =K_p(T,U)-K_p(a,b)\delta_{a,b}(T,U)
\]

has the same permitted bidegree and agrees with \(K_p\) at every other field point, but satisfies

\[
\widetilde K_p(a,b)=0.
\]

If symmetry in `T,U` is imposed, use the symmetric correction
\(\delta_{a,b}+\delta_{b,a}\) for \(a\ne b\). Hence the degree information plus all recorded values cannot exclude an isolated omitted failure. This is an exact countermodel to the proposed inference, not just a statistical caution.

Across primes the obstruction is even stronger: one may leave every scanned \(K_p\) unchanged and make the above modification at one unscanned prime. All 2295 observations and all stated degree bounds remain true.

# 3. The sampled points violate the hypotheses of randomized PIT

Schwartz--Zippel assumes random points chosen independently from a prescribed finite set for one fixed polynomial. The scan points are instead selected by the arithmetic conditions

\[
A_p(\lambda_r)=A_p(\lambda_s)=0.
\]

They are therefore deterministic and highly conditioned points on a prime-dependent zero locus. There is no theorem here saying that this locus is uniformly distributed in \(\mathbf F_p^2\), that the pairs are independent, or that different primes provide independent trials for one common object.

In particular, it is invalid to multiply 2295 Schwartz--Zippel probabilities. The trials concern different polynomials over different fields, and the points were not drawn from the distribution to which the lemma applies. The simultaneous empirical success of the one-sided border and the Racah kernel also gives no probabilistic independence: both are constructed from the same Hasse/Newton data and can be strongly algebraically correlated.

There is a second direction error. Randomized PIT controls the chance that a nonzero polynomial happens to return `0` at a random test point. Here the observed values return nonzero. Once the polynomial is fixed, each such result is already a deterministic witness of nonidentity. PIT has no mechanism that turns many nonzero witnesses into a theorem that no zero exists elsewhere.

# 4. What bounds would be needed in a legitimate PIT application?

Suppose, contrary to the present setup, that one had one fixed polynomial

\[
f\in\mathbf F_q[X_1,\ldots,X_n]
\]

of known total degree \(D\), and sampled `m` independent uniform points from a set \(S^n\), \(|S|=N\). Then

\[
\Pr[\text{all `m` evaluations are zero}\mid f\ne0]
 \le\left(\frac DN\right)^m.
\]

For a nontrivial bound one needs

\[
N>D.
\]

For soundness error at most \(\varepsilon\), it is enough that

\[
\left(\frac DN\right)^m\le\varepsilon,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
N\ge D\,\varepsilon^{-1/m}.
\]

For one fixed \(K_p\) with \(D_p\le2p-2\), evaluating over an extension field \(\mathbf F_{p^k}\) would make the standard bound nontrivial once

\[
p^k>2p-2.
\]

Thus `k=2` suffices for every odd prime. This would be a legitimate randomized method for finding a witness that this one \(K_p\) is not the zero polynomial. Once a nonzero value is found, the witness itself is exact and no residual probability remains.

It still would not prove

\[
K_p(\lambda_r,\lambda_s)\ne0
\quad\text{for every zero pair},
\]

and it would not connect different primes. Therefore neither the extension-field bound nor the 2295-trial formula advances the all-gap theorem.

A deterministic black-box PIT formulation does not help either. A hitting set for all bidegree-\((p-1,p-1)\) polynomials over \(\mathbf F_p\) must contain the entire grid \(\mathbf F_p^2\): if it omits \((a,b)\), the polynomial \(\delta_{a,b}\) vanishes on the hitting set. A smaller hitting set would require a substantially narrower, rigorously specified circuit class for \(K_p\), not merely its degree.

# 5. Nonidentity is not zero-freeness on the zero-pair scheme

Let `M_p(T,U)` denote the product of the factors excluded by the physical problem: diagonal, mate, pole, and any other explicitly removed components. Let `I_p` be the ideal encoding the two Hasse-zero conditions and, if necessary, the actual spectral-node selectors. Schematically,

\[
I_p=\langle A_p(T),A_p(U),\text{node equations}\rangle,
\]

followed by saturation away from `M_p`.

The desired theorem says that `K_p` is a unit in the saturated coordinate algebra

\[
R_p=
\left(\mathbf F_p[T,U]/I_p\right)_{M_p}.
\]

By contrast, `K_p` being a nonzero polynomial says almost nothing about whether its image is a unit in `R_p`. Even `K_p` being nonzero in the quotient is weaker: a nonzero element can vanish at some maximal ideal of the zero-pair locus.

An exact algebraic certificate would have the form

\[
M_p(T,U)^N
 =B_p(T,U)K_p(T,U)
  +C_p(T,U)A_p(T)
  +D_p(T,U)A_p(U)
  +\text{node-selector terms}.
\]

On an admissible zero pair, the right side would reduce to
\(B_pK_p\), while the left side would be nonzero, proving \(K_p\ne0\). This is a saturated Nullstellensatz/Bezout certificate and remains meaningful even if the zero-pair algebra is nonreduced.

Equivalent structural targets are:

1. prove multiplication by `K_p` is invertible in the finite saturated quotient, or equivalently that its quotient-ring norm/determinant is nonzero;
2. prove a suitable saturated resultant is nonzero;
3. use the Newton Christoffel--Darboux formula and prove the terminal determinant, or terminal-ratio difference, is nonzero at every admissible pair;
4. derive a uniform product formula reducing \(K_p(\lambda_r,\lambda_s)\) to explicit `p`-units.

Any of these would be genuine additional input. The finite scan could then discharge the bounded exceptional primes left outside a uniform large-`p` argument.

# 6. Exact logical status of the 2295 evaluations

The scan has three legitimate uses:

```text
1. It is an exact finite-range verification, assuming the scanner and its
   exhaustiveness checks are correct.
2. It rules out small counterexamples in the tested range and is strong
   evidence for choosing the Racah/terminal-ratio route.
3. It can close finitely many exceptional primes after a uniform theorem
   handles all sufficiently large primes.
```

It cannot supply any of the following:

```text
- a degree bound for a polynomial in the prime p;
- a common field containing all 2295 evaluations as PIT trials;
- a random or independent sampling law on the Hasse-zero pairs;
- a proof that K_p is a unit on the constrained zero-pair locus;
- a proof excluding a first failure above 200000.
```

# Final answer

Under the current definitions, the answer is **no**.

The exact obstruction is the conjunction of:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{there is no single fixed polynomial }F(p),\\
\text{the fields and polynomial degrees vary with }p,\\
\text{the points are conditioned Hasse-zero pairs, not random points},\\
\text{and PIT proves nonidentity, not universal zero-freeness}. 
\end{gathered}}
\]

Moreover, the bidegree \((p-1,p-1)\) is precisely large enough to support a delta polynomial at one omitted field point, so degree alone has no predictive force even at a fixed prime.

A valid Schwartz--Zippel test would require one fixed polynomial of total degree `D`, one fixed sampling field/set of size `N>D`, and independent uniform trials, with error bounded by \((D/N)^{2295}\). For the stated fixed-`p` degree bound \(D_p\le2p-2\), the base field \(\mathbf F_p\) is too small; \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\) would suffice only for testing nonidentity of that one polynomial. None of this proves the all-gap statement.

The missing proof must therefore be algebraic: a uniform saturated Bezout/Nullstellensatz certificate, quotient-ring unit theorem, resultant nonvanishing theorem, or the exact Newton terminal-ratio inequality.
