# Q672: Supersingular fiber count in the Apéry K3 family

## Short answer

No. The zeros of the symmetric polynomial $Q_p(u)$ are **not exactly the supersingular fibers of the Apéry K3 family**. They are related, but they detect a much smaller and more arithmetic condition: the vanishing of a particular Frobenius trace coefficient (equivalently the mod-$p$ vanishing of the Apéry period coefficient).

The K3 interpretation is useful, but it does **not immediately imply AFP**. A naive supersingular-fiber count would give bounds of size $\sqrt p$ or $p^{1/2+o(1)}$, not the required $O(1)$.

The missing ingredient remains the stronger statement that the Apéry zero divisor has unusually large monodromy / small intersection with the Frobenius-zero locus.

---

# 1. What does $Q_p(u)=0$ actually mean?

The Apéry recurrence gives a rank-2 (or rank-3 before decomposition) $F$-crystal / Picard-Fuchs object. Modulo $p$, the coefficient

$$b_r \pmod p$$

is a trace function associated with Frobenius on a unit-root piece.

After the reflection symmetry

$$b_r=b_{p-1-r},$$

we write

$$u=r(p-1-r),$$

and obtain

$$b_r=Q_p(u).$$

Therefore

$$Q_p(u_0)=0$$

means:

> the Frobenius trace of the Apéry period at the parameter corresponding to $u_0$ vanishes.

This is a **trace-zero condition**, not automatically a supersingularity condition.

---

# 2. Why the elliptic analogy is misleading

For the Legendre elliptic family,

$$y^2=x(x-1)(x-t),$$

supersingularity means

$$a_p(t)=0.$$

The Hasse polynomial has degree about $p/2$, so there are approximately $p/2$ supersingular parameters.

This happens because elliptic moduli has dimension 1 and supersingularity is a codimension-one condition.

The same heuristic applied directly to the Apéry family would predict roughly $O(p)$ special fibers, not $O(1)$.

Thus "supersingular fiber = zero of period polynomial" cannot be the whole story.

---

# 3. Generic Picard rank of the Apéry K3

The Apéry K3 family is a highly algebraic one-parameter family.

The generic Picard number is believed/known to be

$$\rho_{gen}=19,$$

not 20.

Reason:

- A generic K3 surface has $\rho=0$.
- The Apéry family is a lattice-polarized K3 family with many fixed algebraic cycles.
- The transcendental lattice has rank

$$22-19=3.$$

The Apéry differential equation has order 3, matching this rank-3 transcendental motive.

The weight-4 modular form interpretation is therefore consistent with a rank-3 transcendental lattice.

A rank-2 transcendental lattice ($\rho=20$) would correspond to a singular K3 / CM K3 situation, which is exceptional, not generic.

---

# 4. Consequence: supersingularity is too strong

For a K3 surface over $F_p$:

$$\rho=22$$

means supersingular.

Starting from generic

$$\rho=19,$$

supersingularity requires three extra algebraic cycles.

In a one-parameter family this is not a simple hypersurface condition. It is a much rarer Noether-Lefschetz intersection.

However, "rare" does not automatically mean $O(1)$.

The natural expectation from arithmetic geometry is closer to

$$O(p^{1/2})$$

for a one-dimensional parameter space.

Therefore:

$$Q_p(u)=0 \not\Longleftrightarrow X_u \text{ supersingular}.$$

The zero set of $Q_p$ is generally larger than the supersingular set.

---

# 5. What is the correct geometric object?

The correct object is the **unit-root Frobenius eigenvalue**.

For ordinary fibers,

$$Frob_p|_{H^2_{tr}}$$

has eigenvalues

$$\alpha_p,\beta_p,\gamma_p.$$

The Apéry coefficient is controlled by the unit root:

$$b_n \sim c(t)\alpha(t)^n.$$

Modulo $p$,

$$b_n\equiv \alpha(t)^n h(t),$$

where $h(t)$ is the Hasse invariant / unit-root splitting factor.

The zero condition is therefore

$$h(t)=0,$$

not

$$\rho(X_t)=22.$$

The factor $h(t)$ is the true analogue of the Hasse polynomial.

For elliptic curves these two notions coincide because the transcendental part has rank 2. For the Apéry K3 they separate.

---

# 6. Explaining the observed data

The data:

| p | # roots of Q_p | modular coefficient |
|---|---:|---:|
|11|1|0|
|17|3|16|
|53|1|23|
|151|3|144|
|181|3|116|
|193|2|180|

shows no supersingular pattern.

If the roots were supersingular fibers, the count would correlate strongly with the supersingular K3 theory and CM phenomena. Instead we see tiny irregular counts (1--3), which is exactly what a large-monodromy trace-zero polynomial predicts.

The small root counts are evidence **against** a generic supersingular interpretation.

---

# 7. Relation to AFP

AFP needs:

$$\#\{r:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\}=O(1).$$

The K3 supersingularity route gives at best:

$$\#Z_p=O(p^{1/2}).$$

This is insufficient.

The stronger phenomenon must come from the one-dimensional unit-root representation:

1. The zero divisor of $Q_p$ has degree $(p-1)/2$.
2. Its Galois group appears experimentally close to $S_{(p-1)/2}$.
3. Random polynomial heuristics predict only $O(1)$ roots over $F_p$.

Thus the relevant theorem is not:

> Apéry K3 has few supersingular fibers.

It is:

> The Apéry Hasse/unit-root zero divisor has large geometric monodromy.

---

# 8. Higher congruences

The Beukers-Stienstra congruence

$$b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod{p^3}$$

is important, but it constrains the special endpoint fiber. It does not directly prohibit interior zeros.

Supercongruences give strong information about Frobenius eigenvalues at special points, but $b_r\equiv0\pmod p$ for an interior $r$ is a local Hasse invariant phenomenon.

A possible route would be:

- construct the full crystalline Frobenius matrix over $\mathbb Z_p$;
- use supercongruences to constrain its determinant/unit root;
- prove that the Hasse polynomial has no large repeated or structured factors.

This again returns to the monodromy problem.

---

# Final conclusion

The Apéry K3 interpretation is valuable but does **not solve AFP**.

The correct hierarchy is:

$$
\text{Apéry K3 family}
\rightarrow
\text{rank-3 transcendental motive}
\rightarrow
\text{unit-root Hasse polynomial}
\rightarrow
Q_p(u)
$$

and

$$
Q_p(u)=0
$$

means a Frobenius trace/Hasse invariant zero, not necessarily a supersingular K3 fiber.

The expected bound from K3 supersingularity alone is roughly $O(\sqrt p)$.

The observed $O(1)$ root counts require the stronger arithmetic statement:

**the Apéry Hasse divisor has large monodromy (approximately symmetric Galois group), causing random-polynomial root statistics.**

This is precisely the missing theorem identified in the AFP program.
