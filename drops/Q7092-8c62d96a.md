# Local intersection valuation audit for Apéry gap resultants

## 1. Candidate excess-valuation statement

Let
\[
S_{d,r}=\operatorname{Res}_x(N_d(x),N_r(x+d)).
\]
Write the exact universal content factor as
\[
S_{d,r}=C_{d,r}\,S^{\rm prim}_{d,r},
\]
where $C_{d,r}$ contains only factors forced by leading coefficients, factorial denominators, and known Apéry structural constants, with their exact $p$-adic multiplicities removed.

A natural desired lemma is:

> If $p\nmid C_{d,r}$ and the two polynomials have a common simple root modulo $p$, then
> \[
> v_p(S_{d,r})\ge 1.
> \]
> Equivalently the primitive resultant detects every genuine transverse intersection.

This statement is true by the definition of the resultant. The difficult strengthening would be an **excess theorem**:

> If the common root is one of the roots arising from a genuine quadruple collision, then
> \[
> v_p(S_{h_1,h_2})-v_p(C_{h_1,h_2})\ge 1
> \]
> uniformly after exact structural normalization.

That stronger assertion does not follow from local intersection theory alone.

## 2. Sylvester-matrix interpretation

The resultant is the determinant of the Sylvester matrix. A common root modulo $p$ means the Sylvester matrix loses rank modulo $p$.

For a simple common root $\alpha$:

- the kernel dimension is exactly one;
- the determinant acquires one factor of $p$;
- the valuation is generically exactly one.

Thus local intersection theory gives
\[
v_p(S)=1+\text{higher lifting contribution}
\]
for a transverse intersection, but it gives no reason that the valuation exceeds the structural valuation.

The witness itself contributes a rank drop of one, not an automatic extra rank drop beyond every known content factor.

## 3. Concrete obstruction: coincidence of structural and witness valuation

A simple algebraic model already shows the failure of the proposed excess principle.

Take
\[
F(x)=p x,
\qquad G(x)=p(x-1)+x.
\]
Both have structural factor $p$ in the first coefficient. After removing the universal content predicted from coefficients, the remaining resultant contribution can have exactly one factor of $p$ from the common-root condition and no extra factor.

More generally, if
\[
F=p^a F_0,
\qquad G=p^b G_0
\]
with $F_0,G_0$ primitive and sharing a simple root modulo $p$, then
\[
v_p(\operatorname{Res}(F,G))
\]
contains the additive content contribution plus the intersection multiplicity. There is no theorem forcing the intersection multiplicity to be larger than one.

Therefore a common-root witness can have exactly the same $p$-adic depth as the structural baseline.

## 4. Singular cases

There are three exceptional situations.

### (a) Leading coefficient vanishes modulo p

The degree drops. The ordinary resultant no longer measures the affine intersection with the expected multiplicity. One must first remove leading-coefficient primes or use a homogeneous resultant.

### (b) Multiple roots

If
\[
F(\alpha)=G(\alpha)=F'(\alpha)=G' (\alpha)=0,
\]
then the local intersection multiplicity can exceed one. This can create extra $p$-valuation.

However, this is an exceptional singular locus and cannot be assumed for every Apéry collision.

### (c) Structural carrier primes

If $p\mid U_H$, deleting the entire prime rather than its exact universal valuation removes both:

1. the known structural contribution;
2. potentially the only witness contribution.

Thus the current saturation procedure is too aggressive for an excess-valuation argument.

## 5. Correct replacement lemma

The useful statement would be:

> **Exact-content primitive witness lemma.** For $p$ outside the structural discriminant set, if $N_d$ and $N_r(\cdot+d)$ have a common simple root modulo $p$, then
> \[
> v_p(S_{d,r})=v_p(C_{d,r})+1.
> \]

This is plausible because the intersection is transverse. But it is not a stronger lower bound; it is an exact valuation statement.

To use it for GM-large one would need control of the number of such primes or the weighted sum of their exact valuations.

## 6. Consequence for the quadruple-corank argument

The proposed strategy cannot replace the exceptional-prime treatment merely by saying that every actual common root creates an additional factor after saturation.

The missing ingredient is a weighted exceptional-prime lemma of the form
\[
\sum_{p\mid U_H}\! Z(p)\log p \ll H^{3+o(1)}
\]
or an equivalent bound on the number of zero residues contributed by carrier primes.

Exact local intersection theory controls multiplicity at a fixed prime, but it does not control how many exceptional primes occur or how many zero residues each carries.

Hence the valuation route is viable only after replacing broad carrier deletion by exact content normalization and adding a separate weighted exceptional-prime estimate.
