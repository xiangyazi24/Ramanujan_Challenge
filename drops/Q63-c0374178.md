# Can the Apéry combinatorial formula bypass the defining-characteristic problem?

## Short answer

Probably not by itself, but it changes the target into a more classical arithmetic problem. The combinatorial formula gives strong structure, and hypergeometric methods are the most plausible route, but no known theorem currently gives the required bound

\[
\#\{p\in(m,2m]:p\mid b_m\}=o(m/\log m)
\]

(or even a sufficiently strong uniform bound) directly from the \(_4F_3\)-representation.

The obstruction is that the problem is not estimating a single finite-field hypergeometric value; it is counting the primes p for which a parameter-dependent hypergeometric value vanishes.

---

## 1. What the combinatorial formula gives

The Apéry number is

\[
b_m=\sum_{k=0}^m {m\choose k}^2{m+k\choose k}^2.
\]

For primes p>m every summand is a p-adic unit. Therefore a prime divisor p in (m,2m] is caused entirely by cancellation:

\[
\sum_k A_k\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

This is a very strong restriction. It removes the usual source of divisibility from individual summands.

However, positivity over the integers does not prevent cancellation after reduction modulo p.

---

## 2. Kummer carries are useful only below m

For p≤m, Kummer gives control:

\[
v_p({n\choose k})=\#\text{ carries in base }p.
\]

Hence valuations of individual terms can be analyzed digitwise.

But the difficult primes for the GCD problem are the large primes

\[
m<p\le2m.
\]

For these primes every term has valuation zero, so Kummer gives no information. The entire issue becomes the distribution of the residue

\[
 b_m\pmod p.
\]

---

## 3. Hypergeometric reformulation

The sum is a truncated hypergeometric value:

\[
b_m={}_4F_3\left(\begin{matrix}-m,-m,m+1,m+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};1\right).
\]

Over finite fields, Greene, McCarthy and others have developed identities expressing analogous sums in terms of finite-field hypergeometric functions.

These results can give:

- evaluations of individual values modulo p;
- relations with Frobenius traces;
- bounds when the finite-field parameters vary inside a fixed family.

But here the parameter m is varying simultaneously with the characteristic p, and we need a zero-counting theorem over the moving diagonal region

\[
p\sim m.
\]

That is precisely where existing finite-field hypergeometric theory does not immediately apply.

---

## 4. The saddle point idea

The asymptotic saddle point explains the size

\[
\log b_m\sim m\log(17+12\sqrt2).
\]

It controls the real size of the sum, but modular cancellation is a different phenomenon.

To use it one would need a statement of the form:

> the dominant range of k contains enough independent residue oscillation modulo p that the probability of complete cancellation is O(1/p).

No current saddle-point theorem provides this for Apéry sums.

---

## 5. What a successful theorem would look like

The desired result is analogous to a large sieve statement:

\[
\sum_{m\le M}\left|\sum_{p\in(X,2X]}1_{p\mid b_m}-\sum_p\frac1p\right|^2=o(M).
\]

Equivalently, one wants cancellation in the family of hypergeometric traces.

This would bypass the Mellin sheaf crystalline gap because it attacks the integer sequence directly.

---

## 6. Likely route

The most promising hybrid route is:

1. Express b_m modulo p as a finite-field hypergeometric trace.
2. Identify the corresponding l-adic sheaf for fixed p.
3. Average over the parameter m rather than over p.
4. Apply trace-function large sieve technology.

This is essentially replacing the original moving-characteristic problem by a moving-parameter problem.

The difficulty is that the characteristic and parameter ranges are coupled.

---

## Conclusion

The combinatorial prime-factorization approach is a genuine possible bypass, but the final missing ingredient is not Kummer theory. It is a new hypergeometric cancellation theorem:

\[
\#\{p\in(m,2m]:{}_4F_3(m;1)\equiv0\pmod p\}\ll 1.
\]

Finite-field hypergeometric theory gives the right language, but currently does not supply this zero-density estimate. A proof would likely be a major result connecting Apéry numbers, trace functions, and prime-divisor statistics.
