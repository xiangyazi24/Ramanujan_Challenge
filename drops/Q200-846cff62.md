# The “Picard–Fuchs conductor theorem”: precise verdict

## Executive verdict

**No theorem of the proposed form exists, and the proposed statement is false even for a fixed, rigid, geometric rank-2 Picard–Fuchs system with only three regular singularities.**

There are three separate issues:

1. **The Apéry numbers are coefficients, not values.** If
   \[
   B(x)=\sum_{n\ge 0} b_nx^n,
   \]
   then \(b_n=[x^n]B(x)\); one does not have \(b_n=B(n)\). The Picard–Fuchs variable \(x\) and the coefficient/character variable \(n\) are different variables.

2. **Artin–Schreier pullback only applies to an algebraic function.** For a morphism \(f:U\to\mathbf A^1\), the sheaf \(f^*\mathcal L_\psi\) has trace \(\psi(f(x))\). A Frobenius trace function
   \[
   x\longmapsto t_{\mathcal F}(x)=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_x\mid\mathcal F_{\bar x})
   \]
   is not itself a morphism \(U\to\mathbf A^1\). In general, the expression
   \[
   (t_{\mathcal F})^*\mathcal L_\psi
   \]
   is simply **not defined**.

3. Even a pointwise estimate \(|S_c|\ll\sqrt p\) would imply only \(|Z_p|\ll\sqrt p\) by Fourier inversion, not \(|Z_p|\le 2\). A genuinely bounded-conductor *family in the variable \(c\)* could potentially give \(|Z_p|=O(1)\), but constructing that family is exactly the missing theorem, and it does not follow from Picard–Fuchs regularity or rigidity.

Thus the answer to the central question is:

\[
\boxed{\text{regular-singular Picard–Fuchs data do not control }\sum_n\psi(c b_n).}
\]

The obstruction is not a technical gap in Katz's theory. It is a mismatch of variables and of sheaf-theoretic operations.

# 1. First correction: the Apéry Picard–Fuchs equation

For the Apéry numbers

\[
b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2,
\]

the recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}-(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)b_n+n^3b_{n-1}=0.
\]

Consequently the generating function

\[
B(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nx^n
\]

satisfies the **third-order** operator

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L_A B=0,
\qquad
\mathcal L_A=
\theta^3-x(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+x^2(\theta+1)^3,
}
\]

where \(\theta=x\,d/dx\).

The operator displayed in the question,

\[
\theta^4-x(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5),
\]

is not the minimal Apéry generating-function operator: it has the wrong order and is missing the \(x^2(\theta+1)^3\) term.

The leading coefficient of the third-order equation is proportional to

\[
x^3(1-34x+x^2),
\]

so the singular set over \(\overline{\mathbf Q}\) is

\[
0,\quad 17-12\sqrt2,\quad 17+12\sqrt2,\quad \infty.
\]

Since \((17+12\sqrt2)(17-12\sqrt2)=1\), writing the two finite nonzero points as the inverses merely swaps them.

The K3 Picard–Fuchs local system has rank 3. There is also an underlying rank-2 elliptic/Kummer description whose symmetric square produces the rank-3 system, but this does not turn \(b_n\) into a value of a rank-2 period at \(x=n\).

The essential identity is

\[
\boxed{b_n=[x^n]B(x),\qquad b_n\ne B(n).}
\]

Indeed, the complex period \(B(x)\) has a finite radius of convergence, while \(b_n\) is an integer defined for every \(n\). Any entire interpolation of the integer sequence \(n\mapsto b_n\) is a different analytic object and is not the Picard–Fuchs period \(B(x)\).

# 2. What Deligne–Katz conductor bounds actually say

Let \(U/\mathbf F_p\) be a fixed smooth curve, and let \(\mathcal F\) be a geometrically nontrivial middle-extension \(\ell\)-adic sheaf on \(U\), pointwise pure of weight 0. If its rank, singular set, and Swan conductors are bounded, then Grothendieck–Lefschetz, Grothendieck–Ogg–Shafarevich, and Deligne's Riemann Hypothesis give a bound of the form

\[
\left|\sum_{x\in U(\mathbf F_p)}t_{\mathcal F}(x)\right|
\ll_{\operatorname{cond}(\mathcal F)}\sqrt p,
\]

provided the geometrically trivial part has been removed.

Similarly, if \(f:U\to\mathbf A^1\) is a rational or regular function of bounded degree, then

\[
\sum_{x\in U(\mathbf F_p)}t_{\mathcal F}(x)\psi(cf(x))
\]

is the trace sum of

\[
\mathcal F\otimes f^*\mathcal L_{\psi_c},
\]

and its conductor is controlled by the conductor of \(\mathcal F\) together with the pole orders of \(f\).

The Fourier–Deligne transform treats expressions such as

\[
\widehat t_{\mathcal F}(c)
=
\sum_x t_{\mathcal F}(x)\psi(cx).
\]

This is a **linear transform of the trace function**.

The desired Apéry expression is instead

\[
S_c=
\sum_x\psi\!\left(c\,t_{\mathcal F}(x)\bmod p\right).
\]

This applies a nonlinear, \(p\)-dependent function to the value of a trace function. It is not the Fourier transform of \(\mathcal F\), not an Artin–Schreier twist of \(\mathcal F\), and not a standard tensor, symmetric-power, convolution, or middle-convolution operation.

The distinction is:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_x t_{\mathcal F}(x)\psi(cx)
\quad\text{is sheaf-theoretic,}
}
\]

whereas

\[
\boxed{
\sum_x\psi(c\,t_{\mathcal F}(x))
\quad\text{has no general bounded-conductor construction.}
}
\]

There is an additional coefficient-field problem. An \(\ell\)-adic trace belongs to a number field embedded in \(\overline{\mathbf Q}_\ell\), while \(\psi\) is a character of \(\mathbf F_p\). There is no canonical map

\[
\overline{\mathbf Q}_\ell\longrightarrow\mathbf F_p.
\]

For Apéry numbers one can reduce the integer \(b_n\) modulo \(p\), or use a crystalline/unit-root realization, but that reduction is extra arithmetic structure. It is not supplied by the bounded conductor of an \(\ell\)-adic companion. This is precisely the crystalline/mod-\(p\) gap already identified in the project.

## Correction to the formulation in Q184

The notation

\[
(c\cdot b)^*\mathcal L_\psi
\]

used in the earlier Q184 response is not legitimate when \(b(j)\) means a Frobenius trace table. Such a pullback exists only after one exhibits an actual algebraic morphism

\[
b:U\to\mathbf A^1.
\]

A trace function on \(U(\mathbf F_p)\) is not automatically such a morphism.

# 3. The exact sheaf one can always construct—and why its rank grows with \(p\)

For each fixed prime \(p\), every function

\[
f_p:\mathbf F_p\to\mathbf F_p,
\qquad
f_p(n)=b_n\pmod p,
\]

has a unique interpolation polynomial

\[
P_p(T)\in\mathbf F_p[T],
\qquad
\deg P_p\le p-1,
\qquad
P_p(n)=b_n\quad(n\in\mathbf F_p).
\]

Let

\[
D_p=\deg P_p.
\]

Then one may define, over the \(c\)-line, the cohomological transform

\[
\mathcal K_p
=
R^1\pi_!
\mathcal L_{\psi(cP_p(T))},
\]

where

\[
\pi:\mathbf A^1_T\times\mathbf G_{m,c}\to\mathbf G_{m,c}
\]

is projection. For \(c\ne0\), Grothendieck–Lefschetz gives, up to the conventional sign,

\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_c\mid(\mathcal K_p)_{\bar c})
=
-\sum_{T\in\mathbf F_p}\psi(cP_p(T))
=-S_c.
\]

Because \(D_p<p\), the Artin–Schreier sheaf

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(cP_p(T))}
\]

has Swan conductor \(D_p\) at \(T=\infty\). For nonconstant \(P_p\), Grothendieck–Ogg–Shafarevich gives

\[
\dim H_c^1
\left(\mathbf A^1_{\overline{\mathbf F}_p},
\mathcal L_{\psi(cP_p(T))}
\right)
=D_p-1.
\]

Thus the generic rank is

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal K_p)=D_p-1.}
\]

This is the precise cohomology group asked for in Q184/Q200. It exists, but its rank is controlled by the degree of the **index interpolation polynomial** \(P_p\), not by the order or number of singularities of the Picard–Fuchs equation in the generating-function variable \(x\).

There is also a completely tautological realization

\[
\mathcal T_p
=
\bigoplus_{n=0}^{p-1}\mathcal L_{\psi(b_n c)}
\]

on the \(c\)-line. Its trace is exactly \(S_c\), but

\[
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal T_p)=p.
\]

Moreover, every index with \(b_n=0\) contributes a trivial summand:

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(0\cdot c)}\simeq\overline{\mathbf Q}_\ell.
\]

Hence

\[
|Z_p|
=
\text{multiplicity of the trivial sheaf inside }\mathcal T_p.
\]

Trying to prove bounded rank after this construction is therefore essentially the original zero problem in sheaf language; it is not a consequence of the rank of the original Mellin or Picard–Fuchs sheaf.

# 4. A fixed rigid rank-2 Picard–Fuchs counterexample

The proposed general theorem fails in the strongest relevant setting.

Consider

\[
F(x)
={}_2F_1\!\left(\frac12,\frac12;1;16x\right)
=
\sum_{n\ge0}\binom{2n}{n}^2x^n.
\]

It satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\left[\theta^2-4x(2\theta+1)^2\right]F=0.
}
\]

This is a second-order Fuchsian Picard–Fuchs equation with regular singularities

\[
0,\quad \frac1{16},\quad \infty.
\]

It is the Gauss–Manin/Picard–Fuchs equation of the Legendre elliptic family after the change of parameter \(\lambda=16x\). Its rank-2 hypergeometric local system is irreducible and rigid.

Set

\[
a_n=\binom{2n}{n}^2.
\]

For every odd prime \(p\) and \(0\le n<p\), Kummer's carry criterion gives

\[
p\mid\binom{2n}{n}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
n\ge\frac{p+1}{2}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\#\{0\le n<p:a_n\equiv0\pmod p\}
=
\frac{p-1}{2}.
\]

Now define

\[
S_{p,c}
=
\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\psi(ca_n).
\]

Summing over \(c\in\mathbf F_p^\times\), orthogonality gives

\[
\sum_{c\ne0}S_{p,c}
=
(p-1)|Z_p|-(p-|Z_p|)
=
p|Z_p|-p.
\]

With \(|Z_p|=(p-1)/2\), this becomes

\[
\sum_{c\ne0}S_{p,c}
=
\frac{p(p-3)}2.
\]

Consequently, for some \(c\ne0\),

\[
\operatorname{Re}S_{p,c}
\ge
\frac{p(p-3)}{2(p-1)},
\]

and hence

\[
\boxed{|S_{p,c}|\gg p.}
\]

This is not a family of ODEs varying with \(p\). It is one fixed, geometric, rigid rank-2 Picard–Fuchs equation with three regular singularities.

It disproves every assertion of the form

\[
\left|\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\psi(ca_n)\right|
\le C(d,S)\sqrt p
\]

for Taylor coefficients of Fuchsian solutions.

It also explains the conductor growth. The interpolation polynomial for \(n\mapsto a_n\bmod p\) has at least \((p-1)/2\) distinct roots and is not identically zero, so

\[
D_p\ge\frac{p-1}{2}.
\]

Thus the associated exponential-sum sheaf on the \(c\)-line has rank at least

\[
D_p-1\ge\frac{p-3}{2}.
\]

A fixed regular-singular Picard–Fuchs system can therefore produce coefficient-index Artin–Schreier conductor growing linearly with \(p\).

This counterexample also shows why rigidity cannot rescue the proposed theorem.

# 5. Why rigidity does not imply the desired bound

Rigidity is a statement about a **linear local system in its geometric variable**. Roughly, an irreducible rigid local system is determined by its local monodromy conjugacy classes. Katz's middle-convolution algorithm can construct and classify such systems, and it can control the rank and local monodromy of the original sheaf and of sheaves obtained by legitimate linear/tensor/Fourier operations.

Rigidity does **not** assert that every nonlinear function of Frobenius traces is a bounded-conductor trace function.

In particular, the map

\[
g\longmapsto \psi(\operatorname{tr}g\bmod p)
\]

is not the character of a fixed algebraic representation of \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) over characteristic zero. By contrast:

- \(\operatorname{tr}(g)\) is the character of the standard representation;
- \(\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Sym}^k g)\) is the character of a fixed algebraic representation for fixed \(k\);
- tensor, exterior, and symmetric powers remain in the Tannakian category generated by the original sheaf.

The additive character

\[
\psi_p(\operatorname{tr}g\bmod p)
\]

varies with \(p\) and is not a fixed polynomial character. Expanding it in irreducible characters of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) generally requires complexity growing with \(p\). This is why symmetric-power results do not imply a bounded-rank realization of \(\psi\circ\operatorname{tr}\).

Also, four singularities by themselves do not imply rigidity: rank-2 four-point systems generically have an accessory parameter. Whether a particular Apéry realization is rigid or becomes rigid after a pullback/twist is not decisive here. The Legendre example shows that even an indisputably rigid hypergeometric Picard–Fuchs system can have maximally nonuniform coefficient values modulo \(p\).

# 6. What the cited references do—and do not—provide

## 6.1 Katz, *Exponential Sums and Differential Equations*

The relevant parts are indeed Chapters 7 and 8, but they do not contain the proposed theorem.

Chapter 7, “The \(\ell\)-adic theory,” includes:

- §7.3: construction of irreducible sheaves via Fourier transform;
- §7.4–7.5: local monodromy of Fourier transforms and numerical consequences;
- §7.8–7.10: Fourier-transform-stable classes and examples;
- §7.11–7.14: Sato–Tate laws and classical monodromy examples for one-variable exponential-sum families.

Chapter 8 constructs \(\ell\)-adic hypergeometric complexes and hypergeometric sums from explicit multiplicative and additive character data; §8.2 is the basic definition/construction.

These chapters apply to sums obtained from explicit algebraic kernels and to Fourier/convolution operations on sheaves. They do not define a functor

\[
\mathcal F\longmapsto
\text{“the sheaf with trace }\psi(t_{\mathcal F})\text{.”}
\]

No such bounded-conductor functor exists in general, as the Legendre counterexample demonstrates.

## 6.2 Katz–Laumon Fourier transform

Katz and Laumon's Fourier-transform theorem controls cohomological transforms with kernels of the form

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(xy)}
\]

or, more generally, bounded-complexity algebraic phases. It explains and bounds

\[
\sum_x t_{\mathcal F}(x)\psi(cx).
\]

It does not apply to

\[
\sum_x\psi(c\,t_{\mathcal F}(x)).
\]

## 6.3 Katz, *Rigid Local Systems*

This book develops rigidity, middle convolution, Fourier transform and reconstruction from local monodromy. It controls local systems under those categorical operations. It does not assert bounded conductor after taking an additive character of the numerical Frobenius trace.

## 6.4 Adolphson–Sperber

Their Newton-polyhedron theory bounds exponential sums

\[
\sum_{x\in(\mathbf F_p^\times)^m}\psi(f(x))
\]

for an explicit Laurent polynomial \(f\), with cohomology controlled by the Newton polyhedron and nondegeneracy hypotheses. The phase must be an algebraic function of bounded Newton complexity. A coefficient table \(n\mapsto b_n\) supplied by a Picard–Fuchs equation is not automatically such a phase.

## 6.5 Fu–Wan symmetric products

Fu and Wan study \(L\)-functions of \(\operatorname{Sym}^k\mathrm{Kl}\) and compute local monodromy, degrees, and bad factors. Symmetric powers are fixed algebraic representations of the original monodromy group. They do not model the \(p\)-dependent nonlinear function

\[
\psi_p(\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Frob}).
\]

Thus none of the four cited routes supplies the claimed “Picard–Fuchs conductor theorem.”

# 7. Square-root cancellation would not by itself give \(|Z_p|\le2\)

Let

\[
Z_p=\{n\in\mathbf F_p:b_n=0\},
\qquad
S_c=\sum_{n\in\mathbf F_p}\psi(cb_n).
\]

Fourier inversion gives

\[
|Z_p|
=
\frac1p\sum_{c\in\mathbf F_p}S_c
=
1+\frac1p\sum_{c\ne0}S_c,
\]

because \(S_0=p\).

If one knows only

\[
|S_c|\le C\sqrt p
\qquad(c\ne0),
\]

then the triangle inequality yields

\[
|Z_p|
\le
1+C\frac{p-1}{\sqrt p}
=
O(\sqrt p).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{|S_c|=O(\sqrt p)\text{ pointwise does not imply }|Z_p|=O(1).}
\]

It would improve the present \(p^{2/3}\)-type zero bound to a square-root bound, but it would not prove \(|Z_p|\le2\).

A stronger statement could work: if \(c\mapsto S_c\) were the trace function of a uniformly bounded-conductor sheaf of the expected weight, then one could apply Deligne a second time to

\[
\sum_{c\ne0}S_c
\]

and obtain \(O(p)\), hence \(|Z_p|=O(1)\). But that requires a genuine bounded-conductor sheaf on the \(c\)-line. The interpolation construction above has rank \(D_p-1\), and the tautological construction has rank \(p\). Showing bounded rank/conductor is essentially the missing coefficient-index theorem, not a formal consequence of the Picard–Fuchs equation.

Even a generic \(O(1)\) conclusion would not automatically give the exact numerical bound 2; that would require additional local-monodromy or degree information.

# 8. What theorem would actually be needed for Apéry

A viable theorem must live in the **coefficient/character variable**, not merely in the Picard–Fuchs parameter.

Any one of the following would be genuinely sufficient new input.

## A. A bounded-degree algebraic index model

Construct, uniformly in \(p\), an algebraic map or correspondence of bounded degree whose value at \(n\in\mathbf F_p\) is \(b_n\bmod p\). Then Artin–Schreier pullback and Fourier transform would have bounded conductor.

No such model is currently known. The Picard–Fuchs equation in \(x\) does not provide it.

## B. A bounded-conductor exponential transform of the Apéry Mellin object

Construct sheaves \(\mathcal K_p\) on the \(c\)-line such that

\[
t_{\mathcal K_p}(c)=S_c
\]

on all finite extensions, with rank and Swan conductors bounded independently of \(p\). This would be a major new theorem. It is not an instance of standard Artin–Schreier pullback, and the Legendre counterexample shows that such a theorem cannot hold for arbitrary rigid Picard–Fuchs systems.

## C. A uniform crystalline/index-space stationary-phase theorem

Construct a \(p\)-adic analytic interpolation of \(n\mapsto b_n\) on the relevant residue domain and prove uniform bounds for its overconvergence, ramification, Newton polygon, or number and multiplicity of stationary points. This would address the correct mod-\(p\)/crystalline object.

Again, regular singularity of the generating-function connection does not automatically imply these index-space bounds.

The empirical observation that the finite difference \(b_{n+1}-b_n\) has very few zeros may be evidence for such a special Apéry theorem, but it is extra arithmetic structure, not a general consequence of \((d,S)\).

# 9. Final answer to the precise question

The proposed theorem

> For every solution of a Fuchsian ODE of order \(d\) with \(S\) regular singularities, the coefficient/evaluation function modulo \(p\) satisfies
> \[
> \left|\sum_j\psi(cf_p(j))\right|\le C(d,S)\sqrt p
> \]

is not a known theorem and is in fact false.

For the Apéry problem, the argument fails because:

1. \(b_n\) is a coefficient of the Picard–Fuchs solution, not its evaluation at \(x=n\);
2. the complex/\(\ell\)-adic geometric variable is not the coefficient/character variable;
3. \(\psi\circ t_{\mathcal F}\) is not an Artin–Schreier pullback of \(\mathcal F\);
4. rigidity controls the original linear local system, not this nonlinear \(p\)-dependent operation;
5. the canonical sheaf realizing \(S_c\) has rank governed by a \(p\)-dependent interpolation degree and can have rank \(\asymp p\);
6. a fixed rigid Legendre Picard–Fuchs system gives \(|S_{p,c}|\gg p\) for some \(c\) and every large odd prime;
7. even pointwise square-root cancellation would yield only \(|Z_p|=O(\sqrt p)\), not \(|Z_p|\le2\).

Therefore this route does **not** solve the one-prime Apéry zero problem. The correct missing theorem is a bounded-complexity statement for the Apéry sequence in the coefficient/character variable—most plausibly crystalline, difference-equation, or explicit algebraic in nature—not a general conductor theorem for regular-singular Picard–Fuchs equations.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Picard–Fuchs rigidity is valuable background, but it does not exponentiate Frobenius traces.}
}
\]
