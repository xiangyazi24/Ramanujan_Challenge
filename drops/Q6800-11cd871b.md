# Audit of the bounded-conductor l-adic proposal

## 1. What the proposed realization would require

The proposed object is a sheaf on A^2 whose trace at (r,d) is

    e_p(Delta(r,d)),

uniformly for d up to about p^(1/2+o(1)). The suggested mechanism is a bounded-conductor sheaf, for example a rank-one Artin-Schreier sheaf pulled back by a rational function of bounded complexity.

The known characteristic-zero expression

    Delta(r,d) = N_d(r) / product_{j=1}^d (r+j)^3,

with deg N_d = 3(d-1), already shows that the specializations in the r-direction have increasing algebraic complexity as d grows. This is a serious obstruction to the naive fixed rational-function picture.

## 2. Does specialization degree/pole growth rule out a rank-one Artin-Schreier pullback?

For the literal ansatz

    L_psi(f(r,d))

where f is a fixed rational function of bounded degree over the base, the answer is essentially yes.

Restricting to a geometric fiber d=d0 gives an Artin-Schreier sheaf attached to the rational function f(r,d0). Its conductor is controlled by the pole divisor (including pole orders after reduction modulo p). A bounded-degree rational f has uniformly bounded pole divisor and bounded Swan contribution. Therefore every specialization would have uniformly bounded conductor.

The Apéry formula, if used as an actual characteristic-zero rational function identity before reduction, has d-dependent denominator with d apparent poles and numerator degree growing linearly in d. Unless massive cancellations occur after specialization, the induced one-variable functions cannot all come from a bounded-degree fixed f.

Thus:

* It rules out the naive ``one fixed bounded-degree rational phase'' model.
* It does not rule out every bounded-conductor sheaf construction.

## 3. Why this does not kill all bounded-conductor sheaves

A bounded-conductor sheaf on A^2 need not be a rank-one Artin-Schreier pullback by a single rational function.

Possible more complicated mechanisms include:

* higher-rank l-adic sheaves whose trace functions have complicated pointwise descriptions;
* geometric constructions where the (r,d) variables enter through a correspondence, pushforward, or Fourier transform;
* sheaves whose generic geometric complexity is bounded even though a chosen formula for one matrix coefficient has many apparent poles.

For such objects, the relevant invariant is the conductor of the sheaf itself, not the degree of one chosen expression for its trace.

Therefore unbounded degrees/poles in the displayed formula are evidence against one model, not a proof that bounded conductor is impossible.

## 4. Restriction to d=d0 and conductor behavior

If a bounded-conductor sheaf F exists on A^2, restricting to the fiber

    i_{d0}: A^1 -> A^2,  r |-> (r,d0)

is not automatically conductor-neutral. The conductor of the restricted sheaf is controlled by the interaction of the singular locus of F with the fiber.

For a lisse sheaf on a suitable open set, generic fibers usually have bounded conductor inherited from F. However, special fibers can meet singular divisors non-transversely and acquire extra local monodromy.

Thus a correct contradiction would require proving that the restrictions to many d=d0 fibers have conductor growing with d0, while a hypothetical ambient bounded-conductor sheaf would force uniformly bounded fiber conductors. The displayed rational expression alone is not that proof, because it may not be the intrinsic sheaf-theoretic conductor.

## 5. Equality only on F_p-points

Another caveat is important: equality of trace functions on F_p-points is much weaker than equality of sheaves.

Two different sheaves can have the same trace values on one finite field. A finite-field trace function can admit exotic realizations, especially after allowing constructions depending on p. Pointwise interpolation over F_p can hide arbitrarily complicated geometry.

To deduce a geometric contradiction one needs a uniform statement over varying p, with a fixed compatible sheaf or compatible family. A single equality

    trace(F)(r,d) = e_p(Delta(r,d))

for one p does not force the rational expression defining Delta to be the underlying sheaf map.

Therefore the correct audit conclusion is:

* fixed rank-one AS pullback: strongly implausible and obstructed;
* arbitrary bounded-conductor compatible sheaf: not ruled out by specialization degree alone;
* finite-field pointwise coincidences: insufficient evidence either way.

## 6. Fourier completion audit

Assume independently that a complete two-variable Fourier transform satisfies

    |F(t,xi)| <= C p

for all frequencies. Let the original sum be over a d-interval I of length D. Completion in d writes the interval indicator through Fourier coefficients.

For an interval of length D in F_p,

    ||1_I^hat||_1 << p log p.

Hence using only the complete Fourier bound gives

    |sum_{r,d in strip} e_p(Delta(r,d))|
       << (Cp) * (p log p)
       = C p^2 log p

if completion is performed only against the d-variable and the complete kernel is normalized in the usual way.

To obtain an O(p log p)-type estimate one would need a stronger normalization: for example a complete two-variable sum already bounded by O(1) after the Fourier normalization, or an additional saving in the other variable.

A bare bound

    |F(t,xi)| <= C p

is therefore not enough by itself to imply p log p. The L1 cost must be tracked with the exact Fourier convention.

## 7. Does r-boundary completion also matter?

Yes, if the original sum has a non-complete r-range.

The strip here is nonwrapping:

    1 <= r <= p-2-d.

This has an r-dependent boundary. Completing only in d leaves the r-cutoff untreated. A full rectangular completion argument introduces Fourier coefficients for the r-indicator as well.

The product L1 bound is then typically

    ||1_R^hat||_1 ||1_I^hat||_1
       << (p log p)^2

for arbitrary intervals, so a complete-kernel estimate must be correspondingly stronger to recover a useful bound.

One may avoid the second logarithm only if there is a special treatment of the triangular/nonrectangular domain, or if one variable is summed by another argument before completion.

## Final verdict

The bounded-conductor route is not disproved, but the strongest naive version is not credible.

VERDICT:

1. A fixed rank-one Artin-Schreier pullback by a bounded-degree rational function is ruled out heuristically and, with a genuine uniform conductor comparison, can likely be ruled out rigorously.

2. Unbounded specialization degrees and apparent poles do not rule out all bounded-conductor l-adic realizations, because trace functions may arise from higher-rank, transformed, or correspondence-based sheaves.

3. Fiber restrictions require an intrinsic conductor argument, not merely reading poles from one formula.

4. Equality on F_p-points alone is too weak; one needs a compatible geometric family over varying primes.

5. A complete Fourier kernel bound |F| <= Cp gives an L1 completion cost of the Fourier coefficients. For a d-interval alone this is a p log p factor, leading to Cp^2 log p under the unnormalized convention. A p log p final estimate needs extra normalization/saving.

6. Nonwrapping r-boundaries cannot generally be ignored; full two-variable completion pays for r and d boundary Fourier norms unless the geometry of the strip is exploited separately.
