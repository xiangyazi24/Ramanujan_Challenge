# Function-field Apéry GCD: the literal model is trivial, a natural recurrence pencil is exactly coprime, and Mason–Stothers does not model the integer obstruction

## Executive conclusion

There are three different constructions that can be called a “function-field Apéry sequence,” and they must not be conflated.

1. **Literal base change of the Apéry recurrence to** \(\mathbf F_q(T)\).  This produces only constants: the two solutions still lie in \(\mathbf F_q\), not in \(\mathbf F_q[T]\).  Before the first singular index, their polynomial gcd is therefore a unit.  At the index \(n\equiv-1\pmod{\operatorname{char}\mathbf F_q}\), the recurrence ceases to determine the next term, so there is no fixed-\(q\), \(n\to\infty\) sequence defined by the recurrence alone.

2. **A non-isotrivial recurrence pencil**, obtained by replacing the Apéry parameter \(17\) by an indeterminate \(T\).  This is the closest precise interpretation of “the same recurrence with polynomial coefficients.”  In this model one can prove a stronger theorem than the desired \(o(n)\) bound:

   \[
   \gcd_{k[T]}(A_n(T),B_n(T))=1
   \]

   for every admissible \(n\).  The proof is one line from the Casoratian; Mason–Stothers is not needed.

3. **The diagonal/hypergeometric Apéry polynomial family**.  There is a canonical polynomial deformation

   \[
   \mathcal B_n(T)=
   [x_1^n x_2^n x_3^n x_4^n]
   \frac{1}{(1-x_1-x_2)(1-x_3-x_4)-T x_1x_2x_3x_4},
   \]

   and it specializes to \(b_n\) at \(T=1\).  However, it does **not** satisfy the original second-order Apéry recurrence, and it has no canonical companion polynomial playing the role of \(a_n\).  In characteristic \(p\) it also has the exact Frobenius degeneracy

   \[
   \mathcal B_{pn}(T)=\mathcal B_n(T)^p.
   \]

   In particular,

   \[
   \mathcal B_{p^r}(T)=(T+4)^{p^r},
   \qquad
   \deg\operatorname{rad}\mathcal B_{p^r}=1,
   \]

   although \(\deg\mathcal B_{p^r}=p^r\).  This is precisely the all-derivatives-zero case excluded from Mason–Stothers.

Therefore:

> **A function-field theorem can be proved for the natural recurrence pencil, but it is exact coprimality for a reason that erases the hard part of the integer problem.  Mason–Stothers does not prove a faithful analogue of the Apéry GCD conjecture.**

The obstruction to transfer is not merely that the integer \(abc\) theorem is unknown.  The function-field parameter sees **horizontal irreducible divisors in \(T\)**, whereas the original conjecture concerns **vertical rational primes in the coefficient ring**.  The Casoratian is a nonzero constant, hence a unit in \(k[T]\), but it is not a unit in \(\mathbf Z\) after denominator clearing.  The original cross-prime problem lives entirely in this vertical arithmetic.

## 1. The original recurrence and its exact Casoratian

Write

\[
P_n=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

The Apéry recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3Y_{n+1}=P_nY_n-n^3Y_{n-1},
\qquad n\ge1.
\tag{1.1}
\]

The two solutions relevant to the GCD problem have initial data

\[
B_0=1,\quad B_1=5,
\qquad
A_0=0,\quad A_1=6.
\tag{1.2}
\]

At \(T=17\), these are the usual \(b_n\) and companion \(a_n\).

Define the Casoratian

\[
W_n=A_nB_{n+1}-A_{n+1}B_n.
\]

Substituting the recurrence for \(A_{n+1}\) and \(B_{n+1}\) gives

\[
W_n=rac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}W_{n-1}.
\]

Since

\[
W_0=A_0B_1-A_1B_0=-6,
\]

we obtain the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
A_nB_{n+1}-A_{n+1}B_n=-\frac6{(n+1)^3}.}
\tag{1.3}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
A_{n-1}B_n-A_nB_{n-1}=-\frac6{n^3}.}
\tag{1.4}
\]

This identity is the decisive fact in every function-field formulation.

## 2. Literal base change to \(\mathbf F_q(T)\)

Let \(q=p^f\), and map every integer coefficient in (1.1) to the prime field \(\mathbf F_p\subset\mathbf F_q\).  Keep the initial conditions (1.2).

### Proposition 2.1 — literal base change is isotrivial

Assume \(p>n\) and \(p\ne2,3\).  Then the terms

\[
A_j,B_j\qquad(0\le j\le n)
\]

obtained from (1.1) lie in \(\mathbf F_q\).  Viewed as elements of \(\mathbf F_q[T]\), they have

\[
\gcd_{\mathbf F_q[T]}(A_n,B_n)=1.
\]

#### Proof

The recurrence coefficients and initial data are constants.  Since \(p>n\), every denominator \((j+1)^3\) used up to index \(n\) is invertible in \(\mathbf F_q\), so induction gives \(A_j,B_j\in\mathbf F_q\).

Moreover, (1.4) remains valid and its right side is a nonzero element of \(\mathbf F_q\).  Thus \(A_n\) and \(B_n\) cannot both vanish.  Any nonzero constant is a unit in \(\mathbf F_q[T]\), so their polynomial gcd is a unit. ∎

Thus no nonconstant irreducible polynomial \(P(T)\) can divide the literal base-changed \(b_n\), unless \(b_n=0\); even in that case it cannot divide both solutions because of (1.4).

### The fixed-characteristic obstruction

This does not give an asymptotic theorem for fixed \(q\).  At \(j=p-1\), the leading coefficient of the recurrence vanishes:

\[
(j+1)^3=p^3=0\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_q.
\]

The recurrence becomes a constraint involving \(Y_{p-1}\) and \(Y_{p-2}\), but it no longer determines \(Y_p\).  Hence:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The recurrence alone does not define a canonical sequence for all }n
\text{ over a fixed }\mathbf F_q.}
\]

The binomial formula still defines \(b_n\bmod p\) for all \(n\), but that is extra structure; the rational companion \(a_n\) and the denominator-clearing operation do not extend canonically through the singular indices.

So the most literal proposed analogue is simultaneously:

- trivial before the first singular index; and
- undefined as an \(n\to\infty\) recurrence problem over fixed \(\mathbf F_q(T)\).

## 3. A precise non-isotrivial recurrence pencil

A natural way to introduce a genuine function-field parameter while preserving the second-order recurrence is to replace the Apéry parameter \(17\) by \(T\):

\[
(n+1)^3Y_{n+1}
=(2n+1)\bigl(Tn(n+1)+5\bigr)Y_n-n^3Y_{n-1}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

At \(T=17\), this is exactly (1.1).

Let \(k\) be a field.  Define two solutions in \(k[T]\) by

\[
B_0(T)=1,\quad B_1(T)=5,
\qquad
A_0(T)=0,\quad A_1(T)=6,
\tag{3.2}
\]

and use (3.1) for \(n\ge1\).

When \(\operatorname{char}k=0\), this defines both sequences for every \(n\).  When \(\operatorname{char}k=p\), it defines them through index \(n\) provided \(p>n\).  Division by \((j+1)^3\) is division by a scalar in \(k\), so every term is a polynomial in \(T\).

### Theorem 3.1 — exact function-field coprimality

Let \(k\) have characteristic zero, or characteristic \(p>n\) with \(p\ne2,3\).  For the two solutions of (3.1)–(3.2),

\[
\boxed{
\gcd_{k[T]}\bigl(A_n(T),B_n(T)\bigr)=1.}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Consequently the horizontal function-field GCD has degree exactly zero:

\[
\deg\gcd(A_n,B_n)=0=o(n).
\]

#### Proof

The middle coefficient of the recurrence may depend on \(T\), but it cancels from the Casoratian calculation.  Therefore the same identity (1.4) holds:

\[
A_{n-1}(T)B_n(T)-A_n(T)B_{n-1}(T)
=-\frac6{n^3}\in k^\times.
\]

Let \(H(T)\) divide both \(A_n(T)\) and \(B_n(T)\).  Then \(H(T)\) divides the left side, hence divides the nonzero scalar \(-6/n^3\).  A polynomial dividing a unit is a unit. ∎

### General form of the argument

More generally, suppose

\[
c_{j+1}Y_{j+1}=Q_j(T)Y_j-d_jY_{j-1}
\]

with \(c_{j+1},d_j\in k^\times\) and \(Q_j(T)\in k[T]\).  For two solutions,

\[
W_j=\frac{d_j}{c_{j+1}}W_{j-1}.
\]

If the initial Casoratian is a nonzero scalar, then no nonconstant polynomial can divide both coordinates at any index.  This is a Wronskian/Casoratian phenomenon, not an \(abc\) phenomenon.

### What this theorem does—and does not—mean

It is a complete proof of one precise function-field analogue.  It is also too easy for the intended application.  The proof works because all scalar denominators are units in \(k[T]\).  Those same scalars carry the difficult rational-prime valuations over \(\mathbf Z\).

## 4. The diagonal and hypergeometric polynomial deformation

There is a different, genuinely geometric polynomial family.  Armin Straub proved that the Apéry numbers are the diagonal coefficients of

\[
\frac1{(1-x_1-x_2)(1-x_3-x_4)-x_1x_2x_3x_4}.
\]

Insert a parameter \(T\) in the product term and define

\[
R_T(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)
=
\frac1{(1-x_1-x_2)(1-x_3-x_4)-T x_1x_2x_3x_4}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Set

\[
\mathcal B_n(T)
=[x_1^nx_2^nx_3^nx_4^n]R_T.
\tag{4.2}
\]

### Proposition 4.1 — exact diagonal formula

One has

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal B_n(T)
=
\sum_{k=0}^n
\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2T^{n-k}.}
\tag{4.3}
\]

In particular,

\[
\mathcal B_n(1)=b_n.
\]

#### Proof

Expand (4.1) as

\[
R_T
=
\sum_{j\ge0}
T^j(x_1x_2x_3x_4)^j
(1-x_1-x_2)^{-j-1}
(1-x_3-x_4)^{-j-1}.
\]

For \(0\le j\le n\),

\[
[x_1^{n-j}x_2^{n-j}](1-x_1-x_2)^{-j-1}
=
\frac{(2n-j)!}{j!(n-j)!^2}.
\]

Put \(k=n-j\).  Then

\[
\frac{(2n-j)!}{j!(n-j)!^2}
=
\frac{(n+k)!}{(n-k)!k!^2}
=
\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}.
\]

Squaring the coefficient from the two factors and replacing \(j\) by \(n-k\) proves (4.3). ∎

If

\[
\mathcal A_n(X)
=
\sum_{k=0}^n
\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2X^k,
\]

then

\[
\mathcal A_n(X)
={}_4F_3\!\left(
\begin{matrix}-n,-n,n+1,n+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};X
\right)
\]

and

\[
\mathcal B_n(T)=T^n\mathcal A_n(T^{-1}).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Thus the answer to the diagonal/hypergeometric question is **yes**.

### But this is not the same recurrence family

The first two diagonal polynomials are

\[
\mathcal B_0(T)=1,
\qquad
\mathcal B_1(T)=T+4.
\]

By contrast, the original self-starting Apéry recurrence at \(n=0\) forces

\[
B_1=5B_0=5.
\]

Therefore the diagonal deformation cannot satisfy the unchanged second-order Apéry recurrence.  It is not the recurrence pencil of Section 3.

This distinction is unavoidable:

- preserving the second-order recurrence gives the Casoratian and exact coprimality;
- preserving the diagonal/binomial family gives \(\mathcal B_n(T)\), but loses the canonical second solution and the original Casoratian problem.

A termwise harmonic deformation of the classical formula for \(a_n\) can be invented, but it is not canonical, is not the diagonal of (4.1), and need not satisfy the same recurrence as \(\mathcal B_n(T)\).  Hence there is no well-defined GCD conjecture for the diagonal family until a companion is explicitly chosen.

## 5. Mason–Stothers and the radical of \(\mathcal B_n(T)\)

For polynomials over a field, the Mason–Stothers theorem says:

> If nonzero pairwise coprime polynomials \(U,V,W\) satisfy \(U+V=W\), and they do not all have zero derivative, then
> \[
> \max(\deg U,\deg V,\deg W)
> \le \deg\operatorname{rad}(UVW)-1.
> \]

The derivative hypothesis is essential in positive characteristic.  If all three polynomials are \(p\)-th powers, the theorem is silent.

### Proposition 5.1 — exact Frobenius degeneracy

In \(\mathbf F_p[T]\), for every \(n\ge0\),

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal B_{pn}(T)=\mathcal B_n(T)^p.}
\tag{5.1}
\]

#### Proof

Write

\[
c(N,k)=\binom Nk^2\binom{N+k}{k}^2.
\]

In (4.3) with \(N=pn\), Lucas's theorem gives

\[
\binom{pn}{j}\equiv0\pmod p
\qquad\text{unless }p\mid j.
\]

For \(j=pk\), Lucas gives

\[
\binom{pn}{pk}\equiv\binom nk,
\qquad
\binom{pn+pk}{pk}\equiv\binom{n+k}{k}\pmod p.
\]

Hence only the terms \(j=pk\) survive, and

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal B_{pn}(T)
&=
\sum_{k=0}^n c(n,k)T^{p(n-k)}\\
&=
\left(
\sum_{k=0}^n c(n,k)T^{n-k}
\right)^p
=
\mathcal B_n(T)^p.
\end{aligned}
\]

∎

Iterating (5.1) gives

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal B_{p^rn}(T)=\mathcal B_n(T)^{p^r}.}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Taking \(n=1\),

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal B_{p^r}(T)=(T+4)^{p^r}.}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
\deg\mathcal B_{p^r}=p^r,
\qquad
\deg\operatorname{rad}\mathcal B_{p^r}=1.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Every derivative in (5.3) vanishes.  Thus the most obvious hoped-for conclusion from Mason—linear radical degree—is not merely unproved; it is false in fixed positive characteristic.

### Why a radical estimate would not prove the GCD theorem anyway

Mason gives a **lower bound** for the radical of a product appearing in an additive identity.  The desired theorem is an **upper bound** for the common divisor of two different polynomials.

Large radical does not imply small gcd.  For example, let \(H_n(T)\) be squarefree of degree \(n\), and let

\[
F_n(T)=H_n(T),
\qquad
G_n(T)=H_n(T)(T-1).
\]

Both polynomials have maximal-size radicals, but

\[
\deg\gcd(F_n,G_n)=n.
\]

Applying Mason to a three-term recurrence for one solution can at best lower-bound the radical of a product of several consecutive terms and coefficient polynomials.  It does not control simultaneous zeros of two independent solutions at the same index.  In the recurrence pencil, the simultaneous-zero problem is already settled exactly by the Casoratian.

## 6. The exact horizontal-versus-vertical obstruction

Let

\[
L_n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,n),
\qquad
D_n=L_n^3,
\]

and define the denominator-cleared integers

\[
\alpha_n=D_na_n,
\qquad
\beta_n=D_nb_n.
\]

Multiplying (1.4) by \(D_{n-1}D_n\) gives

\[
\boxed{
\alpha_{n-1}\beta_n-\alpha_n\beta_{n-1}
=-6\frac{D_{n-1}D_n}{n^3}.}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\gcd(\alpha_n,\beta_n)
\mid
6\frac{D_{n-1}D_n}{n^3}.}
\tag{6.2}
\]

This is the same Bézout relation as in the polynomial proof, but its meaning changes completely with the coefficient ring.

### Over \(k[T]\)

The right side of (1.4) is a nonzero scalar.  Every nonzero scalar is a unit.  Therefore it has no horizontal prime divisors, and the gcd is one.

### Over \(\mathbf Z\)

The right side of (6.1) is a nonunit integer with many rational-prime divisors and substantial valuations.  The conjecture asks for strong cancellation inside precisely this allowed vertical prime support.

Thus the original problem is not the horizontal analogue of the polynomial problem.  Its difficulty is concentrated in the constant ring.

Geometrically:

- irreducible polynomials in \(T\) are horizontal closed points of the parameter line;
- ordinary rational primes are vertical divisors of an arithmetic surface over \(\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf Z\).

The Casoratian excludes horizontal common zeros but permits vertical common divisibility.  Passing from \(\mathbf Z\) to \(\mathbf F_q[T]\) turns all nonzero constants into units and deletes the hard valuations.

### Specialization does not repair the gap

Even if two polynomials are coprime in \(\mathbf Q[T]\), their values at a fixed integer can have a large gcd.  Polynomial coprimality only implies that common prime divisors of specializations are constrained by a resultant/Bézout constant; that constant may itself have large arithmetic height and many prime factors.

For the Apéry pencil, the Bézout constant is exactly the scalar in (1.4), and after denominator clearing it becomes the large integer in (6.1).  Therefore the function-field proof does not control the specialization at \(T=17\).

## 7. Positive characteristic is not automatically friendlier for GCD problems

The failure is not peculiar to the diagonal family.  Frobenius can make function-field GCDs larger than their characteristic-zero analogues.

Ailon and Rudnick proved strong bounded-gcd results for \(a(T)^n-1\) and \(b(T)^n-1\) over characteristic zero under multiplicative independence.  Silverman proved that over a finite constant field there are infinitely many \(n\) for which the degree of the corresponding gcd is at least a positive constant times \(n\), even under natural restrictions on \(n\).

This is exactly the phenomenon visible in (5.1)–(5.3): Frobenius repetitions create high multiplicities and coherent common factors.  Therefore “function-field \(abc\) is unconditional” is not, by itself, evidence that a positive-characteristic GCD analogue should be easier.

## 8. Direct answers to the four requested points

### 8.1 What is the correct formulation of the recurrence over \(\mathbf F_q(T)\)?

There is no unique correct formulation.

- **Literal base change:** use (1.1) with constant coefficients in \(\mathbf F_q\).  The sequence is constant in \(T\), trivial as a polynomial-gcd problem, and singular at indices \(n\equiv-1\pmod p\).
- **Recurrence pencil:** use (3.1), which specializes to the Apéry recurrence at \(T=17\).  This gives a genuine polynomial family and exact coprimality by Theorem 3.1.
- **Diagonal pencil:** use (4.1)–(4.3).  This gives a canonical geometric/hypergeometric family, but not the same recurrence and not a canonical companion.

A statement of a function-field GCD conjecture must first choose one of these models and define the companion and denominator ideal.  Without that choice, the problem is underdetermined.

### 8.2 Do the function-field Apéry numbers have a diagonal hypergeometric interpretation?

Yes.  The original \(b_n\) remain the diagonal coefficients of Straub's rational function after reduction, and the parameterized diagonal is exactly (4.3).  It is the reversed Apéry polynomial

\[
\mathcal B_n(T)=T^n\,{}_4F_3\!\left(
\begin{matrix}-n,-n,n+1,n+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};T^{-1}
\right).
\]

But this diagonal family is not the second-order recurrence pencil.

### 8.3 Does Mason–Stothers control \(\operatorname{rad}(b_n(T))\)?

Not in the required sense.

1. There is no natural three-term coprime identity isolating \(\mathcal B_n(T)\).
2. Mason lower-bounds a radical; it does not upper-bound a common divisor of two solutions.
3. In fixed characteristic,
   \[
   \mathcal B_{p^r}(T)=(T+4)^{p^r},
   \]
   so the radical has degree one.  This is the Frobenius exception to Mason.
4. For the recurrence pencil, the gcd is already one by the Casoratian, with no use of Mason.

### 8.4 What blocks transfer to \(\mathbf Q\)?

The exact obstruction is the change of units.

- In \(k[T]\), the Casoratian \(-6/n^3\) is a unit, so it excludes every nonconstant common divisor.
- In the denominator-cleared integer problem, the same identity becomes (6.1), whose right side has many nonunit rational-prime factors.
- Those vertical prime valuations are exactly the cross-prime anti-concentration problem in the current project.

Hence the function-field proof does not transfer because it solves a different divisor problem.  Even a hypothetical integer \(abc\) theorem would not automatically supply the missing cross-prime estimate; one would still need an additive identity whose radical controls the common divisors in question.

## 9. What a genuinely faithful function-field analogue would require

A faithful analogy must preserve the arithmetic direction that the naive parameter line erases.  Two possible formulations are:

1. **A Carlitz/Goss analogue.**  Replace ordinary factorials, harmonic denominators, the lcm, and \(\zeta(3)\) by their Carlitz-function-field counterparts.  Then define two explicit approximant sequences and a denominator ideal in \(\mathbf F_q[\theta]\).  This is a new problem; it is not obtained by reducing the classical recurrence coefficientwise.

2. **An arithmetic-surface analogue.**  Work over a two-dimensional coefficient ring in which one variable parametrizes the Apéry family and another retains nonunit “vertical” primes.  Mason on the horizontal curve alone would still not control the vertical gcd.

Until such data are specified, the natural recurrence-pencil theorem above is the only precise complete function-field statement, and it is exact coprimality.

---

## Final verdict

The proposed Mason–Stothers route does **not** yield a nontrivial function-field proof of the original Apéry GCD phenomenon.

What can be proved is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{For the natural Apéry recurrence pencil over }k(T),
\quad \gcd(A_n(T),B_n(T))=1.}
\]

This follows from the Casoratian and is stronger than \(o(n)\), but it is not evidence for the integer conjecture because the polynomial coefficient ring has made the relevant constants into units.

For the genuinely diagonal polynomial family, Mason is blocked both structurally and by the exact Frobenius identity

\[
\mathcal B_{pn}=\mathcal B_n^p.
\]

Therefore the precise obstruction is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the naive function-field model removes the vertical prime valuations
that constitute the original cross-prime problem.}}
\]
