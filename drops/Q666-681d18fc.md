# The shear parameter is killed by involutivity: a non-circular proof of strong \(c\)-reflection

## Executive verdict

The banked Q662 argument stops one algebraic line short of a complete proof. Once one knows

\[
Rb=b,
\qquad
Rc=c+\beta_p b,
\tag{0.1}
\]

for the reversal operator

\[
(Ru)_r=u_{p-1-r},
\tag{0.2}
\]

the shear is automatically zero, because \(R\) is literally an involution:

\[
R^2=1.
\]

Indeed,

\[
 c=R^2c=R(c+\beta_pb)=c+2\beta_pb.
\]

For \(p\ge5\), \(2\) is invertible and \(b\ne0\) because \(b_0=1\). Hence

\[
\boxed{\beta_p=0.}
\tag{0.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{c_{p-1-r}\equiv c_r\pmod p\qquad(0\le r\le p-1),}
\tag{0.4}
\]

and in particular

\[
\boxed{c_{p-1}=0,\qquad c_{p-2}=1.}
\tag{0.5}
\]

This proof is not circular. The logical order is

```text
recurrence covariance under r -> p-1-r
    => reversal R acts on the 2-dimensional solution space and R^2=1
b endpoint calculation
    => Rb=b
Casoratian/Wronskian invariance
    => Rc=c+beta_p b, with quotient eigenvalue +1
involutivity R^2=1
    => 2 beta_p=0
    => beta_p=0
    => c-reflection
    => endpoint identities
    => SWAP matrix.
```

The Wronskian does not determine the shear coefficient by itself. Its role is to determine the **other diagonal entry**: it rules out the possibility that \(R\) acts by \(-1\) on the quotient by the \(b\)-line. Involutivity then kills the residual unipotent shear.

The conclusion is stronger than either proposed route. It proves reflection for **every** \(p\)-integral solution germ of the recurrence, not only for \(c\), and it works at ordinary and nonordinary primes alike.

# 1. Complete recurrence-only proof

Let

\[
P(n)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5
     =(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

For a prime \(p\ge5\), let \(V_p\) be the two-dimensional space of sequences

\[
u=(u_0,u_1,\ldots,u_{p-1})\in\mathbf F_p^p
\]

satisfying

\[
(n+1)^3u_{n+1}=P(n)u_n-n^3u_{n-1}
\qquad(1\le n\le p-2).
\tag{1.1}
\]

Because \(n+1\ne0\pmod p\) on this range, the initial pair \((u_0,u_1)\) determines \(u\) uniquely. Thus \(V_p\cong\mathbf F_p^2\).

## 1.1 Reversal preserves the recurrence

The middle coefficient has the exact antisymmetry

\[
P(-1-x)=-P(x).
\tag{1.2}
\]

This is immediate from the factored form: \(2(-1-x)+1=-(2x+1)\), while

\[
17(-1-x)^2+17(-1-x)+5=17x^2+17x+5.
\]

Define

\[
(Ru)_r=u_{p-1-r}.
\tag{1.3}
\]

For \(1\le n\le p-2\), apply (1.1) at \(m=p-1-n\):

\[
(p-n)^3u_{p-n}
 =P(p-1-n)u_{p-1-n}
  -(p-1-n)^3u_{p-2-n}.
\]

Modulo \(p\),

\[
(p-n)^3=-n^3,
\qquad
(p-1-n)^3=-(n+1)^3,
\qquad
P(p-1-n)=-P(n).
\]

After rearranging, this becomes

\[
(n+1)^3u_{p-2-n}
 =P(n)u_{p-1-n}-n^3u_{p-n},
\]

which is precisely (1.1) for \(Ru\). Hence

\[
R:V_p\longrightarrow V_p
\]

is linear. From its definition,

\[
\boxed{R^2=1.}
\tag{1.4}
\]

No reflection theorem has been used here; (1.4) is merely reversal twice.

## 1.2 The Apéry solution \(b\) is fixed

The two endpoint congruences follow directly from

\[
b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2.
\]

For \(n=p-1\), every term with \(k\ge1\) contains

\[
\binom{p-1+k}{k},
\]

which is divisible by \(p\). Therefore only \(k=0\) survives:

\[
b_{p-1}\equiv1=b_0\pmod p.
\tag{1.5}
\]

For \(n=p-2\), every term with \(k\ge2\) contains

\[
\binom{p-2+k}{k},
\]

which is divisible by \(p\). The terms \(k=0,1\) give

\[
b_{p-2}\equiv1+(p-2)^2(p-1)^2
          \equiv1+4=5=b_1\pmod p.
\tag{1.6}
\]

Thus \(Rb\) and \(b\) have the same initial pair \((1,5)\). Uniqueness for (1.1) gives

\[
\boxed{Rb=b.}
\tag{1.7}
\]

This is the \(b\)-reflection, proved without the SWAP matrix.

## 1.3 What the Casoratian actually determines

For \(x,y\in V_p\), set

\[
W_n(x,y)=x_ny_{n-1}-x_{n-1}y_n.
\tag{1.8}
\]

The recurrence gives

\[
W_{n+1}(x,y)=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}W_n(x,y),
\]

so

\[
W_n(x,y)=\frac{C(x,y)}{n^3}
\qquad(1\le n\le p-1)
\tag{1.9}
\]

for an alternating bilinear constant \(C(x,y)\). For the normalized pair

\[
b_0=1,\ b_1=5,\qquad c_0=0,\ c_1=1,
\]

one has

\[
W_n(b,c)=-\frac1{n^3}.
\tag{1.10}
\]

Reversal preserves this form. Indeed,

\[
\begin{aligned}
W_n(Rx,Ry)
 &=x_{p-1-n}y_{p-n}-x_{p-n}y_{p-1-n}\\
 &=-W_{p-n}(x,y)\\
 &=-\frac{C(x,y)}{(p-n)^3}\\
 &=\frac{C(x,y)}{n^3}\\
 &=W_n(x,y).
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.11}
\]

Consequently \(R\) has determinant \(+1\) on \(V_p\).

Since \(Rb=b\), write in the basis \((b,c)\)

\[
Rc=\beta_pb+\delta_pc.
\tag{1.12}
\]

Then

\[
[R]_{(b,c)}=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&\beta_p\\
0&\delta_p
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det R=\delta_p.
\]

Equation (1.11) gives

\[
\delta_p=1,
\]

hence exactly the shear form banked in Q662:

\[
\boxed{Rc=c+\beta_pb.}
\tag{1.13}
\]

This pinpoints the Wronskian's role. It does **not** see \(eta_p\), because adding a multiple of \(b\) to \(c\) leaves \(W(b,c)\) unchanged. It does see the quotient eigenvalue \(\delta_p\), and proves that it is \(+1\), not \(-1\).

## 1.4 Involutivity kills the shear

Now apply \(R\) once more:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c
  &=R^2c\\
  &=R(c+\beta_pb)\\
  &=Rc+\beta_pRb\\
  &=(c+\beta_pb)+\beta_pb\\
  &=c+2\beta_pb.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore

\[
2\beta_pb=0.
\]

Since \(p\ge5\) and \(b_0=1\),

\[
\boxed{\beta_p=0.}
\tag{1.14}
\]

This proves \(Rc=c\).

### Basis-free version

The same proof works for every \(u\in V_p\). From (1.7) and (1.11),

\[
W(b,Ru)=W(Rb,Ru)=W(b,u).
\]

Thus

\[
W(b,Ru-u)=0.
\]

Two solutions with zero Casoratian are linearly dependent, so

\[
Ru-u=\lambda(u)b
\tag{1.15}
\]

for some \(\lambda(u)\in\mathbf F_p\). Applying \(R+1\) to (1.15),

\[
0=(R+1)(R-1)u=(R+1)(\lambda(u)b)=2\lambda(u)b.
\]

Hence \(\lambda(u)=0\). We obtain the stronger theorem

\[
\boxed{u_{p-1-r}=u_r
\quad\text{for every }u\in V_p\text{ and every }0\le r\le p-1.}
\tag{1.16}
\]

This is the strong-reflection statement already used downstream in the repository.

# 2. Answer to Route B: the endpoint proof is direct and non-circular

Once Q662 has established (1.13), the shortest endpoint proof is only two evaluations.

At \(r=0\),

\[
c_{p-1}=c_0+\beta_pb_0=\beta_p.
\tag{2.1}
\]

At \(r=p-1\), using \(c_0=0\) and \(b_{p-1}=1\),

\[
0=c_0=c_{p-1}+\beta_pb_{p-1}=2\beta_p.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Thus \(eta_p=0\), and (2.1) gives

\[
\boxed{c_{p-1}=0.}
\tag{2.3}
\]

Then at \(r=1\),

\[
c_{p-2}=c_1+\beta_pb_1=1.
\tag{2.4}
\]

This uses neither the SWAP matrix nor \(c\)-reflection as an input. It uses only:

1. the already proved shear law;
2. \(b_0=b_{p-1}=1\);
3. the fact that reversal is an involution, equivalently that the shear formula is valid at both endpoints.

If one wants to avoid even evaluating the shear formula at \(r=p-1\), the operator proof \(R^2c=c\) gives the same conclusion.

## 2.1 The SWAP identity is an output

Let \(T_p\) denote the full transfer from the left state to the right state in the convention of the question:

\[
T_p
\binom{u_1}{u_0}
=
\binom{u_{p-1}}{u_{p-2}}.
\]

Strong reflection gives

\[
\binom{u_{p-1}}{u_{p-2}}
=
\binom{u_0}{u_1}.
\]

Since this holds for every initial pair,

\[
\boxed{
T_p=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.}
\tag{2.5}
\]

Thus the correct dependency is

```text
strong reflection => SWAP,
```

not the other way around.

# 3. Answer to Route A: what geometry would have to prove

## 3.1 A geometric involution alone is not enough

Suppose an order-two geometric correspondence acts on a two-dimensional realization and fixes the \(b\)-line. Before using a pairing, its matrix can have the form

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&\beta\\
0&\varepsilon
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

The condition \(A^2=1\) gives

\[
\varepsilon^2=1,
\qquad
\beta(1+\varepsilon)=0.
\tag{3.2}
\]

There are two cases over a field of odd characteristic:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\varepsilon=+1 & \beta=0,\\
\varepsilon=-1 & \beta\text{ arbitrary}.
\end{array}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Indeed,

\[
\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}^2=I
\]

for every \(eta\). Therefore the statement “the correspondence is an involution and fixes the holomorphic period” does **not** by itself prove diagonal action.

What closes the argument is the extra statement

\[
\det A=+1,
\tag{3.4}
\]

or equivalently that the induced action on the quotient by the \(b\)-line is \(+1\). In the recurrence realization, (3.4) is exactly Casoratian invariance.

Once (3.4) is known, \(A\) is unipotent. An order-two unipotent matrix is the identity in odd characteristic:

\[
\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^2
=
\begin{pmatrix}1&2\beta\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\]

so \(eta=0\).

The same statement holds over a torsion-free integral lattice: a unipotent integral involution has zero off-diagonal term because \(2\beta=0\) in \(\mathbf Z\).

## 3.2 The proposed K3 interpretation needs two cautions

There are two distinct objects that should not be silently identified.

First, the map

\[
r\longmapsto p-1-r
\]

is a characteristic-\(p\) symmetry of the **recurrence clock** or Kummer/Mellin index. Its elementary source is (1.2). The current banked argument does not require, and the repository does not presently identify, this index reversal with an automorphism of one fixed K3 fiber.

Second, the companion \(c\) is not automatically a second vector in the same pure integral \(H^2\)-period lattice as \(b\). The repository's categorical bookkeeping describes the companion direction as a mixed extension / \(p\)-adic normal-function direction rather than simply a second pure holomorphic period. An automorphism of the pure \(H^2\) piece therefore would not by itself determine the action on \(c\); it would have to lift to the extension.

A fully geometric proof would need to establish all of the following:

```text
1. A genuine order-two correspondence realizes r -> p-1-r.
2. It acts on the full object containing both b and the companion direction c.
3. It fixes the b-line.
4. It preserves the relevant pairing/orientation, so its determinant is +1.
5. Its action is integral (or at least defined over F_p with p odd).
```

Then the same two-line matrix argument forces identity on the \((b,c)\)-block. In other words, geometry would not need to compute \(eta_p\); it would only need to justify the involution and the determinant sign.

At present the elementary recurrence proof supplies these facts more directly and with fewer categorical assumptions.

# 4. A number-theoretic formula for \(eta_p\)

Under the convention

\[
Rc=c+\beta_pb,
\tag{4.1}
\]

the cleanest formula is obtained at \(r=0\):

\[
\boxed{\beta_p=c_{p-1}.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This works uniformly for every \(p\ge5\), because \(b_0=1\).

For \(p\ge7\), one may also use \(r=1\):

\[
c_{p-2}=c_1+\beta_pb_1=1+5\beta_p,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{\beta_p=\frac{c_{p-2}-1}{5}.}
\tag{4.3}
\]

The quotient written in the question has the opposite sign under convention (4.1): since \(b_{p-2}=b_1=5\),

\[
\frac{c_1-c_{p-2}}{b_{p-2}}=-\beta_p.
\tag{4.4}
\]

If Q662 used \(Rc=c-\beta_pb\), then the question's sign is the corresponding one. Since the answer is zero, the sign did not affect the computation, but it matters in the exact formula.

At \(p=5\), \(b_{p-2}=b_1=5\equiv0\pmod5\), so (4.4) is undefined; (4.2) remains valid.

The formula making vanishing conceptually obvious is

\[
\boxed{\beta_p\in\mathbf F_p[2].}
\tag{4.5}
\]

Indeed,

\[
0=(R^2-1)c=(R+1)(R-1)c=(R+1)(\beta_pb)=2\beta_pb.
\]

Since \(\mathbf F_p\) has no nonzero additive \(2\)-torsion for odd \(p\), \(eta_p=0\).

## 4.1 Group-cohomological interpretation

The fixed line \(\mathbf F_pb\) is a trivial module for the group

\[
C_2=\langle R:R^2=1\rangle.
\]

The difference

\[
(R-1)c=\beta_pb
\]

is a \(1\)-cocycle. The cocycle relation at \(R^2=1\) is

\[
\beta_pb+R(\beta_pb)=2\beta_pb=0.
\]

Thus

\[
H^1(C_2,\mathbf F_pb)=0
\qquad(p\ne2),
\tag{4.6}
\]

which is the abstract reason the extension splits. This is the finite-group averaging principle: because \(|C_2|=2\) is invertible, a unipotent \(C_2\)-extension of the trivial representation by itself cannot be nontrivial.

This also explains the exceptional role of characteristic \(2\): there a nonzero shear could have order two. The project is in \(p\ge5\), so that obstruction never arises.

The computation through \(p=499\) is therefore a useful regression test, but it is no longer evidence for an open assertion; \(eta_p=0\) is forced algebraically for every \(p\ge5\).

# 5. Harmonic-number and logarithmic-solution formulas

There is an exact variation-of-constants formula that clarifies both the appeal and the limitation of the harmonic approach.

From

\[
W_n(b,c)
=b_nc_{n-1}-b_{n-1}c_n
=-\frac1{n^3},
\]

we obtain over \(\mathbf Q\)

\[
\frac{c_n}{b_n}-\frac{c_{n-1}}{b_{n-1}}
 =\frac1{n^3b_nb_{n-1}}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Since \(c_0/b_0=0\), telescoping gives

\[
\boxed{
 c_n=b_n\sum_{k=1}^n
 \frac1{k^3b_kb_{k-1}}.}
\tag{5.2}
\]

This is a compact partial-fractions/logarithmic formula for the normalized companion. More elaborate binomial-harmonic formulas for the Apéry numerator are equivalent refinements of the same second-solution construction.

## 5.1 A clean proof on the zero-free subfamily

Suppose temporarily that

\[
b_j\not\equiv0\pmod p
\qquad(0\le j\le p-1),
\]

so every denominator in (5.2) is a \(p\)-adic unit. Put

\[
s_k=\frac1{k^3b_kb_{k-1}}.
\]

Using \(b_{p-1-r}=b_r\),

\[
b_{p-k}=b_{k-1},
\qquad
b_{p-k-1}=b_k.
\]

Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
s_{p-k}
 &=\frac1{(p-k)^3b_{p-k}b_{p-k-1}}\\
 &\equiv-\frac1{k^3b_{k-1}b_k}\\
 &=-s_k\pmod p.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.3}
\]

The terms \(k\) and \(p-k\) cancel in pairs, and \(p\) odd gives no fixed index. Since \(b_{p-1}=1\), (5.2) yields

\[
c_{p-1}\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Thus the harmonic/partial-fractions picture contains the same reflection mechanism.

## 5.2 Why this is not a uniform proof

At a prime with an Apéry zero, some \(b_k\) or \(b_{k-1}\) in (5.2) is divisible by \(p\). The individual summands then have \(p\)-adic poles and cannot be reduced term by term modulo \(p\). The final value \(c_n\) remains \(p\)-integral because the poles cancel globally, but ordinary first-order reflection congruences do not control that cancellation after inversion.

The prime \(p=11\), where \(b_5\equiv0\), is already a sharp warning: the desired conclusion \(c_{10}\equiv0\) is true, but the naive reduction of (5.2) is undefined.

Wolstenholme-type facts such as

\[
H_{p-1}\equiv0\pmod{p^2}
\]

or the standard congruences for

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}k^{-m}
\]

control unweighted harmonic sums. They do not directly resolve the extra poles introduced by \(b_kb_{k-1}\). A uniform harmonic proof would require either:

1. a higher-precision reflected congruence sufficient to pair the polar terms in \(\mathbf Q_p\); or
2. a regularized telescoping identity in which the poles cancel before reduction.

Such a regularization is plausible, but it would essentially repackage the recurrence-reversal proof above. It is not needed for \(eta_p=0\).

## 5.3 Why a unit-root or regulator proof cannot be fundamental here

Strong reflection holds for every prime \(p\ge5\), including primes at which the relevant unit root disappears. Therefore a proof depending on ordinarity or on an ordinary unit-root regulator would have the wrong domain of validity.

The \(p\)-adic normal-function viewpoint may explain the companion direction geometrically, but the vanishing of the shear is a simpler finite-characteristic representation-theoretic fact:

\[
\text{determinant }+1
\quad+\quad
\text{order }2
\quad+\quad
p\ne2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{no unipotent shear}.
\]

# 6. Direct answers to the four questions

## (1) Route B

Yes. There is a direct, non-circular proof:

\[
\boxed{c_{p-1}=0,\qquad c_{p-2}=1.}
\]

The shortest proof is \(Rc=c+\beta b\), \(R^2=1\), \(Rb=b\), hence \(2\beta b=0\). The complete recurrence-only proof is Sections 1.1--1.4. The SWAP identity is derived only afterward.

No harmonic-number or regulator input is required.

## (2) Route A

A genuine geometric involution would force diagonal action only after one also proves that its action on the quotient has eigenvalue \(+1\), equivalently determinant \(+1\) on the relevant two-dimensional block. An involution fixing one line can otherwise have matrix

\[
\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
\]

with arbitrary \(eta\).

Moreover, identifying recurrence-index reversal with a K3 automorphism, and identifying \(c\) with a second pure \(H^2\)-period, are additional geometric assertions not presently needed or banked. The companion direction must be treated as the appropriate mixed/normal-function extension.

## (3) Formula for \(eta_p\)

With \(Rc=c+\beta_pb\),

\[
\boxed{eta_p=c_{p-1}}
\]

for every \(p\ge5\), and for \(p\ge7\),

\[
\boxed{eta_p=(c_{p-2}-1)/5.}
\]

The quotient in the question equals \(-\beta_p\) under this sign convention. The intrinsic formula is

\[
2\beta_p=0,
\]

so \(eta_p=0\) in every odd characteristic.

## (4) Harmonic/Kummer/Wolstenholme route

The exact identity

\[
 c_n=b_n\sum_{k=1}^n(k^3b_kb_{k-1})^{-1}
\]

gives an elegant pair-cancellation proof when the entire \(b\)-row is nonzero modulo \(p\). It is not uniform because Apéry zeros create \(p\)-adic poles in the individual summands. Standard Wolstenholme congruences do not by themselves remove those poles.

The recurrence involution is the uniform regularized argument and already proves the result at zero-free, ordinary, nonordinary, and multiple-zero primes simultaneously.
