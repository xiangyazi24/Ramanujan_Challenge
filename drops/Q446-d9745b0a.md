# Transfer matrices, Frobenius, and the first Apéry zero

## Executive verdict

There is a useful transfer-matrix interpretation of the first zero, but it is **not** a Pisano-period or matrix-order problem, and the proposed period-
\(p\) monodromy matrix is not defined in the stated gauge.

The correct conclusions are:

1. The indexing in the question is off by one and the target coordinate is reversed. For \(1\le r<p\),
   
   \[
   \binom{b_r}{b_{r-1}}
   =\Phi_p(r)\binom51,
   \qquad
   \Phi_p(r):=M_p(r-1)\cdots M_p(1),
   \]
   
   with \(\Phi_p(1)=I\). Hence
   
   \[
   \boxed{
   r\in Z_p
   \iff e_1^{\!T}\Phi_p(r)(5,1)^T=0
   \iff \Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1].}
   \]
   
   It is the **first component**, not the second, that vanishes. Including \(M(0)\) while starting from \((5,1)^T\) is incorrect; \(M(0)\) is singular and sends \((5,1)^T\) to \((25,5)^T\).

2. In group-theoretic language, \(r_p\) is the first time the deterministic prefix word
   
   \[
   g_r=M_p(r-1)\cdots M_p(1)\in \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)
   \]
   
   enters the coset
   
   \[
   \mathcal C_p=\{g:g[5:1]=[0:1]\}.
   \]
   
   This coset has density \(1/(p+1)\) in \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\). It is a **prefix-hitting problem**, not the order of one element.

3. The coefficient matrices are periodic as rational functions of the clock only on their regular domain. The raw matrix has a pole at \(n=p-1\), so
   
   \[
   M_p(p-1)\cdots M_p(0)
   \]
   
   does not exist. Clearing denominators produces a polynomial matrix, but the gauge becomes singular modulo \(p\); its period product is not the crystalline Frobenius of the Apéry motive.

4. The eigenvalues of a partial product \(\Phi_p(r)\) do not have an intrinsic spectral meaning. Under a time-dependent gauge they are not even invariant. The invariant datum is the matrix coefficient
   
   \[
   e_1^{\!T}\Phi_p(r)(5,1)^T=b_r.
   \]

5. The project **does** contain a correct spectral reformulation, but it is a different one. For each character index \(j\), the marked rank-three Frobenius factor is
   
   \[
   P_{p,j}(X)=(X-p)(X^2-a_{p,j}X+p^3),
   \qquad a_{p,j}\equiv b_j\pmod{\mathfrak p}.
   \]
   
   Thus
   
   \[
   \boxed{
   r_p=\min\{j\ge1:\text{the marked character-}j\text{ Frobenius has no slope-zero root}\}.}
   \]
   
   In this precise sense \(r_p\) is the first **nonordinary character twist**. It is not the first supersingular point of the original K3 moduli curve.

6. The differential-equation singularities are in the generating-function variable, whereas \(r\) is a coefficient or Mellin-character index. There is no canonical notion of the “nearest singularity” in \(\mathbf F_p\), and \(r/p\) is not a moduli coordinate.

7. The Pollard-rho heuristic in the question uses the wrong scale. A random orbit needs about \(\sqrt p\) steps for its **first collision**, but about \(p\) steps to hit one **specified target**. Therefore \(r_p\asymp p\) is compatible with mixing, not evidence against it.

8. Reflection changes the random model substantially. On the lower half there are about \(p/2\) independent candidate positions, each with hit probability about \(1/p\). The natural prediction is a Poisson point process of intensity \(1/2\), explaining
   
   \[
   \Pr(Z_p=\varnothing)\approx e^{-1/2}
   \]
   
   and the observed Poisson\((1/2)\) law for the number of reflected zero pairs.

9. Consequently, if \(r_p\) is the **smallest** zero among all nonempty zero sets, the baseline prediction for
   
   \[
   \alpha_p=\frac{2r_p+1}{p}
   \]
   
   is not uniform. Conditional on at least one zero pair, the Poisson model predicts
   
   \[
   \Pr(\alpha_p\le t\mid Z_p\ne\varnothing)
   =\frac{1-e^{-t/2}}{1-e^{-1/2}}.
   \]
   
   Uniformity is the correct prediction after conditioning on **exactly one reflected pair**, \(z_p=2\).

10. The most plausible proof route is therefore not an order computation. It is an averaged **Poisson/factorial-moment theorem for the zero point process**, built from the gap-polynomial machinery and a horizontal same-characteristic dispersion theorem. The transfer matrices provide the exact incidence formulation, but no currently available autonomous finite-field dynamics theorem supplies the required shrinking-target estimate.

## 1. Correct transfer formulation

Put

\[
A(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)
     =34n^3+51n^2+27n+5
\]

and

\[
M(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
A(n)/(n+1)^3&-n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For \(n\ge1\),

\[
\binom{b_{n+1}}{b_n}=M(n)\binom{b_n}{b_{n-1}}.
\]

Since

\[
\binom{b_1}{b_0}=\binom51,
\]

we obtain, for \(r\ge1\),

\[
\binom{b_r}{b_{r-1}}
=M(r-1)\cdots M(1)\binom51.
\tag{1.1}
\]

For \(1\le n\le p-2\),

\[
\det M(n)=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\ne0\pmod p,
\]

so all matrices appearing in `(1.1)` lie in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\).

At \(n=0\), however,

\[
M(0)=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det M(0)=0.
\]

It can be used only if one starts from an artificial vector \((b_0,b_{-1})\); the value of \(b_{-1}\) is then killed by the zero coefficient. It must not be inserted in front of \((b_1,b_0)=(5,1)\).

Thus the exact projective criterion is

\[
\boxed{
 b_r\equiv0\pmod p
 \iff
 \Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1],
 \qquad
 \Phi_p(r)=M_p(r-1)\cdots M_p(1).}
\tag{1.2}
\]

The target is the vertical line

\[
L_0=\{(0,y):y\in\mathbf F_p\},
\]

not \(\{(x,0)\}\).

### 1.1 Riccati form

Whenever \(b_{n-1}\ne0\), set

\[
x_n=\frac{b_n}{b_{n-1}}\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p).
\]

Then

\[
x_{n+1}
=\frac{A(n)}{(n+1)^3}
 -\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\frac1{x_n}.
\tag{1.3}
\]

The zero condition is \(x_r=0\). This is a nonautonomous Riccati cocycle. The no-consecutive-zero lemma guarantees that the projective orbit never has two successive target hits for \(p>3\).

## 2. Why there is no Pisano-order analogue

For a constant matrix \(T\), one has \(v_n=T^nv_0\). The orbit lies in the cyclic subgroup generated by \(T\), and its period is controlled by the order of \(T\) in \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) or \(\mathrm{PGL}_2\). This is the Fibonacci/Pisano situation.

For Apéry,

\[
g_r=M(r-1)\cdots M(1)
\]

is a path-ordered word, and

\[
g_{r+s}
e g_r^s,
\qquad
g_{r+s}\ne g_sg_r
\]

in general. The shifted product is

\[
g_{r+s}
=M(r+s-1)\cdots M(r)\,g_r,
\]

whose first factor depends on the starting time \(r\).

Therefore \(r_p\) is not the order of \(g_1\), \(g_r\), or any evident fixed element. It is the first entrance time of the sequence of prefixes \(g_r\) into

\[
\mathcal C_p=\{g\in\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p):g[5:1]=[0:1]\}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

The set \(\mathcal C_p\) is a coset of the stabilizer of \([5:1]\). Since \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) acts transitively on \(p+1\) projective points,

\[
\frac{|\mathcal C_p|}{|\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)|}
=\frac1{p+1}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

If the prefixes behaved like independent uniform group elements, one would expect order-\(p\) waiting time for this coset.

### 2.1 What periodic Floquet theory would say in a regular problem

Suppose, hypothetically, that \(T(n+p)=T(n)\) and every \(T(n)\) were invertible. Define the one-period monodromy

\[
C=T(p-1)\cdots T(0).
\]

Then the evolution at a fixed phase after \(k\) periods is controlled by powers \(C^k\). The eventual projective period divides

\[
p\,\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{PGL}_2}(C).
\]

Even in that regular setting, the first hit **inside the initial period** is determined by the prefix products \(T(r-1)\cdots T(0)\), not by the order of \(C\).

The Apéry recurrence fails the regular hypotheses at the period boundary, so even this limited Floquet conclusion is unavailable in the raw gauge.

### 2.2 The actual finite-state analogue is the Lucas automaton

The genuinely useful global structure modulo \(p\) is Gessel's Lucas law. If

\[
n=n_0+n_1p+\cdots+n_kp^k,
\qquad 0\le n_i<p,
\]

then

\[
b_n\equiv\prod_{i=0}^k b_{n_i}\pmod p.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Consequently,

\[
p\nmid b_n
\iff n_i\notin Z_p\quad\text{for every base-}p\text{ digit }n_i.
\tag{2.4}
\]

This is a \(p\)-automatic multiplicative automaton, not a periodic orbit generated by one group element. A zero digit is absorbing. If \(Z_p=\varnothing\), the nonzero digit values generate a subgroup of \(\mathbf F_p^\times\), and multiplicative orders affect the **values** of the automaton, but they do not determine a first zero \(r_p\).

## 3. Why the proposed period product is not Frobenius

The rational coefficient function satisfies

\[
M(n+p)=M(n)
\]

where both sides are defined. But \(M(p-1)\) has the denominator \(p^3\), so

\[
P(p)=M(p-1)\cdots M(0)
\]

is not a matrix over \(\mathbf F_p\).

### 3.1 Clearing denominators does not fix the conceptual problem

Set

\[
B_n=(n!)^3b_n.
\]

Then

\[
B_{n+1}=A(n)B_n-n^6B_{n-1},
\]

with polynomial transfer matrix

\[
\widetilde M(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
A(n)&-n^6\\1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

This matrix is \(p\)-periodic in \(n\), but

\[
\det\widetilde M(n)=n^6,
\]

so it is singular when \(n\equiv0\pmod p\). Moreover the gauge

\[
b_n\longmapsto B_n=(n!)^3b_n
\]

is invertible modulo \(p\) only for \(n<p\). At and beyond \(n=p\), the factorial gauge collapses.

This is exactly what the repository's transfer-coordinate audit records: the product containing the \(n=0\) factor has rank one, while the full two-solution fundamental matrix is invertible only before the characteristic boundary.

Therefore a product of the matrices in `(3.1)` is a coefficient-recursion object. It is not the Frobenius matrix of the motive.

### 3.2 Dimension mismatch

The Apéry generating function

\[
F(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nt^n
\]

satisfies the order-three Picard--Fuchs equation

\[
\Bigl[
\theta^3
-t(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+t^2(\theta+1)^3
\Bigr]F=0,
\qquad \theta=t\frac{d}{dt}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Its crystalline or rigid Frobenius acts on a rank-three cohomological solution space. The coefficient recurrence happens to have a two-dimensional state. A naive \(2\times2\) period product cannot simply be identified with this rank-three Frobenius.

Dwork Frobenius is obtained from \(p\)-adic analytic continuation and a Frobenius pullback on the differential module. Its unit-root formulas involve Hasse invariants and ratios of Frobenius truncations, not the undefined product \(M(p-1)\cdots M(0)\).

Also, a \(p\)-adic unit root means valuation \(0\). It is not generally “\(1+O(1/p)\).” If a chosen normalization is congruent to \(1\) modulo \(p\), the \(p\)-adic notation is \(1+O(p)\), not \(1+O(1/p)\).

## 4. Why prefix eigenvalues do not control the hit

Let

\[
\Phi_p(r)=M_p(r-1)\cdots M_p(1).
\]

The hit is

\[
e_1^{\!T}\Phi_p(r)v_0=0,
\qquad v_0=(5,1)^T.
\tag{4.1}
\]

This is a matrix coefficient depending on:

- both eigenvalues;
- both eigenvectors;
- the decomposition of the initial vector in the eigenbasis;
- the position of the target covector \(e_1^T\).

The unordered eigenvalue pair alone cannot determine `(4.1)`.

There is a stronger obstruction: prefix eigenvalues are not gauge invariant. For a time-dependent invertible gauge \(H(n)\), put

\[
M'(n)=H(n+1)M(n)H(n)^{-1}.
\]

Then

\[
\Phi'_p(r)=H(r)\Phi_p(r)H(1)^{-1}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

This is generally not a conjugation, because \(H(r)\ne H(1)\). Hence the trace and eigenvalues of a prefix can change under an equivalent formulation of the same recurrence. The transformed initial and target lines change with it, and the incidence condition remains invariant.

Thus the right invariant object is the projective cocycle and its matrix coefficient, not the spectrum of each prefix.

A full closed-period monodromy, when it exists in a regular periodic difference equation, transforms by conjugation and has invariant eigenvalues. The Apéry clock has a singular period boundary, and \(r_p\) lies inside the first characteristic window anyway.

## 5. The correct spectral interpretation already present in the project

The repository's marked toric/Kummer construction gives, for every \(1\le j\le p-2\), an effective rank-three Frobenius polynomial

\[
P_{p,j}(X)
=(X-p)(X^2-a_{p,j}X+p^3),
\qquad
 a_{p,j}\equiv b_j\pmod{\mathfrak p}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Write \(m=v_{\mathfrak p}(a_{p,j})\). The two roots of the quadratic factor have slopes

\[
\begin{cases}
(0,3),&m=0,\\
(1,2),&m=1,\\
(3/2,3/2),&m\ge2.
\end{cases}
\tag{5.2}
\]

After adjoining the Tate root of slope \(1\), one obtains:

- if \(b_j\not\equiv0\pmod p\), slopes \((0,1,3)\), with one unit root;
- if \(b_j\equiv0\pmod p\) and \(m=1\), slopes \((1,1,2)\);
- if \(m\ge2\), slopes \((1,3/2,3/2)\).

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
 j\in Z_p
 \iff
 \text{the marked character-}j\text{ Frobenius has no slope-zero eigenvalue}.}
\tag{5.3}
\]

This yields the exact spectral restatement

\[
\boxed{
 r_p
 =\min\{j\ge1:\mathcal V_{p,j}\text{ is nonordinary}\}.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

This is much closer to a useful arithmetic formulation than the eigenvalues of the transfer prefixes.

### 5.1 Nonordinary is not the same as supersingular

The congruence \(b_j\equiv0\pmod p\) only removes the slope-zero root. If \(v_{\mathfrak p}(a_{p,j})=1\), the quadratic slopes are \((1,2)\), not \((3/2,3/2)\). Thus a zero is a nonordinary or Hasse-zero event, but not necessarily a supersingular event.

For a K3 surface, “supersingular” also has a standard stronger meaning involving the entire \(H^2\) slope structure or Picard rank. It should not be used for every coefficient congruence \(b_j\equiv0\pmod p\).

### 5.2 The central modular congruence is only one index

The known relation

\[
b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p\bigl(\eta(2\tau)^4\eta(4\tau)^4\bigr)\pmod p
\]

identifies the central fixed point of reflection with one modular-form coefficient. It does not identify all prefix spectra, and it gives no formula for the first noncentral zero.

## 6. The ODE singularities do not locate \(r_p\)

In the standard generating-function coordinate \(t\), the leading coefficient of `(3.2)` contains

\[
1-34t+t^2.
\]

Thus the finite nonzero singularities are

\[
t=17\pm12\sqrt2,
\tag{6.1}
\]

or their images under whatever reciprocal/Möbius normalization is chosen. The values

\[
\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}{34}
\]

are not the singularities in this standard coordinate.

There are three separate reasons why a “nearest singularity” principle cannot define \(r_p\):

1. **Different variables.** The singularities live in the generating-function parameter \(t\). The integer \(r\) is a coefficient index.

2. **No canonical distance.** The finite field \(\mathbf F_p\) has no intrinsic linear ordering or nearest-point metric. Choosing representatives in \([0,p)\) is not invariant under a change of coordinate on the moduli curve.

3. **Mellin duality.** In the project's marked-coordinate interpretation, \(j\) labels a multiplicative character \(x\mapsto x^j\). The normalized number \(j/(p-1)\) is a character angle in the Mellin dual, not a point \(t=j/p\) on the original K3 moduli line.

The singularities can still matter globally: they determine local monodromy, the geometric monodromy group, functional equations, and reflection symmetries. Those structures may constrain the **statistics** of the character-indexed Frobenius classes. They do not identify the first zero with the least residue near a singular parameter.

The pole at \(n=-1\) in the recurrence matrix is likewise a clock/gauge singularity. It is not one of the geometric singular fibers of the Picard--Fuchs family.

## 7. Coefficient zeros are not supersingular moduli points

Let

\[
A_p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b_jt^j.
\]

There are two different zero loci:

- evaluation roots \(t\in\overline{\mathbf F}_p\) for which \(A_p(t)=0\);
- coefficient zeros \(j\in\mathbf Z/(p-1)\) for which \(b_j=0\).

The set \(Z_p\) is the second object. It is a finite-Mellin zero set. The repository's toric identity realizes \(b_j\) as a character-twisted Frobenius trace/Hasse coordinate. It does not make \(j/p\) into a geometric fiber parameter.

Therefore \(r_p\) is safely described as:

> the smallest positive representative of a character exponent whose marked Frobenius object is nonordinary.

It is not safely described as:

> the smallest supersingular point on the Apéry moduli curve.

The word “smallest” itself depends on the chosen representatives \(1,2,\ldots,p-2\). It is an archimedean ordering imposed on a cyclic character group, not an algebraic invariant of that group.

## 8. Correct random-hitting heuristic

### 8.1 Fixed target versus collision

For a random sequence of states in a set of size \(p\):

- the first repeated state occurs after about \(\sqrt p\) samples (birthday paradox);
- the first visit to one specified state occurs after about \(p\) samples.

Pollard rho uses the first phenomenon. The event \(b_r=0\), or

\[
\Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1],
\]

is the second phenomenon.

Thus \(r_p\) of order \(p\) is exactly the scale expected from mixing in \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\), whose size is \(p+1\).

There is no implication that the orbit “covers all residues before hitting zero.” A random orbit of length \(p\) visits only about \((1-e^{-1})p\) distinct states, yet its first specified-target hit is still of order \(p\).

### 8.2 Reflection gives intensity \(1/2\)

The Apéry reflection law is

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Ignoring the rare central fixed point, zeros occur in reflected pairs. Define the lower-half point process

\[
\Xi_p
=\sum_{\substack{0\le r<(p-1)/2\\b_r\equiv0\pmod p}}
 \delta_{(2r+1)/p}
\quad\text{on }[0,1].
\tag{8.2}
\]

There are about \(p/2\) candidate sites. If each has zero probability \(1/p\), the expected number of points is \(1/2\). The natural model is therefore

\[
\Xi_p\Longrightarrow \operatorname{PPP}\!\left(\tfrac12\,dx\right).
\tag{8.3}
\]

It predicts

\[
K_p:=\frac{z_p}{2}\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)
\tag{8.4}
\]

and

\[
\Pr(Z_p=\varnothing)\longrightarrow e^{-1/2}=0.606530\ldots.
\tag{8.5}
\]

This matches the repository's large scan extremely well: the zero-pair histogram is close to Poisson\((1/2)\), and about \(60.7\%\) of primes have no zero.

### 8.3 The first-zero distribution is not uniform in the full nonempty family

Let

\[
\alpha_p=\frac{2r_p+1}{p}
\]

for primes with \(Z_p\ne\varnothing\). Under `(8.3)`,

\[
\Pr(\alpha_p>t)=e^{-t/2}
\]

before conditioning on nonemptiness. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\Pr(\alpha_p\le t\mid Z_p\ne\varnothing)
=\frac{1-e^{-t/2}}{1-e^{-1/2}}.}
\tag{8.6}
\]

The density is

\[
f(t)=\frac{\tfrac12e^{-t/2}}{1-e^{-1/2}},
\qquad 0<t<1.
\tag{8.7}
\]

This law is mildly biased toward the left. Its mean is about \(0.4585\), rather than \(1/2\).

For the Weyl convention \(e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}\), its conditional Fourier coefficient is

\[
\int_0^1 e(ht)f(t)\,dt
=\frac{1/2}{1/2-2\pi i h},
\qquad h\in\mathbf Z\setminus\{0\},
\tag{8.8}
\]

which is nonzero. Thus the all-nonempty-prime Poisson model does **not** predict Weyl sums tending to zero.

### 8.4 Why a uniform histogram may nevertheless appear

Condition on exactly one lower-half point, equivalently \(z_p=2\). A Poisson point process conditioned to contain exactly one point has a uniform point location. Therefore

\[
\alpha_p\mid(z_p=2)
\quad\text{is predicted to be uniform on }[0,1].
\tag{8.9}
\]

Moreover, among nonempty Poisson\((1/2)\) samples,

\[
\Pr(K_p=1\mid K_p\ge1)
=\frac{(1/2)e^{-1/2}}{1-e^{-1/2}}
\approx0.7707.
\]

So roughly \(77\%\) of the nonempty samples are doublets. The minimum distribution can look nearly uniform at moderate ranges even though the limiting Poisson-minimum law is not exactly uniform.

The computation should therefore separate:

1. primes with \(z_p=2\);
2. all primes with \(z_p>0\);
3. primes with \(z_p\ge4\);
4. the rare central-zero primes.

## 9. What Weyl sum should be proved?

If the intended claim is uniform distribution of

\[
\alpha_p=\frac{2r_p+1}{p},
\]

then Weyl's criterion asks, for every fixed nonzero integer \(h\),

\[
\sum_{\substack{p\le X\\Z_p\ne\varnothing}}
 e\!\left(h\frac{2r_p+1}{p}\right)
=o\!\left(\#\{p\le X:Z_p\ne\varnothing\}\right).
\tag{9.1}
\]

If \(\omega_p=2r_p+1\), the normalization must contain \(1/p\). A sum of the form

\[
\sum_p e(\theta\omega_p/2)
\]

without \(p\) in the denominator is not the Weyl sum for \(\alpha_p\); it measures a different integer-phase statistic.

For the doublet family, the clean target is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{\substack{p\le X\\z_p=2}}
 e\!\left(h\frac{2r_p+1}{p}\right)
=o\!\left(\#\{p\le X:z_p=2\}\right).}
\tag{9.2}
\]

For all nonempty primes, the Poisson prediction is instead

\[
\frac{
\sum_{p\le X,\,Z_p\ne\varnothing}e(h\alpha_p)
}{
\#\{p\le X:Z_p\ne\varnothing\}
}
\longrightarrow
\frac{1/2}{1/2-2\pi ih}.
\tag{9.3}
\]

Equation `(9.3)` is a more discriminating empirical test than a histogram.

## 10. What a transfer-matrix proof would actually require

The transfer formulation converts a zero indicator into

\[
1_{Z_p}(r)
=1_{\{\Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1]\}}.
\]

A first spectral target would be an incomplete cocycle-incidence estimate such as

\[
\sum_{r\in I}
 e_p(hr)
 \left(
 1_{\{\Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1]\}}-\frac1{p+1}
 \right)
\tag{10.1}
\]

for intervals \(I\subset[1,(p-1)/2]\).

But the main term in a full interval is only \(O(1)\). A conventional Deligne or expander error of size \(O(\sqrt p)\) is vastly too large. This is a **shrinking-target problem at scale \(1/p\)**, not ordinary equidistribution against a fixed positive-density set.

Equivalently, additive orthogonality gives

\[
1_{b_r=0}
=\frac1p\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(tb_r),
\]

so `(10.1)` leads to two-variable sums involving

\[
\sum_{r,t}e_p(hr+tb_r).
\tag{10.2}
\]

The repository has deliberately not claimed that \(r\mapsto b_r\) is a bounded-conductor trace function on an additive \(r\)-line. Its interpolation degree and finite-prefix linear complexity grow with \(p\). Thus standard one-variable trace-function technology does not presently supply `(10.2)` at the required rare-event scale.

### 10.1 Why group generation is insufficient

Even if the set of matrices \(M(1),M(2),\ldots\) generates \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\), a spectral gap for the associated Cayley graph controls a **random word** in the generators. The Apéry word

\[
M(r-1)\cdots M(1)
\]

is one highly structured deterministic word. Generation excludes confinement to a proper subgroup, but it does not prove that the prefixes equidistribute.

A genuinely useful nonstationary expansion theorem would have to bound operator sums such as

\[
\left\|
\sum_{r\in I}
\rho\bigl(M(r-1)\cdots M(1)\bigr)
\right\|
\tag{10.3}
\]

for every relevant nontrivial representation \(\rho\), with strength sufficient for a target of density \(1/p\). No such theorem is presently available for this Apéry cocycle.

### 10.2 Autonomous orbit theorems do not directly apply

Results of Chang--D'Andrea--Ostafe--Shparlinski--Sombra and related work concern iterates of a fixed polynomial/rational dynamical system or parametrized autonomous systems. Here the step map changes with the clock.

One may augment the state and write a skew product

\[
(n,x)\longmapsto(n+1,F_n(x)),
\]

but the clock translation traverses every residue once, the map has a singular divisor, and the degree growth/iterate structure used by autonomous results is absent or degenerate. This reformulation is exact, but it does not turn existing orbit-length theorems into a Poisson-scale hitting theorem.

## 11. The Frobenius/Mellin route and its barrier

The correct spectral family `(5.1)` suggests a different program. Let

\[
\mathcal V_{p,j}
\]

be the marked character-\(j\) object. Then \(j\in Z_p\) is a same-characteristic nonordinarity condition.

A proof of the point-process law would require uniform information on the collection

\[
\{\mathcal V_{p,j}:1\le j\le(p-2)/2\}
\]

as both \(j\) and \(p\) vary.

Ordinary \(\ell\)-adic equidistribution or Rankin--Selberg moments control complex/\(\ell\)-adic trace distributions for fixed test functions. They do not directly count the event

\[
a_{p,j}\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak p},
\]

where the residue characteristic is the same \(p\) defining the family. The target has probability about \(1/p\), so it shrinks with \(p\). A square-root equidistribution error again exceeds the expected count.

The missing input is therefore a **horizontal \(p\)-adic nonordinarity theorem**, preferably with joint control at several character indices. For example, for distinct normalized intervals \(I_1,\ldots,I_m\) and nonnegative integers \(k_1,\ldots,k_m\), one would want

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}
\sum_{p\le X}
\prod_{a=1}^m
\bigl(\Xi_p(I_a)\bigr)_{k_a}
\longrightarrow
\prod_{a=1}^m
\left(\frac{|I_a|}{2}\right)^{k_a}.
\tag{11.1}
\]

This is the factorial-moment criterion for

\[
\Xi_p\Longrightarrow\operatorname{PPP}(\tfrac12 dx).
\]

It would simultaneously prove:

- the Poisson\((1/2)\) zero-pair law;
- the void probability \(e^{-t/2}\);
- the first-zero distribution `(8.6)`;
- uniformity conditional on \(z_p=2\);
- the corresponding Weyl limits.

## 12. Why the gap-polynomial route is currently more concrete

For two zeros separated by \(h\), the recurrence gives a gap polynomial condition

\[
p\mid b_r,\ b_{r+h}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
N_h(r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{12.1}
\]

The repository has developed:

- exact continuant/gap polynomials;
- degree and height control;
- reflection;
- pairwise coprimality/resultant machinery;
- empirical Poisson zero-pair statistics.

These are precisely the ingredients relevant to the factorial moments in `(11.1)`. A \(k\)-tuple of lower-half zeros imposes \(k-1\) gap-polynomial conditions. Averaging those simultaneous conditions over primes is a direct route toward a Poisson point-process theorem.

This route still needs a uniform prime-aspect result for growing gaps. It is not complete. But it attacks the actual rare-event statistic, unlike eigenvalue orders of the coefficient-transfer prefixes.

A sensible hierarchy is:

1. **One-point intensity:** prove an average asymptotic for
   
   \[
   \sum_{p\le X}\Xi_p(I).
   \]

2. **Two-point factorial moment:** prove the correct average for
   
   \[
   \sum_{p\le X}(\Xi_p(I))_2.
   \]

3. **Higher factorial moments / tail:** control simultaneous roots through multiple gap polynomials.

4. **First-zero law:** derive void probabilities by inclusion--exclusion or Poisson convergence.

5. **Prime-aspect Weyl sums:** apply the resulting limiting point-process law.

## 13. Direct answers to the five questions

### Question 1: Is \(r_p\) related to a group order?

**Not in the Pisano sense.** It is the first time a noncommutative prefix cocycle enters a coset of a point stabilizer in \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\). There is no fixed element whose powers are the partial products. In a hypothetical everywhere-invertible periodic difference equation, a full-period monodromy order would control repetition across periods, but not the initial within-period hit. The Apéry recurrence also has a singular period boundary.

The global modulo-\(p\) analogue is the Lucas digit automaton, not a Pisano period.

### Question 2: Is there a spectral interpretation through \(P(p)\) or partial-product eigenvalues?

**Not through the proposed \(P(p)\).** It is undefined in the rational gauge and is not crystalline Frobenius after denominator clearing. Prefix eigenvalues are gauge-dependent and insufficient to determine the hit.

**Yes through the marked Frobenius family:** \(r_p\) is the first character exponent at which the rank-three marked object loses its slope-zero unit root.

### Question 3: Is \(r_p\) controlled by the nearest ODE singularity modulo \(p\)?

**No known or invariant relation.** The quoted singularity locations are not those of the standard Apéry coordinate, there is no canonical finite-field notion of nearest, and the coefficient index is not the moduli variable. Singularities may determine monodromy and hence global statistics, not the least zero location.

### Question 4: Is \(r_p\) the smallest supersingular K3 point?

**No.** The safe statement is “smallest nonordinary character twist.” Coefficient zeros and evaluation/moduli roots are different. Loss of a unit root is weaker than supersingularity, and \(r/p\) is a character angle, not a K3 moduli point.

### Question 5: Can dynamics prove equidistribution or Weyl cancellation?

The transfer dynamics gives the right incidence formulation but no existing theorem proves the needed shrinking-target estimate. Also, \(r_p\asymp p\) is consistent with mixing. Before targeting uniformity, distinguish the doublet family from all nonempty zero sets:

- \(z_p=2\): uniform \(\alpha_p\) is the Poisson prediction;
- \(z_p>0\) with \(r_p\) the minimum: the prediction is the truncated exponential `(8.6)`, not uniform.

A proof should target the factorial-moment/Poisson point-process statement `(11.1)`, likely using gap polynomials plus a new horizontal same-characteristic Frobenius dispersion theorem.

# Final conclusion

The transfer matrix supplies a clean and useful statement:

\[
r_p=\min\{r:\Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1]\}.
\]

But this is a first entrance into a stabilizer coset by a deterministic nonautonomous word, not an order problem. The naive period product is singular/undefined and is not Dwork Frobenius. The correct spectral interpretation is instead that \(r_p\) is the first nonordinary **character twist** in the marked rank-three Frobenius family.

That interpretation is conceptually valuable, but proving the distribution of \(r_p\) requires a rare-event theorem at scale \(1/p\). The most natural target is Poisson convergence of the lower-half zero process. It predicts uniformity for the doublet subfamily, a truncated-exponential minimum law for all nonempty primes, and explicit nonzero limiting Fourier coefficients in the latter case. A proof would need the project's gap-polynomial machinery together with a genuinely horizontal, same-characteristic Frobenius/dispersion estimate; neither matrix orders nor ordinary spectral equidistribution presently supplies it.
