# Q507: Barban–Davenport–Halberstam for Apéry zero sets

## Executive summary

The BDH analogy is useful, but the Apéry problem is a **moving-target sparse progression problem**, not a direct instance of BDH.  The main points are:

1. For fixed residues \(Z_p\), the functions
\[
f_p(n)=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}
\]
are periodic sparse functions and their Fourier expansions are explicit.

2. A second-moment BDH estimate can be adapted formally, but the usual large-sieve/BDH mechanism does not automatically give the required third factorial moment.

3. The sparsity \(|Z_p|=O(1)\) helps with diagonal terms but makes off-diagonal cancellation the central issue.

4. The natural replacement for higher BDH is a higher-order dispersion theorem or a multilinear large-sieve estimate. No standard theorem gives this for the Apéry moving sets.

---

## (A) Does BDH apply to varying sets \(Z_p\)?

The classical BDH theorem studies a fixed arithmetic function \(f(n)\):

\[
\sum_{q\le Q}\sum_{a\bmod q}
\left|\sum_{n\le x,n\equiv a(q)}f(n)-\frac{x}{q}\sum_{n\le x}f(n)\right|^2.
\]

The Apéry function is different. For each prime \(p\), define

\[
f_p(n)=\sum_{r\in Z_p}1_{n\equiv r\pmod p}.
\]

The modulus and the residue set vary simultaneously:

\[
p\mapsto Z_p.
\]

Therefore this is not a direct BDH application.

A formal analogue would be

\[
E_2(N,Q)=\sum_{p\le Q}
\left|\sum_{n\le N}f_p(n)-\frac{N|Z_p|}{p}\right|^2.
\]

This is a moving-modulus variance problem.

The missing input is not the mean value of \(f_p\), which is trivial:

\[
\sum_{n\le N}f_p(n)=\frac{N|Z_p|}{p}+O(|Z_p|),
\]

but correlations between the different Frobenius-defined sets \(Z_p\).

---

## (B) Fourier expansion of the Apéry zero indicator

Write

\[
1_{Z_p}(r)=\frac1p\sum_{h\bmod p}
\widehat{Z_p}(h)e_p(hr),
\]

where

\[
\widehat{Z_p}(h)=\sum_{a\in Z_p}e_p(-ha).
\]

Hence

\[
f_p(n)=\frac1p\sum_h\widehat{Z_p}(h)e_p(hn).
\]

The zero frequency is

\[
\widehat{Z_p}(0)=|Z_p|.
\]

The nonzero frequencies are the important terms:

\[
\widehat{Z_p}(h)=\sum_{a\in Z_p}e_p(-ha).
\]

Since \(|Z_p|=O(1)\), the trivial estimate gives

\[
|\widehat{Z_p}(h)|\le |Z_p|=O(1).
\]

This is much stronger than a dense residue set. However, it is not enough: there are about \(p\) frequencies.

A useful Parseval identity is

\[
\sum_{h\bmod p}|\widehat{Z_p}(h)|^2
=p|Z_p|.
\]

Thus the total Fourier mass is only \(O(p)\), but individual cancellation requires additional arithmetic information about the positions of the zeros.

The palindromic relation

\[
Z_p=p-1-Z_p
\]

imposes a phase constraint on \(\widehat{Z_p}\), but does not by itself give square-root cancellation.

---

## (C) Does sparsity help or hurt?

It does both.

### Advantage: diagonal terms are tiny

For a random sparse set,

\[
\|f_p\|_2^2\approx N\frac{|Z_p|}{p}.
\]

Because

\[
|Z_p|=O(1),
\]

the expected contribution is only

\[
O(N/p).
\]

This is much smaller than the dense case.

### Disadvantage: no averaging inside one modulus

A classical BDH proof gains cancellation by averaging over many residue classes:

\[
\sum_a |E(a,q)|^2.
\]

Here there are only a few active classes:

\[
|Z_p|=0,1,2,3.
\]

Therefore the internal averaging mechanism disappears.

The problem moves from

"many residue classes per modulus"

to

"many moduli with one or two special residues each".

This is closer to a sparse sieve with moving local conditions.

---

## (D) From BDH second moment to the third factorial moment

The target is

\[
F_3(X)=\sum_{m<X^2}(K_X(m))_3,
\]

where

\[
K_X(m)=\sum_{p\in P_X}f_p(m).
\]

Expanding gives

\[
F_3(X)=
\sum_{p_1,p_2,p_3}^{\neq}
\sum_{m<X^2}
 f_{p_1}(m)f_{p_2}(m)f_{p_3}(m).
\]

The second moment only controls

\[
\sum_m\left(\sum_pf_p(m)\right)^2.
\]

It does not control triple intersections:

\[
\sum_m f_{p_1}(m)f_{p_2}(m)f_{p_3}(m).
\]

A genuine third-order analogue would require estimates for sums like

\[
\sum_m
\prod_{i=1}^3 f_{p_i}(m).
\]

Expanding Fourier gives three-frequency sums:

\[
\sum_{h_1,h_2,h_3}
\widehat Z_{p_1}(h_1)\widehat Z_{p_2}(h_2)\widehat Z_{p_3}(h_3)
\sum_{m<X^2}e\left(m\sum_i h_i/p_i\right).
\]

The final exponential sum is controlled unless the CRT frequency is close to zero. The hard part is bounding the weighted Fourier mass of the three prime factors.

---

## Is there a higher-moment BDH?

There are analogues:

- higher moment large sieve inequalities,
- multilinear large sieve estimates,
- Vinogradov mean value type bounds,
- dispersion method estimates.

But none applies automatically because the coefficients

\[
\widehat Z_p(h)
\]

come from a different algebraic object for every prime.

A hypothetical theorem sufficient for \((HM)_3\) would be:

\[
\sum_{m<X^2}
\left|\sum_{p\in P_X}f_p(m)-\lambda_X\right|^3
\ll X^2\lambda_X^3.
\]

Equivalently, one needs Poisson-scale control of the third factorial moment.

---

## Relation to trace functions

For classical BDH, the Fourier coefficients are fixed arithmetic objects. Here

\[
\widehat Z_p(h)
=\sum_{r:b_r\equiv0(p)}e_p(hr)
\]

is itself a Frobenius-zero-set Fourier transform.

A successful proof likely needs one of:

1. an \(\ell\)-adic sheaf controlling the zero-set transform;
2. a Katz-style bounded-conductor trace-function estimate;
3. a direct Apéry-specific rigidity theorem.

The existing computation \(|Z_p|=O(1)\) is already stronger than generic trace-function behavior, so the missing theorem is not ordinary BDH but a specialized moving-target BDH.

---

## Final answer

(A) Standard BDH does not directly apply, because \(Z_p\) varies with \(p\). A BDH-type statement can be formulated, but it is a new moving-target variance theorem.

(B) Fourier expansion reduces the problem to exponential sums involving
\(\widehat Z_p(h)\). Sparsity gives small total Fourier mass but not enough cancellation by itself.

(C) \(|Z_p|=O(1)\) helps the diagonal and hurts averaging. The problem is sparse rather than dense.

(D) The third factorial moment requires a genuinely higher-order BDH/dispersion estimate. The natural object is a multilinear Fourier estimate over three independent Apéry zero-set transforms.

The correct viewpoint is therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{BDH is the second-moment shadow; }(HM)_3\text{ requires a third-order moving-target dispersion theorem.}
}
\]
