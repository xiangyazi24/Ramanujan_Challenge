# Explicit conductor calculation for the Apéry trace map

## Summary

The proposed route does not give a bounded conductor. The key distinction is:

- the Mellin sheaf M may have bounded geometric conductor as a sheaf on the parameter space;
- the function j ↦ b_j = Tr(Frob_j | M) is a trace function;
- the Artin–Schreier pullback attached to the scalar function b(j), namely [cb]^*L_ψ, has conductor controlled by the ramification of the morphism b: A¹→A¹.

A tame rank-2 sheaf does not imply that ψ∘Tr(M) has bounded conductor.

The obstruction is the nonlinear exponential composition.

---

## 1. Generic polynomial degree calculation

If b(j) is viewed as an ordinary polynomial over F_p of degree d, then the Artin–Schreier sheaf

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(cb)}=[cb]^*\mathcal L_\psi
\]

has Swan conductor at infinity approximately

\[
\operatorname{Swan}_\infty(\mathcal L_{\psi(cb)})=d
\]

(up to the usual exceptional cancellation cases).

Thus if d=p-1,

\[
\operatorname{Swan}_\infty\sim p.
\]

Deligne then gives only

\[
|S_c|\ll p^{3/2},
\]

which is weaker than the trivial bound.

This part is correct.

---

## 2. Reflection symmetry reduces degree, but not enough

The symmetry

\[
b_j=b_{p-1-j}
\]

implies invariance under

\[
j\mapsto -1-j.
\]

After shifting

\[
x=j+1/2,
\]

one gets an even polynomial:

\[
b(j)=B(x^2).
\]

Therefore the effective degree drops by roughly a factor of two:

\[
\deg(B)\approx (p-1)/2.
\]

Consequently

\[
\operatorname{Swan}_\infty\approx p/2.
\]

This is a genuine improvement, but still linear in p.

Reflection symmetry alone cannot give square-root cancellation.

---

## 3. The recurrence does not imply bounded conductor

The fact that b_j satisfies a second-order recurrence is a statement about the family of values.

It does not mean that the interpolation polynomial b(x) has bounded degree or bounded ramification.

A basic analogy:

A solution of a differential equation with small rank can have arbitrarily high-degree specializations over finite fields. The differential equation controls the sheaf before applying a highly ramified operation; it does not automatically control the Artin–Schreier pullback of the resulting trace function.

The operation

\[
M\longmapsto \psi(\operatorname{Tr}(M))
\]

is nonlinear.

It is not a tensor construction of bounded rank.

---

## 4. Does tame Mellin monodromy imply tame trace map?

No.

Suppose

\[
M
\]

is lisse and tame. Its trace function is

\[
t_M(x)=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_x|M).
\]

The sheaf controlling

\[
\psi(c\,t_M(x))
\]

is not M itself.

It is the Artin–Schreier sheaf

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(c t_M)}.
\]

Its ramification depends on the poles and degree of the scalar function t_M.

A tame sheaf can have a trace function whose exponential pullback is wildly ramified.

Therefore:

\[
\operatorname{Swan}(M)=0
\not\Rightarrow
\operatorname{Swan}(\psi\circ\operatorname{Tr}M)=O(1).
\]

---

## 5. Why the Picard–Fuchs viewpoint does not immediately solve it

The Apéry recurrence comes from a rank-two Picard–Fuchs differential equation.

Geometrically, this gives a rank-two local system with finitely many singular points.

However the desired sum is over finite-field Frobenius traces:

\[
S_c=\sum_j \psi(c\,t_M(j)).
\]

The required sheaf is a new exponential composition.

One would need a theorem of the form:

> For a bounded-conductor lisse sheaf M, the exponential trace sheaf ψ∘Tr(M) also has bounded conductor.

This statement is false in general.

The conductor can grow with p because the trace map itself can have growing degree.

---

## 6. Relation with Katz theory

Katz's Kloosterman sheaf works because the phase is a geometric function:

\[
u\mapsto a\nu+a/\nu.
\]

The variable ν is an actual algebraic coordinate on G_m.

Hence

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(a\nu+a/\nu)}
\]

has controlled ramification.

For Apéry,

\[
b_j=\alpha_j+\beta_j
\]

where α_j,β_j are Frobenius eigenvalues.

The eigenvalue α_j is not an algebraic function of j.

There is no algebraic map

\[
j\mapsto \alpha_j
\]

whose pullback would produce a Kloosterman phase.

The spectral plane picture is therefore only an analogy, not a construction of a Kloosterman sheaf.

---

## 7. Does Sym² help?

The identity

\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Sym}^2M)=\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2
\]

and

\[
= b_j^2-p^w
\]

is useful because it gives an algebraic trace function.

However the original exponential is

\[
\psi(c b_j),
\]

not

\[
\psi(c(b_j^2-p^w)).
\]

Passing to Sym² changes the problem.

It may help for moments such as

\[
\sum_j b_j^2
\]

or quadratic exponential sums, but it does not linearize ψ(c b_j).

---

# What would actually prove |S_c|=O(√p)?

One needs one of the following stronger structures:

1. An algebraic parametrization of the Frobenius eigenvalue:

\[
j\mapsto \alpha_j
\]

with bounded conductor.

This would genuinely reduce to a Kloosterman-type sum.

2. A special theorem for the Apéry family showing that

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(c\operatorname{Tr}M)}
\]

has bounded conductor.

No general theorem gives this.

3. A different trace formula, avoiding the exponential pullback, for example using moments or the geometry of the zero locus.

---

## Final answer

The conductor calculation gives:

- generic degree p-1: Swan ≈ p;
- reflection symmetry: Swan ≈ p/2;
- recurrence/Picard–Fuchs structure: does not by itself reduce Swan to O(1);
- Mellin sheaf tameness: does not imply exponential trace tameness.

Therefore the hoped-for implication

\[
\operatorname{Swan}(M)=O(1)
\Longrightarrow
|\sum_j\psi(cb_j)|\ll\sqrt p
\]

is false in general.

The missing theorem is not a conductor calculation of M. It is a new bounded-conductor construction for the nonlinear exponential trace map ψ∘Tr(M). That is exactly the hard step.
