# Direct/reflected parametrization audit

Let \(n=qp+r\), \(0\le r<p\), and set \(j=\min(r,p-1-r)\). The Apéry reflection law \(b_r\equiv b_{p-1-r}\pmod p\) gives a canonical representative \(j\in[0,(p-1)/2]\) with
\[
p\mid b_r \iff p\mid b_j.
\]

For fixed \(q,n\), the possible primes are exactly described by two affine maps.

## 1. Direct branch

If \(r=j\), then
\[
n=qp+j,
\]
so
\[
 p=\frac{n-j}{q}.
\]
Therefore a direct hit is a value of \(j\) such that
\[
 n-j\equiv0\pmod q,
\qquad (n-j)/q\text{ is prime},
\qquad b_j\equiv0\pmod{(n-j)/q}.
\]

The range is \(0\le j\le (p-1)/2\), with the additional condition \(p>\sqrt n\) in the top-window application.

## 2. Reflected branch

If \(r=p-1-j\), then
\[
n=qp+p-1-j=(q+1)p-(j+1).
\]
Hence
\[
p=\frac{n+1+j}{q+1}.
\]
A reflected hit is therefore a \(j\) satisfying
\[
n+1+j\equiv0\pmod{q+1},
\qquad (n+1+j)/(q+1)\text{ is prime},
\qquad b_j\equiv0\pmod{(n+1+j)/(q+1)}.
\]

The endpoint \(j=(p-1)/2\) is the overlap point of direct and reflected descriptions when \(p\) is odd and \(r=(p-1)/2\); otherwise the two branches are disjoint.

Thus every hit prime corresponds injectively to a pair
\[
(j,\varepsilon),\quad \varepsilon\in\{D,R\},
\]
where the associated prime is one of the two linear functions
\[
L_D(j)=\frac{n-j}{q},\qquad L_R(j)=\frac{n+1+j}{q+1}.
\]

## Double hits

If both branches occur for the same \(j\), then both primes divide the same integer \(b_j\), so
\[
L_D(j)L_R(j)\mid b_j.
\]
The relation between the two possible primes is
\[
q(q+1)L_D(j)+(q)L_R(j)= (q+1)n+q,
\]
up to the obvious rearrangement, so they lie on a fixed affine line. This is the only available coupling.

However, this does not by itself make double hits sparse. The reason is that \(b_j\) is enormous:
\[
\log b_j=\Theta(j),
\]
and for \(j\asymp n\) it has enough arithmetic height to contain two unrelated linear factors. There is no contradiction with having many \(j\) carrying both factors.

## Aggregate certificate attempt

The natural certificate would be
\[
\prod_{j\le n/(2q+1)}\gcd\left(b_j,L_D(j)L_R(j)\right).
\]
If this product had logarithm \(o(n)\), the fixed-q radical would follow.

A resultant/content argument would require a fixed polynomial identity connecting the sequence \(b_j\) to the linear factors \(L_D,L_R\). No such identity is known. The Apéry sequence is holonomic, but the index-dependent evaluation points are changing with \(n\), so standard resultant bounds for fixed polynomial sequences do not apply.

## Concrete obstruction

The obstruction is saturation by the individual factor sizes. A single \(j\) can contribute a prime of size ≈\(n\), while the known information only says this prime divides \(b_j\). Since
\[
\log b_j\asymp j,
\]
there is no height contradiction preventing linearly many such contributions. The affine relation between the two branches controls double hits only after a new input on prime divisors of \(b_j\) is supplied.

## Sharp remaining lemma

A sufficient new statement is:

**Affine two-factor sparsity lemma.** For fixed \(q\), the number of indices
\[
j\le n/(2q+1)
\]
for which both \(L_D(j)\) and \(L_R(j)\) are prime divisors of \(b_j\), weighted by
\[
\log(L_D(j)L_R(j)),
\]
is \(o(n).

Even the weaker one-factor statement
\[
\sum_j \log \gcd(b_j,L_D(j)L_R(j))=o(n)
\]
would solve the fixed-q problem. Presently neither follows from holonomicity, and the affine relation alone is insufficient.
