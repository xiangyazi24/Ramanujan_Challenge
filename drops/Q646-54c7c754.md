# Zero-pair Poisson\((1/2)\) for Apéry zeros: exact null model, finite-\(p\) correction, and Chebotarev interpretation

## Executive verdict

The proposed derivation has the **correct leading parameter** but needs three important corrections.

1. The representation
   \[
   b_r\equiv Q_p\bigl(r(p-1-r)\bigr)\pmod p
   \]
   is valid, with a unique interpolating polynomial of degree **at most** \((p-1)/2\). It is essentially a reformulation of the reflection symmetry
   \(b_r\equiv b_{p-1-r}\pmod p\); by itself it supplies no genericity.
2. For the natural random model of a reflection-symmetric value vector, the zero-pair count has an **exact finite-field binomial law**:
   \[
   K_p\sim \operatorname{Binomial}\!\left(\frac{p-3}{2},\frac1p\right),
   \]
   after respecting the deterministic endpoint pair \(b_0=b_{p-1}=1\). Hence
   \[
   K_p\Longrightarrow \operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).
   \]
   There is no problem caused by \(d/p\to1/2\). On the relevant quotient set, the random evaluations are in fact exactly independent.
3. This is a rigorous theorem about the **random null model**, not yet a theorem about the deterministic Apéry sequence. For Apéry, one would have to prove fixed-order joint equidistribution of the zero events as \(p\) varies. That is precisely the missing arithmetic input.

The clean formula is

```text
m = (p-1)/2
K_p = number of noncentral zero-pairs
I_p = indicator of the central zero r=(p-1)/2

random reflection-symmetric null model:
    K_p ~ Binomial((p-3)/2, 1/p)
    I_p ~ Bernoulli(1/p), independently
    Z_p = 2 K_p + I_p

therefore:
    K_p  => Poisson(1/2)
    Z_p  => 2 * Poisson(1/2)
```

Thus \(Z_p\) has limiting mean \(1\) and variance \(2\), while the pair count has limiting mean and variance \(1/2\). This exactly explains the observed even support and the empirical relation \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_p)\approx2\operatorname{E}(Z_p)\).

# 1. The reflection quotient and the polynomial \(Q_p\)

Let \(p\) be odd and put

\[
N=p-1,
\qquad
m=\frac{p-1}{2}.
\]

The banked reflection identity in the repository is

\[
b_{N-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p,
\qquad 0\le r\le N.
\tag{1.1}
\]

Over \(\mathbf F_p\), the involution is

\[
\iota(r)=N-r=-1-r.
\]

Define the invariant coordinate

\[
u(r)=r(N-r)=-r(r+1).
\tag{1.2}
\]

Then

\[
u(r)-u(s)=-(r-s)(r+s+1),
\]

so

\[
u(r)=u(s)
\iff
s=r\ \text{or}\ s=-1-r.
\tag{1.3}
\]

Thus the fibers of \(u\) are exactly the reflection orbits.

Solving \(u=-r(r+1)\) for \(r\) gives

\[
r^2+r+u=0,
\]

with discriminant

\[
\Delta(u)=1-4u.
\tag{1.4}
\]

Consequently the image is

\[
S_p=u(\mathbf F_p)
=
\{u\in\mathbf F_p:\Delta(u)\text{ is a square, possibly }0\}.
\tag{1.5}
\]

There are

\[
|S_p|=\frac{p+1}{2}=m+1
\tag{1.6}
\]

such values. More precisely:

- \((p-1)/2=m\) values have \(\Delta(u)\) a nonzero square and correspond to two-element reflection orbits;
- the single value
  \[
  u_*=\frac14
  \]
  has \(\Delta=0\) and corresponds to the fixed point
  \[
  r_*=\frac{p-1}{2};
  \]
- \(u=0\) corresponds to the endpoint pair \(\{0,p-1\}\).

Because \(r\mapsto b_r\bmod p\) is constant on every fiber of \(u\), interpolation on the \(m+1\) points of \(S_p\) gives a unique polynomial

\[
Q_p(U)\in\mathbf F_p[U],
\qquad
\deg Q_p\le m,
\tag{1.7}
\]

such that

\[
\boxed{
 b_r\equiv Q_p\bigl(r(p-1-r)\bigr)\pmod p
}
\qquad(0\le r\le p-1).
\tag{1.8}
\]

Two cautions are essential.

First, the degree is **at most** \(m\), not automatically exactly \(m\).

Second, the values of \(Q_p\) on \(\mathbf F_p\setminus S_p\) are merely the extrapolated values of the interpolation polynomial. A root there does not correspond to any Apéry coefficient. Therefore it is conceptually cleaner to study zeros of \(Q_p\) directly on \(S_p\), rather than first count all \(\mathbf F_p\)-roots and then apply a quadratic-residue filter.

The existence of \(Q_p\) is also not special to the Apéry recurrence: **every** function satisfying the reflection symmetry can be represented in this way. All arithmetic content is therefore in how the particular values of \(Q_p\) are distributed, not in the existence of the quotient polynomial.

# 2. Exact random null model

The natural random model is not “a mysterious generic Apéry recurrence.” It is simply a uniformly random reflection-symmetric value vector, subject to the deterministic endpoint value.

Equivalently, take \(Q\) uniformly from the affine space

\[
\mathcal A_p
=
\{Q\in\mathbf F_p[U]:\deg Q\le m,\ Q(0)=1\}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

The condition \(Q(0)=1\) respects

\[
b_0=b_{p-1}=1.
\tag{2.2}
\]

The affine space \(\mathcal A_p\) has dimension \(m\). Consider the evaluation map

\[
\operatorname{ev}:
\mathcal A_p
\longrightarrow
\mathbf F_p^{S_p\setminus\{0\}},
\qquad
Q\longmapsto (Q(u))_{u\in S_p\setminus\{0\}}.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Both sides have \(p^m\) elements. The map is injective: if \(Q_1,Q_2\in\mathcal A_p\) agree on \(S_p\setminus\{0\}\), then \(Q_1-Q_2\) also vanishes at \(0\), hence at all \(m+1\) points of \(S_p\), while having degree at most \(m\). Therefore \(Q_1=Q_2\).

Hence (2.3) is an affine bijection.

This proves the strongest possible independence statement:

\[
\boxed{
\{Q(u):u\in S_p\setminus\{0\}\}
\text{ are exactly independent uniform }\mathbf F_p\text{-variables.}
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

No limiting argument is involved.

## 2.1 Pair sites and the central site

Let

\[
P_p
=
\{u\in\mathbf F_p:\Delta(u)\text{ is a nonzero square}\}
\setminus\{0\}.
\tag{2.5}
\]

These are the admissible noncentral reflection-pair sites, excluding the endpoint pair. Their number is

\[
|P_p|
=
\frac{p-1}{2}-1
=
\frac{p-3}{2}.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Define

\[
K_p=\#\{u\in P_p:Q(u)=0\}
\tag{2.7}
\]

and

\[
I_p=\mathbf 1_{Q(1/4)=0}.
\tag{2.8}
\]

By (2.4),

\[
\boxed{
K_p\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\!\left(\frac{p-3}{2},\frac1p\right),
\qquad
I_p\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/p),
}
\tag{2.9}
\]

and \(K_p,I_p\) are independent.

The corresponding number of zero indices is

\[
\boxed{Z_p=2K_p+I_p.}
\tag{2.10}
\]

The exact probability generating function is

\[
\mathbf E[s^{Z_p}]
=
\left(1-\frac1p+\frac{s}{p}\right)
\left(1-\frac1p+\frac{s^2}{p}\right)^{(p-3)/2}.
\tag{2.11}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\Pr(Z_p=2k)
=
\binom{(p-3)/2}{k}
\frac1{p^k}
\left(1-\frac1p\right)^{(p-3)/2-k+1},
\tag{2.12}
\]

and

\[
\Pr(Z_p=2k+1)
=
\binom{(p-3)/2}{k}
\frac1{p^{k+1}}
\left(1-\frac1p\right)^{(p-3)/2-k}.
\tag{2.13}
\]

The odd part has total probability exactly \(1/p\). Thus the central self-paired zero is a vanishingly rare correction to the even law.

## 2.2 Poisson limit

The finite-\(p\) pair parameter is

\[
\lambda_p
=
\mathbf E[K_p]
=
\frac{p-3}{2p}
=
\frac12-\frac{3}{2p}.
\tag{2.14}
\]

Since

\[
\frac{p-3}{2}\longrightarrow\infty,
\qquad
\frac1p\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
\frac{p-3}{2p}\longrightarrow\frac12,
\]

the law of small numbers gives

\[
\boxed{K_p\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).}
\tag{2.15}
\]

Explicitly, for each fixed \(k\),

\[
\Pr(K_p=k)
=
\binom{(p-3)/2}{k}
\frac1{p^k}
\left(1-\frac1p\right)^{(p-3)/2-k}
\longrightarrow
 e^{-1/2}\frac{(1/2)^k}{k!}.
\tag{2.16}
\]

A standard Le Cam estimate gives total-variation error \(O(1/p)\).

Because \(I_p\to0\) in probability,

\[
Z_p\Longrightarrow 2Y,
\qquad
Y\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).
\tag{2.17}
\]

The limiting generating function is

\[
\mathbf E[s^{2Y}]
=
\exp\!\left(\frac{s^2-1}{2}\right).
\tag{2.18}
\]

The exact first two moments in the endpoint-conditioned model are

\[
\mathbf E[K_p]=\frac{p-3}{2p},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(K_p)
=
\frac{p-3}{2p}\left(1-\frac1p\right),
\tag{2.19}
\]

and

\[
\mathbf E[Z_p]
=1-\frac2p,
\tag{2.20}
\]

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_p)
=
\frac{2p-5}{p}\left(1-\frac1p\right)
\longrightarrow2.
\tag{2.21}
\]

Thus the parameter \(1/2\) has the elementary explanation

\[
\boxed{
\text{about }p/2\text{ independent reflection orbits}
\times
\text{zero probability }1/p
=
1/2.
}
\tag{2.22}
\]

The quadratic-residue description is an equivalent encoding of the orbit quotient, but the orbit count is the more direct source of the factor \(1/2\).

# 3. Answer to (a): is the expected number of roots equal to one?

There are two different questions here.

## 3.1 A genuinely random polynomial

Let \(Q\) be uniformly random among polynomials of degree at most \(d\) over \(\mathbf F_p\). For every fixed \(a\in\mathbf F_p\), the evaluation map

\[
Q\longmapsto Q(a)
\]

is surjective and every value occurs equally often. Hence

\[
\Pr(Q(a)=0)=\frac1p.
\]

By linearity of expectation,

\[
\mathbf E\#\{a\in\mathbf F_p:Q(a)=0\}
=
\sum_{a\in\mathbf F_p}\frac1p
=1.
\tag{3.1}
\]

No independence between different evaluations is needed for this expectation.

The same conclusion holds for a random polynomial of exact positive degree \(d\), or a random monic polynomial of degree \(d\), because one of the remaining coefficients is still uniform and makes each evaluation uniform.

If one conditions on the Apéry endpoint constraint \(Q(0)=1\), then \(0\) can no longer be a root, while every nonzero evaluation remains uniform. Therefore

\[
\mathbf E\#\{a\in\mathbf F_p:Q(a)=0\}
=
\frac{p-1}{p},
\tag{3.2}
\]

not exactly \(1\).

## 3.2 The actual Apéry polynomial \(Q_p\)

For the actual Apéry sequence there is no probability space at a fixed prime. There is one deterministic polynomial \(Q_p\). Thus the expression

\[
\mathbf E[\#\text{roots of }Q_p]
\]

has no literal meaning until an ensemble is specified.

One can take primes \(p\) as the ensemble and postulate that the quotient-value vectors behave like uniform random vectors. But that is exactly the unproved genericity statement. It does **not** follow from the recurrence, from reflection, or from the existence of \(Q_p\).

Indeed the recurrence imposes strong algebraic dependence: once the initial values are fixed, all \(b_r\) are determined. The empirical Poisson law says that the rare zero events nevertheless appear pseudorandom after quotienting by reflection. That is evidence for an arithmetic equidistribution theorem, not a consequence already contained in the recurrence.

So the answer is:

```text
Random-polynomial ensemble: yes, expected root count is exactly 1
(or (p-1)/p after Q(0)=1).

Actual Apéry Q_p: no expectation exists without an additional
prime-aspect equidistribution hypothesis.
```

# 4. Answer to (b): does \(d/p\to1/2\) obstruct Poisson?

No. The concern that random-polynomial root events are approximately independent only when \(d/p\to0\) is too restrictive.

For a random polynomial of degree at most \(d\), evaluations at any \(j\le d+1\) distinct points are **exactly** independent uniform variables. This is the Vandermonde interpolation statement: the map

\[
Q\longmapsto(Q(a_1),\ldots,Q(a_j))
\]

has rank \(j\) whenever the \(a_i\) are distinct and \(j\le d+1\).

Let

\[
R_p=\#\{a\in\mathbf F_p:Q(a)=0\}.
\]

For every fixed \(j\le d+1\),

\[
\mathbf E[(R_p)_j]
=
\frac{(p)_j}{p^j},
\tag{4.1}
\]

where \((x)_j=x(x-1)\cdots(x-j+1)\). If both \(d\to\infty\) and \(p\to\infty\), then for every fixed \(j\), eventually \(j\le d+1\), and

\[
\frac{(p)_j}{p^j}\longrightarrow1.
\tag{4.2}
\]

These are the factorial moments of \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\). Hence

\[
R_p\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1)
\tag{4.3}
\]

whenever \(d\to\infty\), including the regime

\[
d\sim p/2.
\]

For the reflection quotient the conclusion is even stronger. The relevant set \(S_p\setminus\{0\}\) has exactly \(m\) points, the same as the dimension of the affine coefficient space \(Q(0)=1\). Therefore **all** relevant evaluations are jointly independent, not merely every fixed subcollection. This is why (2.9) is an exact binomial law.

If one insists on choosing a polynomial of exact degree \(m\), this conditions the top coefficient to be nonzero. That event has probability \(1-1/p\), so it changes the distribution by only \(O(1/p)\) in total variation and does not change the limit.

Thus the ratio \(d/p=1/2\) is not an obstruction. It is almost the ideal interpolation regime for the quotient model.

# 5. Answer to (c): finite-\(p\) correction and the observed mean \(0.91\)

## 5.1 The quadratic-residue probability

Among all \(u\in\mathbf F_p\), the map \(u\mapsto1-4u\) is a bijection. Therefore

\[
\Pr(1-4u\text{ is a nonzero square})
=
\frac{p-1}{2p}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

This is exactly the correction proposed in the question.

However, it corresponds to an **unconditioned** random polynomial, in which the pair site \(u=0\) is allowed to be a zero. For Apéry,

\[
Q_p(0)=b_0=b_{p-1}=1,
\]

so that site is deterministically excluded. The minimal endpoint-respecting pair parameter is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_p=\frac{p-3}{2p}
=rac12-\frac{3}{2p}.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

The central branch point \(u=1/4\) has probability \(1/p\) in the null model and contributes one zero index rather than a pair. Consequently

\[
\mathbf E[Z_p]=1-\frac2p.
\tag{5.3}
\]

If one ignores the endpoint constraint, then the noncentral pair mean is \((p-1)/(2p)\), while the central contribution restores the total expected zero count to exactly \(1\):

\[
2\cdot\frac{p-1}{2p}+\frac1p=1.
\tag{5.4}
\]

So the finite quadratic-residue correction does not naturally produce a large downward shift in the mean of \(Z_p\).

There are additional deterministic Apéry constraints, such as fixed initial values and the no-two-consecutive-zeros theorem. Conditioning on any fixed number of nonzero sites changes \(\lambda_p\) only by \(O(1/p)\). At the sparse rate \(1/p\), finite-range exclusion constraints likewise affect the law only at order \(1/p\). Hence the robust asymptotic parameter remains \(1/2\).

## 5.2 Pooled finite-prime correction

For a finite set \(\mathcal P\) of \(M\) primes, the endpoint-conditioned null model predicts

\[
\frac1M\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathbf E[K_p]
=
\frac12-
\frac{3}{2M}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\frac1p,
\tag{5.5}
\]

and

\[
\frac1M\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathbf E[Z_p]
=
1-
\frac{2}{M}\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\frac1p.
\tag{5.6}
\]

For hundreds of primes up to \(5000\), these corrections are only a few thousandths. They are much too small to explain a deficit of roughly \(0.09\).

## 5.3 Sampling fluctuation

Under the limiting model,

\[
\mathbf E[K_p]=\frac12,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(K_p)=\frac12,
\]

so for \(M=667\) primes the standard error of the sample mean pair count is

\[
\sqrt{\frac{1/2}{667}}
\approx0.0274.
\tag{5.7}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\mathbf E[Z_p]=1,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Z_p)=2,
\]

and the standard error of the sample mean of \(Z_p\) is

\[
\sqrt{\frac{2}{667}}
\approx0.0548.
\tag{5.8}
\]

A mean \(\overline Z\approx0.91\) is therefore only about

\[
\frac{1-0.91}{0.0548}\approx1.64
\]

standard errors below \(1\). That is completely compatible with sampling noise.

The rounded frequencies in the question give a pair mean around \(0.45\), also less than two standard errors below \(1/2\). The observed excess in the zero-pair-free class, \(62.7\%\) versus \(60.7\%\), is likewise only about one standard deviation at this sample size.

Moreover, the repository's later scans already report the mean moving back to approximately \(1\):

- `problems/3.2/UNDERSTANDING.md` records mean \(|Z_p|=1.0140\) over \(4201\) primes below \(40000\);
- the current `problems/3.2/proof.tex` records the larger scan through \(10^6\) with mean approximately \(1\) and a strong Poisson fit.

Thus there is no evidence here for a corrected limiting parameter below \(1/2\).

## 5.4 The central exception

The self-paired point is

\[
r=\frac{p-1}{2},
\qquad
u=\frac14.
\]

In the null model it vanishes with probability \(1/p\). Hence odd values of \(Z_p\) are expected to be extremely sparse. Across primes up to \(X\), the naive expected number of central exceptions is

\[
\sum_{p\le X}\frac1p,
\]

which grows only like \(\log\log X\). A single exception such as \(p=11\) in a modest sample is therefore entirely compatible with the null model, although the central Apéry value has additional arithmetic structure and should be analyzed separately in any proof.

# 6. Chebotarev: the correct formulation

The Chebotarev analogy is excellent, but the precise group is not merely \(S_d\), and a fixed polynomial over \(\mathbf F_p\) cannot literally have splitting-field Galois group \(S_d\) when \(d\ge3\).

## 6.1 Why “Galois group \(S_d\) over \(\mathbf F_p\)” is not literal

The splitting field of a polynomial over \(\mathbf F_p\) is a finite field \(\mathbf F_{p^n}\). Its Galois group over \(\mathbf F_p\) is cyclic, generated by Frobenius.

An \(S_d\) statement can instead mean one of the following.

1. A fixed polynomial \(F\in\mathbf Q[U]\) has Galois group \(S_d\), and one varies the reduction prime \(p\).
2. A polynomial family \(F_T(U)\) has geometric monodromy \(S_d\) over a function field, and one varies the parameter \(T\in\mathbf F_p\).
3. The generic polynomial over its coefficient space has monodromy \(S_d\), and one averages over coefficient specializations.

In any of these legitimate settings, Chebotarev says that Frobenius conjugacy classes are distributed like elements of \(S_d\), subject to the usual hypotheses and error terms.

The number of roots modulo \(p\) is the number of fixed points of Frobenius on the \(d\) roots. For a uniform permutation \(\sigma\in S_d\), the fixed-point count has distribution

\[
\Pr(\#\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)=k)
=
\frac1{k!}
\sum_{j=0}^{d-k}\frac{(-1)^j}{j!},
\tag{6.1}
\]

which tends to

\[
e^{-1}\frac1{k!}.
\]

Thus the fixed-point count tends to \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\) as \(d\to\infty\).

## 6.2 The quadratic-residue filter requires the hyperoctahedral group

Let \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\) be the roots of \(Q\). To record whether

\[
1-4\alpha_i
\]

is a square, one must adjoin

\[
\sqrt{1-4\alpha_i}
\]

for every root. In the generic linearly disjoint situation, the resulting Galois group is the signed permutation group

\[
W_d=C_2^d\rtimes S_d=C_2\wr S_d,
\tag{6.2}
\]

the hyperoctahedral group.

A root \(\alpha_i\in\mathbf F_p\) with \(1-4\alpha_i\) a nonzero square corresponds to a **positive fixed point** of the signed Frobenius permutation. A root with nonsquare discriminant is a negative fixed point. The branch value \(1-4\alpha_i=0\) is the central exceptional case.

For a uniform element of \(W_d\), let \(Y_d\) be the number of positive fixed points. For an ordered \(j\)-tuple of distinct labels, the probability that all labels are fixed and all signs are positive is

\[
\frac{2^{d-j}(d-j)!}{2^d d!}
=
\frac{1}{2^j(d)_j}.
\tag{6.3}
\]

There are \((d)_j\) ordered \(j\)-tuples. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf E[(Y_d)_j]=\left(\frac12\right)^j
}
\qquad(j\le d).
\tag{6.4}
\]

These are exactly the factorial moments of \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)\). Hence

\[
Y_d\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)
\qquad(d\to\infty).
\tag{6.5}
\]

This is the rigorous Chebotarev version of “Poisson\((1)\) followed by independent quadratic thinning.” The independence is not automatic from \(S_d\); it is encoded by the full wreath-product monodromy.

This calculation is already present in the project in `problems/3.2/proof.tex`, Proposition `prop:poisson`, for the canonical gap polynomials \(N_h\) under the hypothesis that their Galois group is full hyperoctahedral.

## 6.3 Why this does not yet prove the Apéry coefficient law

The Apéry quotient polynomial is \(Q_p\), whose coefficients and degree both depend on \(p\), with

\[
\deg Q_p\asymp p.
\]

There is only one such polynomial at each prime. Thus there is no fixed number-field extension or fixed finite étale cover to which ordinary Chebotarev can be applied.

A direct Chebotarev proof would require at least:

1. a single compatible family whose specialization at \(p\) produces the actual \(Q_p\);
2. full signed monodromy for that family;
3. effective Chebotarev estimates uniform while the degree and conductor grow with \(p\);
4. control showing that the distinguished Apéry specialization is equidistributed rather than exceptional.

None of these is presently available. The repository correctly distinguishes the two aspects:

- for a fixed gap polynomial and varying primes, hyperoctahedral Chebotarev proves the signed fixed-point law;
- for the actual coefficient zeros at a fixed prime, transferring that law is still conjectural.

This is the content of the prime-aspect/orbit-aspect warning in `proof.tex`, Remark `rem:two-aspects`, and of the Chebotarev shortcut warning in `UNDERSTANDING.md`.

So Chebotarev supplies the right **conceptual model**, but not yet a proof for \(Z_p\).

# 7. The precise arithmetic statement needed for Apéry

Let

\[
I_p=\mathbf 1_{b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv0\pmod p},
\qquad
K_p=\frac{Z_p-I_p}{2}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

The exact Poisson conjecture across primes is

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}
\#\{p\le X:K_p=k\}
\longrightarrow
 e^{-1/2}\frac{(1/2)^k}{k!}
\tag{7.2}
\]

for every fixed \(k\ge0\).

By the method of factorial moments, it is enough to prove, for every fixed \(j\ge1\),

\[
\boxed{
\frac1{\pi(X)}
\sum_{p\le X}(K_p)_j
\longrightarrow
\left(\frac12\right)^j.
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Writing \(P_p\) for the set of noncentral reflection-pair orbits, this is

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}
\sum_{p\le X}
\sum_{\substack{u_1,\ldots,u_j\in P_p\\
\text{distinct}}}
\prod_{i=1}^j
\mathbf 1_{Q_p(u_i)=0}
\longrightarrow
2^{-j}.
\tag{7.4}
\]

The random model predicts each joint zero event to have probability \(p^{-j}\), while there are about \((p/2)^j\) ordered orbit tuples, giving

\[
(p/2)^j p^{-j}=2^{-j}.
\]

For \(j=1\), (7.3) is the mean statement

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}\sum_{p\le X}K_p\longrightarrow\frac12.
\tag{7.5}
\]

For all \(j\), it is the exact fixed-order independence theorem needed for Poisson.

This formulation is substantially weaker than asking for the complete vector

\[
(Q_p(u))_{u\in S_p}
\]

to be uniformly distributed. One only needs anti-concentration of simultaneous zeros. Nevertheless, even the first moment is not presently proved at the predicted scale. The existence of \(Q_p\) does not supply it for free.

# 8. Point-by-point assessment of the proposed derivation

## Step 1

> \(b_r=Q(r(p-1-r))\), with \(\deg Q=(p-1)/2\).

**Essentially correct**, with two edits:

\[
\deg Q_p\le\frac{p-1}{2},
\]

and the representation is a tautological consequence of reflection symmetry, not evidence that \(Q_p\) is generic.

## Step 2

> A random degree-\(d\) polynomial has expected one root.

**Correct for a specified random-polynomial ensemble.** Independence is not needed. After imposing \(Q(0)=1\), the expectation over all \(\mathbf F_p\) becomes \((p-1)/p\).

It is **not** automatically a statement about the actual deterministic \(Q_p\).

## Step 3

> A root gives a pair iff \(1-4u\) is a quadratic residue.

**Correct**, with the distinctions:

- nonzero square: one two-element zero-pair;
- zero discriminant: the self-paired center;
- nonsquare: no coefficient index;
- \(u=0\): the endpoint pair, which is deterministically nonzero for Apéry.

## Step 4

> Expected pair count is \(1\times1/2=1/2\).

**Correct at leading order**, but the clean derivation is

\[
\frac{p-3}{2}
\text{ admissible random pair sites}
\times\frac1p
=
\frac{p-3}{2p}	o\frac12.
\]

This avoids multiplying two random quantities and automatically handles the reflection quotient.

## Step 5

> Independence gives Poisson\((1/2)\).

**Exactly correct for the random quotient model.** In fact the finite-\(p\) law is binomial, and independence on the entire relevant set is exact.

**Unproved for Apéry.** For Apéry this step is the substantive arithmetic conjecture, expressible precisely by the factorial-moment limits (7.3).

---

# Final conclusion

The parameter \(1/2\) is not mysterious. It is the universal rare-event parameter obtained after quotienting \(p\) coefficient positions by the reflection involution:

\[
\text{number of independent pair-orbits}\sim\frac p2,
\qquad
\Pr(\text{zero at an orbit})\sim\frac1p.
\]

Therefore

\[
\lambda=\frac p2\cdot\frac1p=\frac12.
\]

The strongest clean statement is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Uniform random reflection-symmetric values}
\Longrightarrow
K_p\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\!\left(\frac{p-3}{2},\frac1p\right)
\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).
}
\]

The ratio \(d/p\to1/2\) is not a problem; it is precisely what makes interpolation on the quotient set give exact independence.

The observed mean \(Z_p\approx0.91\) over \(667\) primes is not explained by the \(1/(2p)\) quadratic-residue correction. That correction is far too small, and the deficit is less than two standard errors. The larger computations already recorded in the repository return the mean to approximately \(1\).

Chebotarev gives the same parameter when the relevant monodromy is the full hyperoctahedral group \(C_2\wr S_d\): zero-pairs are positive fixed points of a signed Frobenius permutation. But applying that theorem to the actual Apéry coefficient polynomial \(Q_p\) would require a uniform growing-degree family and orbit-aspect equidistribution that are not presently established.

Thus the proposed argument is a **correct and unusually precise heuristic**, after the finite corrections above. It is not yet a first-principles proof for the Apéry sequence. The missing theorem is exactly the fixed-order joint anti-concentration statement (7.3).
