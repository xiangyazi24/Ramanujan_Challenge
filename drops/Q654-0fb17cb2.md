# Dwork congruence + swap identity: what reflection really gives

## Executive summary

The reflection/swap symmetry is a real structural theorem, but it does **not** immediately prove AFP. The key point is:

- The swap identity gives a relation between the second half of the Steinberg sum and a **different Steinberg matrix coefficient** of the inverse orbit.
- Steinberg coefficients are not antisymmetric under inversion, so the hoped-for cancellation
  $$S_{\rm right}=-S_{\rm left}+O(1)$$
  does not follow.
- The reflection reduces the number of independent terms by a factor of two, but it does not reduce the sheaf rank or automatically improve square-root cancellation to bounded cancellation.

The correct interpretation is that reflection gives a self-duality constraint on the Mellin/transfer representation. It is useful, but AFP still requires a genuine cancellation theorem for the half-orbit.

---

# (A) Exact Steinberg transformation

Let

$$
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The assumed identity is

$$
M(p-1-r)=\sigma M(r)^{-1}\sigma.
$$

For the Steinberg representation of $G=\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)$, write

$$
\mathrm{St}_{v,w}(g)
$$
for the matrix coefficient between projective vectors $v,w$.

The representation satisfies

$$
\mathrm{St}_{v,w}(aga^{-1})
=
\mathrm{St}_{a^{-1}v,a w}(g)
$$

and contragredience gives

$$
\mathrm{St}_{v,w}(g^{-1})
=
\mathrm{St}_{w,v}(g)
$$

(up to the standard choice of invariant pairing).

Therefore:

$$
\mathrm{St}_{[5:1],[0:1]}(M(p-1-r))
=
\mathrm{St}_{\sigma[0:1],\sigma[5:1]}(M(r)^{-1})
$$

so

$$
=
\mathrm{St}_{[1:0],[1:5]}(M(r)^{-1}).
$$

Using inversion:

$$
\mathrm{St}_{[1:0],[1:5]}(M(r)^{-1})
=
\mathrm{St}_{[1:5],[1:0]}(M(r)).
$$

This is the exact relation.

The original term is

$$
\mathrm{St}_{[5:1],[0:1]}(M(r)).
$$

These are **different matrix coefficients**. They would cancel only if there were an additional involution identifying

$$
\mathrm{St}_{[1:5],[1:0]}(g)=-\mathrm{St}_{[5:1],[0:1]}(g).
$$

There is no such identity in the Steinberg representation.

Hence:

$$
S_{\rm right}
\neq -S_{\rm left}
$$

in general.

# (B) Does reflection halve the Mellin sheaf rank?

No.

Reflection acts on the **parameter space**:

$$
r\mapsto p-1-r.
$$

Equivalently, on multiplicative characters:

$$
\chi\mapsto \chi^{-1}.
$$

This gives a self-duality decomposition:

$$
\mathcal F \cong \iota^*\mathcal F
$$

where $\iota(t)=t^{-1}$.

But rank is a geometric invariant of the sheaf:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal F)
$$

and quotienting by an involution does not divide rank by two.

The quotient changes the base curve from $\mathbf G_m$ to

$$
\mathbf G_m/\{t\sim t^{-1}\}
$$

which is again a curve. The pushforward decomposes into symmetric and antisymmetric pieces, but the original local system does not become half rank automatically.

So:

- domain size: halved;
- sheaf rank: unchanged;
- conductor: may decrease after taking invariant pieces, but not by a factor of two in general.

# (C) Does half-domain + equidistribution prove AFP?

No.

After reflection we need

$$
\sum_{r=1}^{(p-3)/2}
\mathrm{St}_{[5:1],[0:1]}(M(r))=O(1).
$$

The half interval contains about $p/2$ terms.

If the terms behaved independently with variance one, the natural bound is

$$
|S_{\rm left}|\sim \sqrt p.
$$

This is exactly the central limit scale.

Equidistribution of the orbit only proves the one-point distribution:

$$
\#\{r:M(r)v=x\}\approx1.
$$

AFP requires control of the **ordered correlations**:

$$
\sum_{r,s}
\mathrm{St}(M(r))\overline{\mathrm{St}(M(s))}.
$$

A Deligne bound for individual trace sums normally gives square-root cancellation, not bounded sums.

To obtain $O(1)$ one needs an additional rigidity phenomenon, for example:

1. exact telescoping;
2. bounded conductor plus a special monodromy relation;
3. a coboundary identity
   $$
   \mathrm{St}(M(r))=F(r+1)-F(r);
   $$
4. exceptional algebraic cancellation from the Apéry cocycle.

Reflection alone does not provide this.

# (D) Relation to Deligne/Katz-Sarnak

The observed uniformity is compatible with Katz's philosophy.

The transfer matrices define a rank-two lisse sheaf. The important invariants are:

- geometric monodromy group;
- conductor;
- local monodromy at singularities.

For the Apéry recurrence the finite singularities come from denominators:

$$
r+1=0.$$

So there is a singularity at $r=-1$.

At infinity the rational matrix has polynomial growth. After gauge normalization, the local monodromy is determined by the exponents of the Apéry differential equation.

The conductor has the form

$$
\mathrm{cond}(\mathcal F)
=\mathrm{rank}+\mathrm{Swan}_{\infty}+\mathrm{Swan}_{-1}.
$$

It is bounded independently of $p$.

This explains why individual trace statistics look random-matrix-like:

- bounded conductor;
- large monodromy (expected $\mathrm{SL}_2$);
- Deligne square-root bounds.

It explains the constant-scale discrepancy of the **normalized occupancy statistic**, but not an AFP theorem.

# Important correction about the random walk comparison

The random walk comparison in the question mixes two different quantities.

For a genuine random walk on a set of size $N=p+1$:

- after mixing, the empirical occupancy fluctuations satisfy
  $$
  \chi^2/N\approx1;
  $$
- the unnormalized $L^2$ discrepancy is typically constant or $\sqrt N$ depending on normalization.

Thus observing

$$
\chi^2/(p+1)\to1
$$
means the orbit looks like a random sample at the one-point level. It does not imply stronger-than-random cancellation.

The Apéry orbit is unusual because a deterministic cocycle achieves random-like one-point statistics, not because it has proven perfect cancellation.

# Final answer

The swap identity gives

$$
\boxed{
\mathrm{St}_{[5:1],[0:1]}(M(p-1-r))
=
\mathrm{St}_{[1:5],[1:0]}(M(r))
}
$$

not the negative of the original coefficient.

Therefore:

1. Reflection halves the independent zero search space.
2. Reflection does not halve the Mellin sheaf rank.
3. Reflection does not prove AFP by cancellation.
4. Deligne/Katz explains why the orbit is equidistributed and why normalized discrepancy is constant-scale.
5. The remaining AFP problem is a stronger statement: a deterministic Steinberg matrix coefficient sum has bounded total mass rather than square-root size.

The missing ingredient is not reflection; it is an Apéry-specific coboundary/hidden conservation law for the Steinberg observable.
