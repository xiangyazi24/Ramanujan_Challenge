# Audit verdict

The proposed **no-finite-width conclusion is correct for the actual level-six Apéry \(q\)-series**, but the proof as written has one genuinely important analytic flaw and two smaller formulation issues.

The important flaw is the sentence

> near \(q=e^{-\varepsilon}\to1^-\), \(t(q)\to0\), hence \(E(q)=F(t(q))\ge 1\).

That is **not a valid statement about the analytic \(q\)-series \(E\) on the second cusp**.  The identity \(E=F\circ t\) is initially an identity of germs at \(q=0\).  The actual level-six modular parametrization analytically continues that germ to the eta quotient

\[
E(q)=Z(\tau):=
\frac{\eta(2\tau)^7\eta(3\tau)^7}
     {\eta(\tau)^5\eta(6\tau)^5},
\qquad q=e^{2\pi i\tau},
\]

throughout \(|q|<1\).  On the positive real radius \(q=e^{-\varepsilon}\to1^-\), the *direct principal \(t\)-power-series value* \(F(t(q))\) tends to \(F(0)=1\), whereas the modular continuation \(E(q)\) satisfies

\[
E(e^{-\varepsilon})\sim \frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon^2}\to+\infty.
\]

So the two must not be identified pointwise there.  This is the one place where the proposed argument, literally read, breaks.

Fortunately the repair is clean and stronger: use the eta-quotient continuation of \(E\), whose cusp asymptotic is explicit.  Together with

\[
A(e^{-\varepsilon})
 =\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon}-\varepsilon+o(1)\right),
\qquad
H(e^{-\varepsilon})
 \sim-\frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon^2},
\]

one gets the much sharper asymptotic

\[
C_n(e^{-\varepsilon})
\sim
-\frac{4\pi^4}{9\varepsilon^4}
\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi^2 n}{3\varepsilon}-n\varepsilon\right).
\]

This proves radius exactly \(1\), non-rationality, Pólya--Carlson natural boundary, non-D-finiteness, and hence absence of every nonzero finite-order polynomial-coefficient recurrence in the coefficient index \(j\).  The universal-in-\(n\) conclusion is also valid, and in fact the parenthetical “unless all specializations vanish” can be removed when the universal coefficients are honest polynomials in \(n,j\): if all positive-integer specializations vanished identically in \(j\), the coefficient polynomials themselves would be zero.

Thus the final theorem is **YES, after replacing the wrong second-cusp use of \(F(t)\) by the actual eta-quotient continuation of the level-six Apéry germ and stating recurrences as eventual recurrences in \(j\)**.

# 2. Formal integrality: PASS

The integrality assertion is sound, and it can be checked without any modular-form input.

We have

\[
A(q)\in 1+q\mathbb Z[[q]].
\]

Because a unit of \(\mathbb Z[[q]]\) with constant term \(1\) has inverse again in \(\mathbb Z[[q]]\),

\[
A(q)^{-1}\in1+q\mathbb Z[[q]],
\qquad
 t(q)=qA(q)^{-1}\in q\mathbb Z[[q]].
\]

Since all Apéry coefficients \(b_r\) are integers,

\[
F(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}b_r t^r\in\mathbb Z[[t]],
\]

and formal composition at a series with zero constant term gives

\[
E(q):=F(t(q))\in\mathbb Z[[q]].
\tag{5}
\]

Now

\[
\Theta_q\log A
=q\frac{A'(q)}{A(q)}.
\]

There is no denominator problem from the formal logarithm, because the logarithm itself is unnecessary.  Since \(qA'(q)\in q\mathbb Z[[q]]\) and \(A^{-1}\in\mathbb Z[[q]]\),

\[
qA'(q)/A(q)\in q\mathbb Z[[q]],
\]

so

\[
H(q)=1-qA'(q)/A(q)\in\mathbb Z[[q]].
\tag{6}
\]

For the stated \(n\ge1\) one can make this even more manifest:

\[
H(q)A(q)^n
=A(q)^n-qA'(q)A(q)^{n-1}\in\mathbb Z[[q]].
\tag{7}
\]

Combining (5) and (7),

\[
C_n(q)=E(q)H(q)A(q)^n\in\mathbb Z[[q]].
\]

Also

\[
C_n(0)=E(0)H(0)A(0)^n=1,
\]

so none of these specialized series is the zero series.

**Verdict on integrality:** fully correct.

# 3. Holomorphy on the open unit disk: PASS, but only with the modular continuation of \(E\)

## 3.1 \(A\) is holomorphic and zero-free for \(|q|<1\)

For every \(r<1\),

\[
\sum_{m\ge1}\sup_{|q|\le r}|q|^m<\infty.
\]

Hence the product

\[
\prod_{3\nmid m}(1+q^m)^{12}
\]

converges normally on compact subsets of the unit disk.  Each factor is nonzero there, since \(|q^m|<1\), and the normally convergent product is zero-free.  Thus \(A\) is holomorphic and nonvanishing on \(|q|<1\).

Consequently \(A'/A\) and hence \(H=1-qA'/A\) are holomorphic on \(|q|<1\).

## 3.2 The composition \(F(t(q))\) alone does *not* prove disk-wide holomorphy

This is a point that must be stated explicitly.

The ordinary Apéry generating function \(F(t)=\sum b_r t^r\) has finite radius of convergence.  Therefore, merely knowing that \(t(q)\) is holomorphic for \(|q|<1\) does **not** imply that the principal Taylor series \(F(t(q))\) converges for every \(q\) in the unit disk.

So the sentence

> “\(E(q)=F(t(q))\), therefore \(E\) is analytic for \(|q|<1\)”

would be an invalid proof if no additional level-six modular identity were invoked.

For the **actual** Apéry series the missing input is exactly (4): the germ \(F(t(q))\) equals

\[
E(q)=Z(\tau)
=rac{\eta(2\tau)^7\eta(3\tau)^7}
       {\eta(\tau)^5\eta(6\tau)^5}.
\]

Writing the eta quotient as a \(q\)-product, the initial powers of \(q\) cancel because

\[
7\cdot2+7\cdot3-5\cdot1-5\cdot6=0,
\]

and

\[
E(q)=
\prod_{m\ge1}
\frac{(1-q^{2m})^7(1-q^{3m})^7}
     {(1-q^m)^5(1-q^{6m})^5}.
\tag{8}
\]

Every denominator factor is nonzero for \(|q|<1\), and again the logarithms of the factors form a normally convergent series on compacta.  Therefore (8) is holomorphic and nonzero throughout the open unit disk and has value \(1\) at \(q=0\).

Thus the formal series (5) is the Taylor series at \(0\) of a function holomorphic on \(|q|<1\).

## 3.3 Therefore \(C_n\) is holomorphic on \(|q|<1\)

All three factors \(E,H,A^n\) are holomorphic there, so

\[
C_n(q)=E(q)H(q)A(q)^n
\]

is holomorphic throughout the open unit disk.

**Verdict on analyticity:** correct for the actual level-six Apéry series, but it requires the eta-quotient continuation of \(E\).  It does not follow from formal composition alone.

# 4. The proposed positivity step at \(q\to1^-\): FAILS AS WRITTEN

The proposal argues:

\[
t(q)\to0,
\qquad
E(q)=F(t(q))\ge1

because the Apéry coefficients are positive.

The conclusion \(E(q)>1\) eventually happens to be true for the analytic continuation, but **that reasoning is wrong**.

To see the branch issue in the sharpest possible way, take

\[
q=e^{-\varepsilon},\qquad \varepsilon\downarrow0.
\]

We will prove below that \(A(q)\to\infty\), hence

\[
t(q)=q/A(q)\to0.
\]

If one directly substitutes this small positive number into the principal Taylor series \(F(t)\), then

\[
F(t(q))\to F(0)=1.
\tag{9}
\]

\[
E(e^{-\varepsilon})
\sim\frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon^2}\to+\infty.
\tag{10}

The set in the \(q\)-disk on which the **principal** \(t\)-Taylor series can be substituted without crossing its singular locus need not connect the \(q=0\) and \(q=1\) cusp neighborhoods.  The modular eta quotient supplies the continuation of the original germ across that obstruction.

Therefore the sentence

> “\(t\to0\), so by positivity of the Apéry coefficients \(E=F(t)\ge1\)”

The correct second-cusp input is the eta quotient (4), from which one obtains (10) directly.

# 6. Radius of convergence exactly \(1\): PASS after the branch repair

\[
C_n(q)=\sum_{j\ge0}c_j(n)q^j.

No hidden appeal to Pólya--Carlson is needed for this step.

# 7. Non-rationality: PASS, with a stronger proof than merely “unbounded”

A rational function that is holomorphic on \(|q|<1\) can have at \(q=1\) only a removable singularity or a pole of finite order.  Therefore, if \(R(q)\) is such a rational function, then for some integer \(M\ge0\),

\[
|R(e^{-\varepsilon})|=O(\varepsilon^{-M})
\qquad(\varepsilon\downarrow0).
\tag{19}
\]

But (17) gives

\[
|C_n(e^{-\varepsilon})|
\ge e^{c n/\varepsilon}
\]

for some absolute \(c>0\) and all sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\).  Exponential growth in \(1/\varepsilon\) dominates every power of \(1/\varepsilon\), contradicting (19).

Hence

\[
\boxed{C_n(q)\text{ is not rational for every fixed }n\ge1.}
\tag{20}
\]

This is rigorous even if one does not try to classify the singularity at \(q=1\) more precisely.

**Verdict on non-rationality:** correct.

# 8. Pólya--Carlson: all hypotheses are met

The classical Pólya--Carlson theorem says:

> If \(G(q)=\sum_{j\ge0}a_jq^j\) has integer coefficients and radius of convergence exactly \(1\), then either \(G\) is rational or the unit circle is a natural boundary for analytic continuation.

For \(C_n\) we have established:

1. \(c_j(n)\in\mathbb Z\) for all \(j\), by Section 2;
2. the radius of convergence is exactly \(1\), by (18);
3. \(C_n\) is not rational, by (20).

Therefore Pólya--Carlson gives

\[
\boxed{
|q|=1\text{ is a natural boundary for }C_n
\quad\text{for every fixed }n\ge1.
}
\tag{21}
\]

There is no need for bounded coefficients, nonnegative coefficients, lacunarity, or any additional arithmetic hypothesis.  Integer coefficients plus radius \(1\) are the relevant Pólya--Carlson hypotheses.

The theorem is being applied to the **one-variable scalar power series \(C_n(q)\)** after fixing \(n\); no multivariate version is needed.

**Verdict on Pólya--Carlson scope:** correct.

# 9. Natural boundary implies non-D-finite: PASS

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that \(C_n\) were D-finite over a characteristic-zero field.  Then there would exist polynomials \(p_0(q),\ldots,p_r(q)\), not all zero and with \(p_r\ne0\), such that

\[
p_r(q)C_n^{(r)}(q)+\cdots+p_1(q)C_n'(q)+p_0(q)C_n(q)=0.
\tag{22}
\]

At every finite point \(q_0\) with \(p_r(q_0)\ne0\), the equation is an ordinary linear differential equation with analytic coefficients.  A local solution can be analytically continued through such an ordinary point.  Thus all finite singularities of the germ are contained in the finite set

\[
\{q:p_r(q)=0\}.
\]

A function whose entire unit circle is a natural boundary has a singular obstruction at every point of that circle, an infinite set.  This contradicts (22).

Hence

\[
\boxed{C_n\text{ is not D-finite for every fixed }n\ge1.}
\tag{23}
\]

This is exactly the standard finite-singularity obstruction for univariate D-finite functions.

**Verdict on the D-finite step:** correct.

# 10. Coefficient recurrence equivalence: PASS with one wording correction

For a formal power series over a field of characteristic zero,

\[
G(q)=\sum_{j\ge0}g_jq^j
\]

is D-finite if and only if its coefficient sequence is P-recursive: there exist a finite integer \(r\ge0\) and polynomials \(P_0(j),\ldots,P_r(j)\), not all zero, such that

\[
\sum_{k=0}^{r}P_k(j)g_{j+k}=0
\tag{24}
\]

for all sufficiently large \(j\).  Shifts \(g_{j-k}\) instead of \(g_{j+k}\) are equivalent after reindexing.

Therefore (23) is equivalent to:

\[
\boxed{
\text{For every fixed }n\ge1,
\text{ there is no nonzero finite-width recurrence }
\sum_{k=0}^r P_k(j)c_{j+k}(n)=0
\text{ valid for all sufficiently large }j.
}
\tag{25}
\]

Two qualifications matter.

* “Fixed width” must mean **finite order independent of \(j\)** after \(n\) is fixed.  The order may have been chosen using \(n\); (25) still rules it out because each fixed specialization is non-D-finite.
* The recurrence must hold on an **infinite tail in \(j\)**.  A polynomial-coefficient relation holding only in a finite window such as \(0\le j\le n\) does not imply D-finiteness and is not excluded by this argument.

Apart from that standard “eventually in \(j\)” convention, the claimed recurrence equivalence is exact.

**Verdict on recurrence equivalence:** correct after explicitly saying “for all sufficiently large \(j\).”

# 11. Universal polynomial recurrence in \(n,j\): valid, and stronger than stated

Suppose there were a universal finite width \(r\) and polynomials

\[
P_0(n,j),\ldots,P_r(n,j)\in\mathbb C[n,j]
\]

not all zero such that, for every integer \(n\ge1\),

\[
\sum_{k=0}^rP_k(n,j)c_{j+k}(n)=0
\tag{26}
\]

for all sufficiently large \(j\) (the starting index may even depend on \(n\)).

Choose \(k_0\) such that \(P_{k_0}(n,j)\ne0\) as a bivariate polynomial.  Write

\[
P_{k_0}(n,j)=\sum_{s=0}^{d}a_s(n)j^s,
\]

where at least one \(a_s(n)\in\mathbb C[n]\) is nonzero.

The specialization \(P_{k_0}(n_0,j)\) is identically zero as a polynomial in \(j\) only if

\[
a_0(n_0)=\cdots=a_d(n_0)=0.
\]

In particular it lies in the zero set of any one nonzero coefficient polynomial \(a_s\), so this can happen for only finitely many \(n_0\in\mathbb C\), hence for only finitely many positive integers \(n_0\).

Therefore for all but finitely many positive integers \(n\), at least one specialized coefficient \(P_k(n,j)\) is a nonzero polynomial in \(j\).  For any such \(n\), (26) becomes a nonzero P-recursive recurrence for \(c_j(n)\), contradicting (25).

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\text{There is no nonzero universal finite-width recurrence with coefficients polynomial in }n,j
\text{ that is valid on the }j\text{-tail for every }n\ge1.
}
\tag{27}
\]

In fact the same proof rules out a nonzero polynomial-coefficient universal recurrence valid for **any infinite set of positive integers \(n\)**.

The proposed parenthetical

> “unless all specializations vanish”

is therefore unnecessary in the polynomial setting.  If every positive-integer specialization of every coefficient polynomial were identically zero in \(j\), each bivariate coefficient polynomial would itself be zero.  The only “universally vanishing specialization” is the trivial recurrence.

There can of course be finitely many exceptional \(n\) at which a particular symbolic coefficient happens to specialize to zero, but that does not rescue a universal relation valid for all \(n\ge1\).

**Verdict on the universal-in-\(n\) sentence:** correct, and it can be strengthened to remove the exception clause.

# 12. Corrected theorem, in proof-ready form

Here is the version I would use as the exact theorem.

## Theorem (level-six Apéry no-finite-width theorem)

Let

\[
A(q)=\prod_{3\nmid m}(1+q^m)^{12}\in1+q\mathbb Z[[q]],
\qquad
 t(q)=q/A(q),
\]

and let

\[
F(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}b_rt^r,
\qquad
b_r=\sum_{k=0}^r\binom rk^2\binom{r+k}k^2.
\]

Let \(E(q)\in\mathbb Z[[q]]\) be the germ \(F(t(q))\) at \(q=0\).  Via the classical level-six modular parametrization, this germ has the holomorphic continuation

\[
E(q)=
\frac{\eta(2\tau)^7\eta(3\tau)^7}
     {\eta(\tau)^5\eta(6\tau)^5},
\qquad q=e^{2\pi i\tau},\quad |q|<1.
\]

Put

\[
H(q)=1-\Theta_q\log A(q)
=1-qA'(q)/A(q),
\]

and for every fixed integer \(n\ge1\),

\[
C_n(q)=E(q)H(q)A(q)^n
=\sum_{j\ge0}c_j(n)q^j.
\]

Then:

1. \(C_n(q)\in\mathbb Z[[q]]\).
2. \(C_n\) is holomorphic on \(|q|<1\).
3. Its Taylor series at \(0\) has radius of convergence exactly \(1\).
4. \(C_n\) is not rational.
5. The unit circle is a natural boundary for \(C_n\).
6. \(C_n\) is not D-finite.
7. Consequently \((c_j(n))_{j\ge0}\) satisfies no nonzero finite-order polynomial-coefficient recurrence in \(j\) valid for all sufficiently large \(j\).
8. Consequently there are no \(r\ge0\) and nonzero polynomials \(P_0(n,j),\ldots,P_r(n,j)\) for which
   \[
   \sum_{k=0}^rP_k(n,j)c_{j+k}(n)=0
   \]
   holds for every \(n\ge1\) and all sufficiently large \(j\) (even allowing the starting \(j\) to depend on \(n\)).

Moreover, with \(q=e^{-\varepsilon}\),

\[
C_n(e^{-\varepsilon})
\sim
-\frac{4\pi^4}{9\varepsilon^4}
\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi^2n}{3\varepsilon}-n\varepsilon\right)
\qquad(\varepsilon\downarrow0).
\]

### Proof

Formal integrality follows from

\[
A^{-1}\in\mathbb Z[[q]],
\quad t=qA^{-1}\in q\mathbb Z[[q]],
\quad E=F(t)\in\mathbb Z[[q]],
\]

and

\[
HA^n=A^n-qA'A^{n-1}\in\mathbb Z[[q]].
\]

The product defining \(A\) is holomorphic and zero-free on the unit disk.  The level-six eta quotient for \(E\) is holomorphic there as well, so \(C_n\) is holomorphic on \(|q|<1\).

The eta transformation at the cusp \(0\) gives the three asymptotics

\[
A(e^{-\varepsilon})
=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon}-\varepsilon+o(1)\right),
\]

\[
H(e^{-\varepsilon})
\sim-\frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon^2},
\qquad
E(e^{-\varepsilon})
\sim\frac{2\pi^2}{3\varepsilon^2},
\]

which yield the displayed asymptotic for \(C_n\).  Thus \(q=1\) is singular, while there are no singularities in \(|q|<1\); the Taylor radius is exactly \(1\).  The exponential \(e^{c/\varepsilon}\) radial growth rules out a rational function, which could have at most a finite-order pole at \(q=1\).  Pólya--Carlson now forces the whole unit circle to be a natural boundary.  A D-finite germ has analytic continuation away from the finite zero set of the leading coefficient of a polynomial differential equation, so it cannot have a natural boundary.  The standard D-finite/P-recursive equivalence gives the fixed-\(n\) recurrence statement.  Finally, a nonzero polynomial in \(n,j\) can specialize to the zero polynomial in \(j\) for only finitely many \(n\), so any nonzero universal recurrence would give a forbidden nonzero fixed-\(n\) recurrence for some (indeed all but finitely many) \(n\).  ∎

# Bottom line

There is a real theorem here.  The natural-boundary/P-recursiveness chain is not the weak point.  The only serious defect in the proposed proof is the **second-cusp branch identification** of \(E\): positivity of the principal Apéry \(F\)-series cannot be used there.  Once the actual level-six eta quotient is inserted as the analytic continuation of the \(q=0\) germ, one obtains an explicit cusp asymptotic, and every subsequent implication is rigorous.

So the corrected result is:

\[
\boxed{
\forall n\ge1,\quad
C_n(q)\in\mathbb Z[[q]],\ R(C_n)=1,\ C_n\text{ has }|q|=1\text{ as a natural boundary},
}
\]

hence

\[
\boxed{
\forall n\ge1,\quad (c_j(n))_{j\ge0}\text{ is not P-recursive},
}
\]

and therefore there is **no nonzero universal finite-width recurrence with coefficients polynomial in \(n,j\)** that holds on the coefficient tail for all positive integers \(n\).
