# Hasse-Witt/Cartier analysis of Apéry zero-producing states

## Executive conclusion

The Cartier/Hasse-Witt viewpoint is genuinely the closest geometric structure behind the Lucas automaton, but it does **not currently give** the desired bounded-complexity description of
\(Z_p^{(1)}=\{r<p:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\}\).

The key distinction is:

- Cartier/Hasse-Witt controls the **global Frobenius action** on the cohomology of the K3 family.
- The set \(Z_p^{(1)}\) records the zeros of an entire coefficient sequence of a period modulo p.

The former is finite-dimensional; the latter is a length-p orbit. The missing theorem would require showing that this finite-dimensional Frobenius data rigidly controls every coefficient zero. No known general theorem does this.

## 1. Exact relation between Hasse-Witt and Apéry coefficients

Let
\[
F(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_n t^n
\]
be the Apéry period. For a Calabi-Yau/K3 Picard-Fuchs family, the Hasse-Witt matrix is obtained from coefficients of a defining equation or period expansion. In a one-parameter family one obtains entries of the form
\[
W_p=(c_{ip-j})_{i,j}
\]
where the \(c_m\) are coefficients of a period or residue form.

The important point is:

\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p
\]

is **not** generally equivalent to one entry of \(W_p\) vanishing.

The coefficient \(b_r\) is an individual Taylor coefficient. The Hasse-Witt matrix packages only the Frobenius-transformed cohomology class. It detects ordinarity/non-ordinarity, not individual coefficient zeros.

The correct relation is indirect:

\[
\text{period coefficients}
\longrightarrow
\text{Cartier/Frobenius action}
\longrightarrow
\text{Hasse-Witt matrix}.
\]

The implication cannot presently be reversed coefficient-by-coefficient.

## 2. The 1x1 K3 Hasse-Witt matrix issue

For a K3 surface,
\[
h^{2,0}=1,
\]
so the Hasse-Witt operator on \(H^0(X,\Omega^2)\) is indeed scalar:
\[
C(\omega)=c_p\omega.
\]

However, this scalar is only one invariant:

- \(c_p\neq0\): ordinary prime.
- \(c_p=0\): non-ordinary/supersingular behavior.

It does **not** imply
\[
b_r=0\iff \phi(c_p,r)=0
\]
for a bounded-degree function \(\phi\).

A single scalar cannot encode p possible coefficient conditions unless there is additional hidden symmetry.

## 3. Ordinary primes and unit roots

For ordinary primes, crystalline Frobenius has a unit root eigenvalue \(u_p\). Dwork theory gives p-adic analytic descriptions such as
\[
F(t)/F(t^p)
\]
and unit-root formulas.

This is powerful for:

- congruences between periods,
- p-adic differential equations,
- supercongruences.

But a zero condition
\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p
\]
is a finite-field orbit hitting condition. It depends on the residue dynamics of the recurrence, not only on \(u_p\).

Therefore:

\[
 u_p \text{ known}\not\Rightarrow |Z_p^{(1)}|=O(1).
\]

## 4. Formal group viewpoint

The formal group gives another finite-height invariant.

For ordinary K3:

\[
\text{height}=1.
\]

The formal group is close to the multiplicative formal group and its logarithm has controlled p-adic behavior.

But the coefficients \(b_r\) are coefficients of the period solution, not simply logarithm coefficients of the formal group. A height-one formal group does not prevent many isolated zeros among the first p coefficients.

A possible future route would be a theorem of the form:

"ordinary formal group + Picard-Fuchs recurrence implies coefficient zero set has bounded complexity."

No such theorem is known.

## 5. Crystalline cohomology

Crystalline Frobenius gives a matrix
\[
\Phi_p:H^2_{crys}(X/W(k))\to H^2_{crys}(X/W(k)).
\]

Newton/Hodge polygons constrain eigenvalue valuations. This explains:

- ordinary versus supersingular primes;
- slopes of Frobenius;
- existence of unit roots.

However, the desired statement is much stronger:

\[
\#\{r<p:b_r=0\}=O(1).
\]

That is a statement about the orbit of a vector under repeated recurrence evolution. Newton polygons constrain eigenvalues, but not the distribution of individual orbit coordinates modulo p.

## 6. Comparison with other sequences

For many Calabi-Yau sequences:

- Domb numbers;
- Almkvist-Zudilin numbers;
- Apéry-like sequences;
- Hadamard products of hypergeometric motives;

there are Lucas congruences and Cartier interpretations.

Typically one knows:

\[
a_{mp+r}\equiv a_m a_r\pmod p.
\]

But a uniform theorem

\[
\#\{r<p:a_r\equiv0\pmod p\}=O(1)
\]

is not known in this generality.

The Apéry case is unusually strong because experiments suggest bounded zero sets, but it is not a standard consequence of being a Calabi-Yau motive.

## 7. Can Cartier give a bounded-degree polynomial detector?

The desired statement would be:

there exists fixed \(F\in\mathbb Z[x]\) such that
\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p\implies F(r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Then
\[
|Z_p^{(1)}|\le \deg F.
\]

Cartier theory alone does not produce such an F.

Why?

The Frobenius operator acts on a finite-dimensional space, while r ranges through a p-dimensional coefficient sequence. The recurrence orbit has effective complexity growing with p.

A bounded-degree detector would require a new rigidity phenomenon:

\[
\text{finite-dimensional motive}
\Rightarrow
\text{bounded algebraic zero set of coefficients}.
\]

This implication is currently unavailable.

# Final assessment

The Hasse-Witt/Cartier route is valuable because it explains:

1. why Lucas congruences exist;
2. why the forbidden digit automaton is natural;
3. why ordinary and supersingular primes behave differently;
4. why the Apéry recurrence comes from a geometric object.

But it does **not yet prove** the key estimate
\[
|Z_p^{(1)}|=O(1).
\]

The missing bridge is not Frobenius theory itself. It is a theorem converting finite-dimensional Frobenius rigidity into pointwise coefficient anti-concentration.

The realistic next targets are therefore:

1. derive stronger Cartier automaton constraints beyond Lucas;
2. identify extra endomorphisms/symmetries of the Apéry K3 motive;
3. prove a bounded-complexity description of the recurrence orbit in \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\);
4. combine Hasse-Witt information with additive combinatorics or large sieve bounds.

Verdict: **Cartier/Hasse-Witt is the correct geometric language, but the decisive bounded-zero theorem is still missing.**
