# Referee verdict

Within the current `Ramanujan_Challenge` proof architecture, R1 remains genuinely open:

$$
\sum_{P<p\le 2P} z_p \ll \frac{P}{\log P},
\qquad
z_p=\#\{0\le r<p:p\mid b_r\}.
$$

None of the proposed black-box routes presently proves it. The fixed-index dual is an exact and useful reformulation, but the height of the Apéry numbers gives only `O(P^2/log P)`, missing the target by a full factor of `P`. Classical Bombieri–Vinogradov does not apply to the variable-modulus, variable-length Apéry orbit. The center modular-form congruence is real, but the center contributes at most one zero per prime and is therefore already harmless for R1. The clean sieve kernel is a dyadic large-prime-radical estimate for the integers `b_r`; standard sieve theory takes the corresponding local densities as input and cannot manufacture them.

The answers to the five questions are therefore:

1. **Fixed-`n` duality:** exact, but size bounds alone do not de-circularize the problem. After reflection, it gives a particularly clean equivalent vertical-incidence statement.
2. **Bombieri–Vinogradov:** no existing BV theorem applies. A new defining-characteristic Mellin/transfer-orbit level-of-distribution theorem would be needed.
3. **Modular form at the center:** the Ahlgren–Ono congruence is even modulo `p^2`, but it controls only one index. Noncentral indices form a growing family of Mellin-character twists, not coefficients of one fixed modular form.
4. **Sieve barrier:** this is primarily a **local-density/atom barrier**, not the classical parity barrier. Gap-polynomial certificates begin only when there are at least two distinct reflection pairs at the same prime; R1 must count primes carrying exactly one pair.
5. **What distinguishes classical Apéry:** not Lucas congruences, Picard–Fuchs rank, or modularity by themselves. The rigorous distinction currently available is the nondegenerate second-order transfer recurrence and its gap-polynomial algebra, together with the absence of carry-forced zero intervals. This proves `z_p=O(p^{2/3})`, but not R1.

# 0. Two normalization corrections

## 0.1 The exponential growth constant

The dominant characteristic root of the Apéry recurrence at infinity is

$$
\alpha=17+12\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^4=33.970562\ldots,
$$

and

$$
\log b_n=n\log\alpha+O(\log n),
\qquad
\log\alpha=3.52549\ldots.
$$

Thus `3.53` is the approximate **natural logarithmic growth rate**, not the exponential base. For primes in `(P,2P]`, the relevant height inequality is

$$
\#\{P<p\le2P:p\mid b_n\}
\le \frac{\log b_n}{\log P}
\le \frac{(3.526+o(1))n}{\log P}.
\tag{0.1}
$$

Using `log 2` instead counts all distinct prime divisors and is much weaker for this question.

## 0.2 Reflection changes the Poisson model

Since

$$
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p,
$$

off-center zeros occur in pairs. Consequently, the literal variable `z_p` cannot converge to an ordinary Poisson distribution unless one ignores its parity constraint. The Malik–Straub heuristic is more accurately written as

$$
z_p=2K_p+\nu_p,
\qquad
K_p\approx \operatorname{Poisson}(1/2),
$$

where `ν_p` is the center indicator. If the center is negligible, this predicts

$$
\Pr(z_p=0)\to e^{-1/2}=0.6065\ldots,
\qquad
\Pr(z_p=2)\to \tfrac12e^{-1/2}=0.3033\ldots,
$$

and `E[z_p]→1`. A reported no-zero frequency near `e^{-1}=0.3679...` corresponds instead to a Poisson mean `1` for the **pair count**, which would give mean `2` for `z_p`. Those conventions should be reconciled before the numerical law is quoted.

The first-moment target itself is unaffected: it predicts one zero, on average, per prime after counting both members of each reflected pair.

# 1. The fixed-index dual: exact reformulation, insufficient height

For an odd prime `p`, define

$$
\nu_p=\mathbf 1_{\{p\mid b_{(p-1)/2}\}},
$$

and let

$$
u_p^{\mathrm{pair}}
=\#\left\{0\le r<\frac{p-1}{2}:p\mid b_r\right\}.
$$

Reflection gives the exact identity

$$
\boxed{z_p=2\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}}+\nu_p.}
\tag{1.1}
$$

Now sum over `P<p≤2P`. Since `r<(p-1)/2` is equivalent to `p>2r+1`, and every such `r` is less than `P`, one obtains

$$
\boxed{
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}}
=
\sum_{0\le r<P}
\#\{P<p\le2P:\ p>2r+1,\ p\mid b_r\}.
}
\tag{1.2}
$$

The center is automatically acceptable:

$$
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\nu_p
\le \pi(2P)-\pi(P)
\ll \frac{P}{\log P}.
\tag{1.3}
$$

Therefore R1 is equivalent, up to an already admissible center term, to

$$
\boxed{
\sum_{0\le r<P}
\#\{P<p\le2P:\ p>2r+1,\ p\mid b_r\}
\ll \frac{P}{\log P}.
}
\tag{1.4}
$$

This is the best way to “de-circularize” the indexing. It turns a prime-row zero count into a vertical large-prime-divisor problem for the fixed integers `b_r`.

## 1.1 Why the height bound loses a factor `P`

Apply (0.1) termwise in (1.4):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{r<P}
\#\{P<p\le2P:p\mid b_r\}
&\le \frac1{\log P}\sum_{r<P}\log b_r\\
&\ll \frac1{\log P}\sum_{r<P}r\\
&\ll \frac{P^2}{\log P}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.5}
$$

The condition `p>2r+1` improves constants near `r=P`, but not the order of magnitude. Taking the minimum of the height bound and the number of available primes still gives a triangular sum of order `P^2/log P`.

Equivalently, let

$$
\operatorname{rad}_{I}(N)=\prod_{\substack{p\in I\\p\mid N}}p.
$$

Define the vertical dyadic radical mass

$$
\mathcal R(P)=
\sum_{0\le r<P}
\log\operatorname{rad}_{(\max(P,2r+1),\,2P]}(b_r).
\tag{1.6}
$$

Because `log p` is comparable to `log P` on the window,

$$
\text{R1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathcal R(P)\ll P,
\tag{1.7}
$$

again up to the harmless center. The height estimate only gives

$$
\mathcal R(P)\le\sum_{r<P}\log b_r\ll P^2.
\tag{1.8}
$$

Thus the desired theorem is a factor-`P` saving over the total available height. No rearrangement of the same height inequality supplies that saving.

## 1.2 Why pair certificates do not repair the first moment

The project’s gap-polynomial theorem is powerful when one prime divides two coefficients at distinct, non-reflection-related indices. Such a coincidence produces a fixed-gap polynomial congruence and can be charged to a bounded-degree certificate.

R1 is dominated by a different regime. After quotienting by reflection, the expected prime has either zero lower-half zeros or exactly one lower-half zero. A prime with exactly one lower-half zero already contributes two members to `Z_p`, but its reflected partner is automatic and supplies no independent equation. A gap certificate between two **distinct lower-half** zeros appears only when

$$
\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}}\ge2,
$$

i.e. usually when `z_p≥4`.

Consequently, gap-polynomial and resultant methods can bound tails and factorial moments such as

$$
\sum_p (\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}})_2,
$$

but they do not bound the atom

$$
\#\{p:\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}}=1\}.
$$

This is the precise “certificate parity” or atom obstruction recorded in the project. It is not a defect of double counting; it is a lack of any second algebraic condition at a singleton prime.

## 1.3 The corrected random-integer heuristic

For fixed `r` and a prime `p` much larger than `r`, the random model assigns probability `1/p` to `p|b_r`. Hence

$$
\mathbb E\#\{P<p\le2P:p\mid b_r\}
\approx\sum_{P<p\le2P}\frac1p
\sim\frac{\log2}{\log P}.
$$

Summing over `P` indices gives order `P/log P`, not `P/log^2 P`. In the reflection-reduced form, it is even cleaner to sum prime first:

$$
\mathbb E\sum_{P<p\le2P}\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}}
\approx
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\frac{(p-1)/2}{p}
\sim \frac12\bigl(\pi(2P)-\pi(P)\bigr).
\tag{1.9}
$$

Doubling the reflected pairs predicts

$$
\mathbb E\sum_{P<p\le2P}z_p
\sim \pi(2P)-\pi(P).
\tag{1.10}
$$

That is exactly the scale in R1.

# 2. Why Bombieri–Vinogradov does not presently apply

The phrase “Bombieri–Vinogradov for polynomial sequences” hides two very different facts.

For a **fixed polynomial** `F∈Z[x]` of degree `d`, the local root number

$$
\rho_F(p)=\#\{x\bmod p:F(x)=0\}
$$

is already at most `d` for almost every prime. Its first moment over primes is therefore trivially `O(π(P))`; Chebotarev can refine the average and identify a main term. BV-type theorems become relevant to distribution in residue classes or to additional prime variables, not to obtaining the bounded local root count itself.

The map

$$
r\longmapsto b_r\pmod p
$$

is not evaluation of one fixed bounded-degree polynomial in `r`. For `r<p`, write the recurrence as a transfer product

$$
\binom{b_{r+1}}{b_r}
=M_rM_{r-1}\cdots M_0\binom{b_1}{b_0},
\tag{2.1}
$$

with rational `2×2` matrices whose denominators are invertible in the interior range. The product length grows with `r`. Elimination produces polynomials whose degree and height grow with the gap or with `p`; the project’s degree `3(h-1)` gap polynomial is already a visible manifestation of this growth.

Thus the standard bounded-degree polynomial, fixed-extension Chebotarev, and fixed-conductor trace-function frameworks do not apply directly.

## 2.1 The additive Fourier identity does not create cancellation

The stated identity is correct. If

$$
S_p(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}e_p(ab_j),
$$

then additive orthogonality gives

$$
\boxed{
z_p=1+\frac1p\sum_{a\ne0}S_p(a).
}
\tag{2.2}
$$

Summing R1 is therefore equivalent to

$$
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\frac1p\sum_{a\ne0}S_p(a)
\ll \frac{P}{\log P}.
\tag{2.3}
$$

But (2.2) is Fourier inversion of the zero atom; it does not smooth that atom. Even a hypothetical pointwise square-root estimate

$$
|S_p(a)|\ll\sqrt p
$$

would give only

$$
z_p\ll\sqrt p
$$

by absolute summation, not average `O(1)`. To reach R1, one needs cancellation among the `p-1` frequencies, cancellation across primes, or a positive majorant specialized to the zero atom.

Likewise, Parseval gives

$$
\sum_{a\bmod p}|S_p(a)|^2
=p\,E_p,
\qquad
E_p=\#\{(r,s)<p:b_r\equiv b_s\pmod p\}.
\tag{2.4}
$$

This controls collision energy, but an `L^2` bound does not isolate the value `0`. A sequence can have globally acceptable collision energy while one prescribed fiber remains anomalously large. Cauchy–Schwarz applied to (2.2) loses approximately `√p`, exactly the scale one is trying to remove.

## 2.2 What a genuine BV analogue would have to say

A useful theorem would need to be tailored to the triangular incidence family, for example

$$
\sum_{r<P}
\sum_{\substack{P<p\le2P\\p>2r+1}}
\left(\mathbf1_{p\mid b_r}-\frac1p\right)
\ll \frac{P}{\log P},
\tag{2.5}
$$

or, more strongly, with a power-saving error. This is a level-of-distribution statement for the **moving recurrence orbit at its defining characteristic**. It is not a consequence of classical BV.

The fixed toric/K3 realization in the repository packages `b_r mod p` as a multiplicative Mellin coefficient of a bounded-rank geometric system. That is the closest available geometric reformulation. However, the event in R1 is exact reduction modulo the same prime `p` that defines the fiber. Complex Frobenius equidistribution is not enough to detect this divisibility.

The recent Bah–Shuddhodan preprint on uniform Mellin equidistribution over varying finite fields is relevant in spirit: it allows bounded-complexity perverse sheaves while the characteristic varies and averages over multiplicative characters. Its output is complex/unitary equidistribution of Frobenius conjugacy classes. R1 instead asks how often a Mellin trace is zero in the defining residue field. That is a discontinuous `p`-adic atomic condition, and no implication from their stated theorem to (2.5) is presently available.

Hence a BV route is not ruled out as a research program, but the needed theorem would be new and substantially more arithmetic than a black-box application of BV.

# 3. The center modular form and the noncentral indices

Ahlgren and Ono prove, for odd primes,

$$
\boxed{
b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p(f_8)\pmod{p^2},
\qquad
f_8(z)=\eta(2z)^4\eta(4z)^4.
}
\tag{3.1}
$$

This strengthens the mod-`p` congruence in the question. It identifies the center zero with a nonordinary-prime event for one fixed weight-four newform.

For R1, however, this is unnecessary. The center contributes at most one per prime, and (1.3) already places the total center contribution at the desired scale. Even the extreme hypothetical statement “the center vanishes for every prime” would still be compatible with R1.

The real kernel is the collection of off-center reflection pairs.

## 3.1 What does extend beyond the center

There is a modular/motivic extension, but not the one needed for a fixed-form sieve. Let

$$
A_p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b_jt^j.
$$

For `1≤r≤p-2`, multiplicative orthogonality gives

$$
\boxed{
b_r=-\sum_{t\in\mathbb F_p^\times}A_p(t)t^{-r}.}
\tag{3.2}
$$

The project’s toric/K3 and symmetric-square/Asai calculations identify the value function `A_p(t)`—after explicit elementary corrections—with Frobenius traces of a fixed geometric family. Thus every noncentral `b_r` is a multiplicative Mellin transform of that family.

This is a genuine uniform geometric statement. But the exponent `r` labels a character of `F_p^×`, and its order can grow with `p`. The family of Mellin twists changes with `(p,r)`. There is no proved identity expressing all off-center coefficients as Fourier coefficients of one fixed finite collection of classical modular forms of bounded level and weight.

Similarly, the characteristic-zero modular parametrization of the Apéry generating function does not make the coefficients in the Hauptmodul variable into Hecke eigenvalues. Converting a `q`-expansion to an `a(q)`-expansion is triangular and mixes all orders up to `r`.

Therefore the referee answer is:

- **Center:** one fixed newform, exact supercongruence.
- **Noncenter:** one fixed geometric family but a growing collection of Mellin-character twists.
- **Missing theorem:** defining-characteristic anti-concentration of those Mellin traces, averaged over both characters and primes.

# 4. The clean sieve formulation and the actual barrier

The cleanest formulation is the weighted radical statement from (1.6):

```text
Vertical dyadic radical (VDR):

For P→∞,
  Σ_{0≤r<P} log rad_{(max(P,2r+1), 2P]}(b_r) = O(P).
```

By `log p≈log P`, VDR is equivalent to the off-center part of R1. It also has the correct random-model main term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\mathcal R(P)
&\approx
\sum_{P<p\le2P}
\frac{(p-1)/2}{p}\log p\\
&\sim \frac12\sum_{P<p\le2P}\log p
\sim \frac P2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
$$

Doubling reflection pairs gives total logarithmic mass asymptotic to `P`.

## 4.1 Why an ordinary Selberg sieve is circular here

For a standard sequence sieve one introduces, for squarefree `d`,

$$
A_d(N)=\#\{0\le r<N:d\mid b_r\}
$$

and seeks an approximation

$$
A_d(N)=g(d)N+R_d
$$

with multiplicative local density `g`, controlled remainders, and a useful level of distribution.

At a prime `d=p` of size comparable to `N`, the local quantity `A_p(N)` is exactly a lower-half portion of `z_p`. In other words, the local density needed to start the sieve is the quantity R1 is asking us to average. Unlike a fixed polynomial, there is no degree bound supplying `A_p(N)=O(1)`.

Thus standard sieve theory does not prove R1; it assumes an equivalent local-density input.

## 4.2 The barrier occurs before the classical parity problem

The classical parity problem concerns a sieve’s inability to distinguish integers with an even or odd number of prime factors. Here the obstruction is earlier:

- a prime with one lower-half Apéry zero is already a legitimate first-moment atom;
- reflection supplies its second zero automatically;
- all gap/resultant certificates require another independent lower-half zero;
- therefore second and higher factorial moments can be controlled without controlling the first.

A concise way to state the missing input is

$$
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\mathbf1_{\{\nu_p^{\mathrm{pair}}\ge1\}}
\ll \frac{P}{\log P}
$$

with sufficient multiplicity control to recover the mean. The left side is trivially at most the number of primes, so the real content is controlling the average number of pairs after the first. But the expected law places substantial mass at exactly one pair, where algebraic coincidence methods are silent.

Equivalently, one needs an upper-bound sieve for the individual divisibility conditions `p|b_r`, not merely a sieve for simultaneous divisibilities `p|gcd(b_r,b_s)`.

# 5. What distinguishes classical Apéry from the Malik–Straub linear-zero examples?

Malik and Straub prove Lucas congruences for all known sporadic Apéry-like sequences. They also show that Cooper’s additional sporadic sequences—the cases with the extra recurrence parameter `d≠0`—behave very differently: for every prime, a fixed proportion of a terminal part of the first `p` terms is divisible by `p`.

This comparison rules out several overly broad explanations.

## 5.1 Lucas congruences do not distinguish the cases

Both the classical Apéry sequence and the linear-zero examples satisfy Lucas congruences. Lucas automaticity explains how a one-digit zero propagates to higher base-`p` digits; it does not bound the number of one-digit zeros.

## 5.2 Picard–Fuchs rank and modularity do not distinguish them by themselves

The sporadic sequences arise from Picard–Fuchs equations and many have modular parametrizations. Cooper’s examples still have these features while possessing linearly many forced zeros. Therefore neither “rank three,” “modular,” nor “large monodromy” alone implies R1.

Monodromy may support a random-value heuristic after an appropriate trace-function dictionary is established, but no theorem currently turns that heuristic into defining-characteristic coefficient anti-concentration.

## 5.3 The proven distinction is local arithmetic and carry structure

For the classical Apéry numbers:

1. The recurrence is a nonsingular second-order transfer system throughout `0≤r≤p-2`.
2. Consecutive zeros are impossible.
3. A pair of zeros at gap `h` forces a nonzero polynomial congruence of degree `3(h-1)`.
4. These facts give the project’s unconditional `z_p=O(p^{2/3})` theorem.
5. In the binomial sum, the `k=0` summand is `1`; divisibility is therefore caused by cancellation among unit contributions, not because every summand is carry-divisible.

In the Malik–Straub linear-zero cases, their explicit factorial/binomial representations exhibit ranges where carrying and support force divisibility. The phenomenon is termwise or support-theoretic rather than a rare cancellation event.

This makes **carrying structure plus recurrence nondegeneracy** the best rigorous answer among the three choices in the question. It is not yet a complete conceptual classification.

The project also finds that, in the deformation `b_r(λ)`, the Apéry specialization `λ=1` is the unique rational fiber where a generic fourth-order recurrence drops to the special second-order Apéry recurrence. That is compelling project-specific evidence that order reduction is part of the rigidity. It should be presented as structural evidence, not as a proof of R1.

# 6. What theorem would actually close R1?

Three equivalent or near-equivalent targets are now visible.

## 6.1 Vertical radical theorem

Prove VDR:

$$
\sum_{r<P}
\log\operatorname{rad}_{(\max(P,2r+1),\,2P]}(b_r)
\ll P.
$$

This is the cleanest fixed-integer formulation.

## 6.2 Defining-characteristic Mellin first moment

For the fixed rank-three K3/Asai family `W`, prove that its Mellin traces satisfy

$$
\sum_{P<p\le2P}
\#\left\{\chi\in\widehat{\mathbb F_p^\times}:
\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_p\mid H_c^1(W\otimes\mathcal L_\chi))
\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak p_p}
\right\}
\ll \frac P{\log P},
\tag{6.1}
$$

with the precise normalization and endpoint corrections from the project. This is a `p`-adic atomic equidistribution theorem, not ordinary Sato–Tate.

## 6.3 A transfer-orbit large sieve

Prove a triangular level-of-distribution estimate such as (2.5), uniformly in dyadic `P`. This would be a BV analogue specifically for the Apéry nonautonomous transfer orbit.

At present, none of these is available. They are different languages for the same first-moment kernel.

# 7. Referee disposition of the proposed routes

## Route 1: fixed-`n` prime divisors

**Exact reduction, no proof from height.** Keep (1.2) and preferably promote the weighted radical form (1.6) as the canonical vertical formulation. Delete any suggestion that the exponential size of `b_n` alone is near the target; it loses a factor `P`.

## Route 2: Bombieri–Vinogradov

**Not a black-box application.** A new transfer-orbit or defining-characteristic Mellin BV theorem would suffice, but the Apéry recurrence does not fall under fixed-polynomial BV. Standard large-sieve `L^2` estimates do not isolate the zero fiber.

## Route 3: the Ahlgren–Ono center

**Correct but irrelevant to the first-moment upper bound.** It controls one index per prime, whose total is already `O(P/log P)`. The noncentral family is Mellin-geometric rather than one fixed modular form.

## Route 4: sieve formulation

**Use VDR.** The main obstruction is unknown local density at primes of the same size as the index range. This is an atom barrier, not merely the classical parity barrier.

## Route 5: structural distinction

**Use the concrete recurrence/carry distinction.** Lucas, Picard–Fuchs rank, and modularity are shared by counterexamples to any naive bounded-zero principle. The classical Apéry recurrence supplies nonvanishing gap certificates and no forced carry-zero interval; that proves sublinearity but has not yet yielded average boundedness.

# Bottom line

R1 is not currently accessible by rearranging the height bound, by classical Bombieri–Vinogradov, or by the center modular-form congruence. The exact reflection dual shows that the problem is equivalent to an average dyadic large-prime-radical bound for the fixed integers `b_r`:

$$
\boxed{
\sum_{r<P}
\log\operatorname{rad}_{(\max(P,2r+1),\,2P]}(b_r)
\ll P.
}
$$

This formulation removes the apparent circularity but exposes the real missing input: an average theorem for singleton large-prime divisibility of a nonautonomous holonomic sequence. Existing gap-polynomial machinery begins only at the second independent zero and therefore cannot see the dominant one-pair atom.

The most faithful description of the open kernel is:

> **Prove defining-characteristic, all-character Mellin anti-concentration for the Apéry K3/Asai family, or equivalently prove the vertical dyadic radical bound above.**
