# Executive verdict

Theorem A is the more tractable **target**, but only after two corrections.

First, the two proposed conditions are not mathematically equivalent. They are two different sufficient conditions for the same conclusion. Theorem B is a much stronger centered-dispersion statement, while Theorem A is an uncentered upper-tail statement about the prime factors of individual Apéry values. Neither implication holds as written.

Second, the useful version of Theorem A should be truncated to the primes that can actually enter the GCD problem. On a dyadic block

\[
I=(N,2N],
\]

put

\[
\Omega_N(c)
 :=\#\{p:\sqrt N<p\le 2N,\ p\mid b_c\}.
\]

The relevant sufficient condition is

\[
\tag{A_rel}
\sum_{c\in I}\Omega_N(c)^2
=o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}\right).
\]

Indeed, if \(c\in(N,2N]\) and a prime is counted by \(B_{\rm low}(c)\), write

\[
c=qp+r,\qquad r=c\bmod p.
\]

Gessel's Lucas congruence gives

\[
b_c\equiv b_qb_r\pmod p.
\]

Since \(r\in Z_p\), one has \(p\mid b_r\), hence \(p\mid b_c\). Also
\(p>\sqrt c\ge\sqrt N\) and \(p\le c\le2N\). Therefore

\[
B_{\rm low}(c)\le\Omega_N(c).
\]

Consequently

\[
\max_{c\in I}B_{\rm low}(c)
\le
\max_{c\in I}\Omega_N(c)
\le
\left(\sum_{c\in I}\Omega_N(c)^2\right)^{1/2}
=o\!\left(\frac N{\log N}\right).
\]

Thus \((A_{\rm rel})\) closes the all-\(n\) problem by an entirely elementary final step.

By contrast, counting every prime \(p>\sqrt N\), including \(p\gg N\), is unnecessary. Those primes do not divide \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,c)\) and never contribute to \(G_c\). They also lie outside the range in which the sets \(Z_p\) and the quotient-cell geometry are directly relevant. The untruncated Theorem A is therefore a strictly stronger and less well-focused prime-factor problem.

My ranking is:

1. **Best target:** the truncated energy \((A_{\rm rel})\), or still better a direct anti-star bound \(\max_{c\in I}\Omega_N(c)=o(N/\log N)\).
2. **Best geometric hybrid:** use the fixed toric marked coordinate already constructed in `oracleC_result.tex`, but seek only the weak upper-tail saving required by \((A_{\rm rel})\), not a full Poisson dispersion theorem.
3. **Most remote:** the full marked two-characteristic crystalline/FKM package in Theorem B.

Theorem A is elementary in formulation, not presently elementary in proof. It still contains a genuine two-characteristic incidence problem. Nevertheless, its required saving is vastly weaker than the cancellation demanded by Theorem B, so it is the more plausible research target.

# 1. What Theorem A really counts

There is a useful row/column distinction.

Let

\[
M(c,p)=1_{p\mid b_c}
\]

for \(c\in I\) and \(\sqrt N<p\le2N\). Then

\[
\Omega_N(c)=\sum_pM(c,p),
\]

and

\[
\sum_{c\in I}\Omega_N(c)^2
=
\sum_{p,q}
\#\{c\in I:pq\mid b_c\}.
\]

Thus Theorem A is a **row energy**: it counts ordered pairs of distinct characteristics occurring in one value \(b_c\).

It is not the quantity that directly measures how many different Apéry values share a common prime. That transposed energy is

\[
\sum_p\left(\#\{c\in I:p\mid b_c\}\right)^2.
\]

This distinction matters in the present project. The no-consecutive-zero lemma, reflection, and the gap-polynomial bounds control how a fixed prime can recur across different indices; they are principally column information. Pairwise coprimality of the gap polynomials also limits repeated return patterns at one characteristic. None of these facts, by itself, bounds how many *different* primes can divide one fixed integer \(b_c\).

The Wronskian has the same orientation. It gives strong common-divisor restrictions between the two Apéry solutions and between adjacent states, hence no consecutive zeros modulo \(p>3\). It does not prevent one isolated \(b_c\) from having many unrelated prime divisors.

Expanding the energy shows why Theorem A is not automatic:

\[
E_A(N)
=\sum_{c\in I}\Omega_N(c)^2
=\sum_{\sqrt N<p,q\le2N}C_{p,q}(I),
\]

where

\[
C_{p,q}(I)=\#\{c\in I:p\mid b_c,\ q\mid b_c\}.
\]

For distinct \(p,q>\sqrt N\), the product \(pq>N\). A squarefree-modulus sieve would therefore need information at moduli already longer than the interval. This is the same short-interval barrier that appeared in Q339/Q342. CRT identifies residue pairs, but it does not force their representatives to cancel when summed over \((p,q)\).

Still, the target is very weak compared with the random scale. In the relevant prime range,

\[
\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le2N}\frac1p=\log2+o(1).
\]

A random integer model therefore predicts \(\Omega_N(c)=O(1)\) on average and

\[
E_A(N)=O(N).
\]

The desired estimate permits almost \(N/\log^2N\) times more mass. It only excludes rows of near-maximal size \(N/\log N\). In particular, one row with

\[
\Omega_N(c)\asymp\frac N{\log N}
\]

already contributes \(\asymp N^2/\log^2N\) and saturates the threshold. Theorem A is therefore best viewed as an **anti-anchored-star theorem**, not as a demand for Poisson statistics.

Theorem B, on the other hand, aims for a centered second moment near the independent main term. Such a theorem would normally give total variance of order \(N^{1+o(1)}\), much closer to the random prediction. It is far stronger than the little-o saving in \((A_{\rm rel})\).

# 2. Is Theorem A elementary?

## 2.1 Elementary reduction, non-elementary arithmetic

The implication

\[
(A_{\rm rel})\Longrightarrow G_n=e^{o(n)}
\]

is elementary. The statement of \((A_{\rm rel})\) involves only ordinary integer prime divisors of the explicit integers \(b_c\). A proof is not logically required to mention K3 surfaces, sheaves, crystalline cohomology, or trace functions.

However, no general elementary theorem gives the needed energy estimate for values of a holonomic sequence. The issue is an upper bound for simultaneous divisibility

\[
pq\mid b_c
\]

averaged over \(c\), with \(p,q\) both larger than the square root of the averaging length. This is precisely a two-large-prime level-of-distribution question.

A general height argument stops at the wrong scale. Since

\[
\log b_c=(\log(17+12\sqrt2))c+O(\log c),
\]

one has, for \(c\asymp N\),

\[
\Omega_N(c)\ll\frac N{\log N}.
\]

Squaring and summing gives only

\[
E_A(N)\ll\frac{N^3}{(\log N)^2},
\]

which is worse than the target by a factor \(N\).

The target is not a formal consequence of holonomicity. The central binomial coefficients are hypergeometric and hence holonomic, yet every prime \(p\in(n,2n]\) divides \(\binom{2n}{n}\). Therefore

\[
\omega\!\left(\binom{2n}{n}\right)\asymp\frac n{\log n},
\]

and on a dyadic interval their analogous energy has size

\[
\asymp\frac{N^3}{(\log N)^2}.
\]

So even exponential growth plus a first-order holonomic recurrence does not imply Theorem A. The Apéry sequence needs genuinely Apéry-specific input.

## 2.2 Why A is nevertheless more tractable than B

There are three reasons.

1. **A needs only a strict saving over an extremal budget.** It does not require an asymptotic formula, square-root cancellation, or a Poisson law.
2. **A permits exceptional pairs.** Many individual \((p,q)\) correlations may be large as long as they cannot organize into a near-full row.
3. **A can be attacked by integral rigidity.** A many-prime forcing argument, a determinant/radical estimate, or a structural anti-star theorem could prove A without controlling every Fourier mode.

Theorem B asks for substantially more: a uniformly bounded-complexity integral marker, two varying characteristics, moving Mellin characters, and a bilinear dispersion estimate at the same-characteristic divisibility scale \(1/p\). There is no black-box Fouvry--Kowalski--Michel theorem that provides this package.

# 3. What the binomial formula and Lucas property really give

The useful exact consequence of the binomial formula is Gessel's Lucas congruence:

\[
 n=\sum_i n_ip^i
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 b_n\equiv\prod_i b_{n_i}\pmod p.
\]

For \(c\in(N,2N]\) and \(p>\sqrt N\), write

\[
c=qp+r,\qquad 0\le r<p.
\]

Then \(q<2\sqrt N\) and

\[
\tag{3.1}
b_c\equiv b_qb_r\pmod p.
\]

Thus

\[
\tag{3.2}
p\mid b_c
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p\mid b_q\ \text{ or }\ r\in Z_p.
\]

This is a valuable structural split.

- The **remainder branch** \(r\in Z_p\) is exactly the moving-target condition in \(B_{\rm low}\).
- The **leading-digit branch** \(p\mid b_q\), with \(q<2\sqrt N\), is inherited from an earlier Apéry value.

Therefore Theorem A does not make the original moving-target problem disappear. It contains that problem and adds an inherited-factor branch. The split may nevertheless support an induction or quotient-cell argument, because the inherited term has a smaller index.

A convenient schematic inequality is

\[
\Omega_N(c)\le L_N(c)+H_N(c),
\]

where

\[
L_N(c)=\#\{\sqrt N<p\le2N:c\bmod p\in Z_p\}
\]

and

\[
H_N(c)=\#\{\sqrt N<p\le2N:p\mid b_{\lfloor c/p\rfloor}\}.
\]

Then

\[
\Omega_N(c)^2\le2L_N(c)^2+2H_N(c)^2.
\]

The first term is the original horizontal dispersion problem. The second is smaller-index arithmetic, but a size bound alone still permits the critical scale. Thus Lucas gives a clean decomposition, not the missing saving.

It is also important not to overread the digit product. Lucas's theorem is a congruence for the *completed sum* \(b_n\). It does not factor the integer \(b_n\) in \(\mathbb Z\), nor does it identify its prime divisors uniformly as \(p\) varies. The base-\(p\) digits themselves depend on \(p\).

Termwise divisibility of the binomial summands is insufficient because the zero event is usually cancellation among nonzero residues. The smallest example is

\[
b_1=1+4=5,
\]

so modulo \(5\) neither positive summand needs to vanish while their sum does. The p-adic audit in `chatgpt_q32_padic_horizontal_mellin.md` makes the general obstruction exact: the minimum-slope part contains \(\rho+1\) p-adic units, and

\[
p\mid b_r
\]

is exactly cancellation of their reductions. There is never a unique minimum-slope term in the interior range.

For primes \(p>c\), there is no nontrivial base-\(p\) digit decomposition at all: \(c\) is one digit. This is another reason to truncate Theorem A at \(p\le2N\). The primes \(p>2N\) are an ordinary large-prime-factor problem for the fixed integer \(b_c\), unrelated to the zero sets \(Z_p\) needed for the GCD.

# 4. Roth, Siegel, and Apéry's rational approximation do not control Theorem A

There is no direct implication from the quality of the Apéry approximants to an upper bound for

\[
\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_n).
\]

The reason is conceptual: rational approximation sees the size of the **reduced denominator**, whereas Theorem A counts all distinct primes in the unreduced integer \(b_n\).

A prime dividing \(b_n\) but not the corresponding scaled numerator does not improve the rational approximation. It merely makes the denominator more composite. There is no contradiction with irrationality, Siegel's theorem, or Roth's theorem.

The Wronskian

\[
a_nb_{n+1}-a_{n+1}b_n=-\frac6{(n+1)^3}
\]

controls common factors of the two solutions and yields the no-consecutive-zero phenomenon. It is relevant to the GCD \(G_n\), because common factors can shrink a reduced denominator. It does not bound the radical of \(b_n\) itself.

Roth's theorem is unavailable for an even more basic reason: it applies to algebraic irrational numbers, while the algebraicity or transcendence of \(\zeta(3)\) is unknown. Moreover, the natural Apéry approximants have essentially exponent \(2\) relative to their exponential denominator, so they sit at the Roth threshold rather than contradicting it.

Known irrationality-measure bounds for \(\zeta(3)\) can constrain an exponentially large common factor between numerator and denominator by a constant in the exponent. They do not force a subexponential radical or a little-o bound for the number of distinct prime divisors.

Siegel's theorem and the Subspace Theorem are also poorly matched to Theorem A. Their strongest prime-support conclusions concern a fixed curve or a fixed finite set \(S\) of places. Here the prime set moves with \(n\) and may contain \(\asymp n/\log n\) primes. Florian Luca's Subspace-Theorem work on binary holonomic sequences gives lower bounds for the cumulative number of prime divisors appearing in many terms; it does not provide the upper row-energy estimate required here.

Thus the irrationality origin of the Apéry sequence is not presently a route to Theorem A.

# 5. Theorem B: what marker is actually needed?

## 5.1 A specific crystalline class is not necessary merely to mark the zero

The repository has already passed the marker-construction stage in a simpler form. In `problems/3.2/oracleC_result.tex`, the fixed Laurent polynomial

\[
\Lambda(x,y,z)=
\frac{(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)((1+y)(1+z)+xyz)}{xyz}
\]

satisfies

\[
\operatorname{CT}_{x,y,z}\Lambda(x,y,z)^j=b_j.
\]

For \(0\le j\le p-2\), torus orthogonality gives the exact defining-characteristic marker

\[
\tag{5.1}
b_j
\equiv
-\sum_{(x,y,z)\in(\mathbb F_p^\times)^3}
\Lambda(x,y,z)^j
\pmod p.
\]

Equivalently, after choosing the Teichmüller character, this is a rank-one Kummer twist on a fixed threefold with uniformly bounded geometric and Betti complexity. Thus a bounded-complexity **geometric carrier** already exists.

This answers question (d) in an important way:

> The missing part of Theorem B is no longer the existence of some bounded-complexity object whose marked coordinate reduces to \(b_j\). The missing part is horizontal, integral, two-characteristic dispersion for the reductions of that coordinate.

A crystalline/F-isocrystal realization may still be the natural language for retaining p-integral information, but it is not logically mandatory. Any integral compatible object with an exact marked coordinate and the required dispersion theorem would suffice.

## 5.2 Why ordinary trace-function technology does not finish the job

The event is

\[
b_j\equiv0\pmod p,
\]

not the vanishing of a characteristic-zero trace. The repository gives the concrete warning

\[
\widetilde c_{11,5}^{\rm tor}=-33\ne0,
\]

while its reduction modulo the chosen prime above \(11\), and hence \(b_5\), is zero. Standard \(\ell\)-adic trace-function estimates control complex or \(\ell\)-adic trace values. They do not automatically control whether those algebraic integers are divisible by their varying defining characteristic.

The exact package isolated in `oracleB_result.tex` is a marked Mellin object such as

\[
\mathcal M_{p,j}
=R\Gamma_{c,\mathrm{rig}}
\left(U_p,\mathcal V_{{\rm tr},p}
\otimes\mathcal K_{\omega_p^{-j}}\right)
\]

with a coordinate \(c_{p,j}\) satisfying

\[
c_{p,j}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b_j\equiv0\pmod p,
\]

followed by a centered estimate uniform in two varying characteristics and in moving characters \(j\). This is a \(1/p\)-scale horizontal large sieve, not ordinary Frobenius equidistribution.

The Fouvry--Kowalski--Michel trace-function framework, including recent bilinear developments, is powerful when a bounded-conductor trace function is given over one finite field and one averages variables inside that field or over primes with a compatible characteristic-zero trace. It does not by itself supply cancellation for the divisibility of cyclotomic traces modulo their own varying characteristics.

## 5.3 Why a resultant, norm, or companion characteristic polynomial is not a shortcut

- A fixed-gap resultant detects common roots of fixed gap polynomials. Its degree, height, and bad-prime set grow with the gap. It controls repeated zeros within one characteristic, not the zero of one moving Mellin coefficient across two characteristics.
- The factorial-gauged companion product has a matrix entry equal to \((j!)^3b_j\), so it is an exact marker for \(j<p\). But its word length is \(j\), and the direct polynomial degree and conductor grow linearly with \(j\). Fixed matrix size does not mean bounded geometric complexity.
- The characteristic polynomial of the companion product remembers only trace and determinant and generally loses the marked matrix entry.
- Taking the norm of a cyclotomic trace is too coarse. Divisibility by the chosen prime above \(p\) is not the same as characteristic-zero vanishing, and a norm mixes all conjugate primes and has coefficient-field degree growing with \(p\).

Thus the toric marker is already simpler than these alternatives. The bottleneck remains dispersion.

# 6. Third routes and how I would rank them

## Route C1: direct anti-star / truncated radical forcing

The exact pointwise target

\[
\tag{C1}
\max_{c\in(N,2N]}
\#\{\sqrt N<p\le2N:p\mid b_c\}
=o\!\left(\frac N{\log N}\right)
\]

is weaker than Theorem A and already sufficient. A proof could try to show that many large prime divisors of one \(b_c\) impose too many independent rank-drop conditions on an integral transfer object.

This is the cleanest arithmetic route. It is also the closest to the repository's many-prime forcing program. The difficulty is that the known gap-polynomial identities transpose the incidence matrix in the wrong direction: they constrain one prime acting at many indices, whereas C1 needs many primes acting at one index.

A successful argument needs a genuinely cross-prime integer carrier—some determinant, minor, or gcd whose logarithmic height is \(o(N)\) but which is forced to be divisible by the product of all bad primes. No such carrier is currently known.

## Route C2: toric marker plus weak upper-tail dispersion

Use the exact fixed toric coordinate (5.1), but do not seek the full centered asymptotic of Theorem B. It is enough to prove that the total two-prime mass is little-o of the extremal budget:

\[
\sum_{c\in I}
\left(\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le2N}1_{p\mid b_c}\right)^2
=o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right).
\]

This may require only a logarithmic or very small power saving over the deterministic pair count, not square-root cancellation for every bilinear form. Conceptually this is the best compromise between A and B.

Modern results on large prime factors of well-distributed sequences show that level-of-distribution hypotheses can yield Poisson--Dirichlet-type conclusions. But applying that philosophy here requires precisely the missing estimates for

\[
\#\{c\in I:d\mid b_c\}
\]

with squarefree \(d=pq>N\). It is a framework for using dispersion, not a source of the dispersion itself.

## Route C3: Lucas quotient cells plus inherited-factor induction

Use (3.2) to split every relevant prime divisor into the moving remainder branch and the smaller-index leading branch. One can then attempt:

1. a direct moving-target estimate for \(r=c-qp\);
2. induction or amortization for primes inherited from \(b_q\), \(q\ll\sqrt N\);
3. dyadic decomposition in \(q\) and \(p\).

This does not remove the central horizontal problem, but it may prevent Theorem A from paying for all prime factors indiscriminately.

## Function-field analogues

A function-field analogue is useful for discovering the right geometric object, but specialization does not currently give the required integer-prime theorem. The obstruction is the quantifier order:

- the characteristic varies with \(p\);
- the Mellin exponent or coefficient index grows with that same \(p\);
- the target is the single residue \(0\), a shrinking same-characteristic condition.

Equidistribution over extensions of a fixed finite field does not control these simultaneous variations. Any specialization theorem strong enough to do so would already contain the missing horizontal large sieve.

## K3 geometry

The Apéry K3 pencil explains the Picard--Fuchs equation, the toric marker, the symmetric-square structure, and special congruences such as the central modular-form slice. It does not turn coefficient/Mellin zeros into evaluation roots of a bounded-degree Hasse polynomial.

The central index itself is a warning: the congruence

\[
b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p(f_8)\pmod p
\]

reduces one moving zero question to nonordinarity for a non-CM weight-four modular form. The repository records the desired density-zero statement for this slice as open. The full collection of moving indices is harder, not easier.

## Dwork--Katz and p-adic slopes

The defining-characteristic Mellin transform does have an exact Gross--Koblitz/Morita description. But the minimum-slope packet contains \(\rho+1\) units, and

\[
p\mid b_r
\]

is exactly cancellation of their residues. There is no unique lowest-valuation term. Newton polygons and carry patterns therefore do not give an upper bound for the zero event. The remaining problem is a horizontal unit-cancellation sieve, essentially Theorem B in p-adic language.

# 7. What is actually known about prime factors of holonomic sequences?

## 7.1 The elementary general upper bound

Let \(m\ne0\) have \(k=\omega(m)\) distinct prime factors. Then

\[
k!\le\prod_{j=1}^kp_j\le\operatorname{rad}(m)\le|m|,
\]

where \(p_j\) is the \(j\)-th prime. Hence, if

\[
\log|a_n|=O(n),
\]

Stirling's formula gives

\[
\tag{7.1}
\omega(a_n)=O\!\left(\frac n{\log n}\right)=o(n).
\]

This does not use holonomicity.

For the Apéry numbers,

\[
b_n\sim Cn^{-3/2}(17+12\sqrt2)^n,
\]

so already

\[
\omega(b_n)\le
(\log(17+12\sqrt2)+o(1))\frac n{\log n}.
\]

Thus the literal question “is there a sequence tending to infinity for which \(\omega(a_n)=o(n)\) is proved?” has a very easy answer: yes, including the Apéry sequence itself. In fact every exponentially bounded integer sequence satisfies it.

The hard statement relevant here is instead

\[
\omega_{(\sqrt n,n]}(b_n)=o\!\left(\frac n{\log n}\right)
\]

uniformly in \(n\), or its energy strengthening. The elementary estimate gives only \(O(n/\log n)\), with no little-o saving.

If all counted primes exceed \(\sqrt N\), the even simpler product bound gives

\[
(\sqrt N)^{\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_c)}\le b_c,
\]

hence again only

\[
\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_c)=O(N/\log N)
\]

for \(c\asymp N\).

## 7.2 There are holonomic sequences with far fewer prime factors

Holonomicity alone allows every possible behavior.

- \(a_n=2^n\) is C-finite and has \(\omega(a_n)=1\).
- The exponentially growing hypergeometric/holonomic sequence
  \[
  a_n=2^n(n+1)
  \]
  satisfies
  \[
  (n+1)a_{n+1}=2(n+2)a_n
  \]
  and
  \[
  \omega(a_n)\le1+\omega(n+1)
  =O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right).
  \]
  Its terms collectively involve infinitely many primes, yet each term has far fewer than \(n/\log n\) distinct factors.
- Polynomial sequences are holonomic and, by size alone, have \(O(\log n/\log\log n)\) distinct prime factors per term.

So there is no phenomenon that “all holonomic sequences have \(\omega(a_n)\asymp n/\log n\).”

## 7.3 Corrections to the examples in the question

### Fibonacci numbers

The statement

\[
\omega(F_n)=\Theta(n/\log n)
\]

is not a consequence of Carmichael's theorem and is not known uniformly.

Carmichael's primitive-divisor theorem, and its Lucas--Lehmer extension by Bilu--Hanrot--Voutier, say that sufficiently high-index terms have at least one prime divisor not occurring earlier. Since \(F_d\mid F_n\) when \(d\mid n\), this gives roughly

\[
\omega(F_n)\ge\tau(n)-O(1),
\]

not \(n/\log n\). For prime \(n\), this lower bound is only constant. The elementary size bound gives

\[
\omega(F_n)=O(n/\log n).
\]

The gap between these statements is enormous; even infinitude of Fibonacci primes is open.

### Factorials

Here the dense prime support is built in:

\[
\omega(n!)=\pi(n)\sim\frac n{\log n}.
\]

### Central binomial coefficients

For every prime \(n<p\le2n\), Legendre's formula gives

\[
v_p\!\left(\binom{2n}{n}\right)=1.
\]

All prime divisors are at most \(2n\), so

\[
\omega\!\left(\binom{2n}{n}\right)\asymp\frac n{\log n}.
\]

### Catalan numbers

Dividing by \(n+1\) removes only \(O(\log n/\log\log n)\) distinct primes, so the Catalan numbers still have \(\Theta(n/\log n)\) distinct prime factors.

These factorial-ratio sequences are special because their product formulas force whole intervals of primes. They are not a good random model for the Apéry sum.

## 7.4 The strongest directly relevant published Apéry result is a lower bound

Luca and Shparlinski proved that, for a density-one set of indices,

\[
\omega(b_n)>c_0\log\log\log n
\]

and

\[
P^+(b_n)>c_0(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2}.
\]

These results use the recurrence and Lucas-type congruences. They show that Apéry numbers are not supported on an exceptionally tiny fixed prime set. They are lower bounds, whereas Theorem A is a strong uniform upper-tail statement.

Luca's theorem on binary holonomic sequences similarly proves that, under nondegeneracy hypotheses, the product of the nonzero terms up to \(N\) has at least \(c\log N\) distinct prime divisors. Again this is cumulative and in the opposite direction from Theorem A.

I do not know a published theorem that gives the truncated energy \((A_{\rm rel})\), or a uniform \(o(n/\log n)\) upper bound for the relevant large-prime divisors of the Apéry numbers.

# 8. Why the Apéry sequence has a reason to differ from factorial products

The correct heuristic comparison is with random integers of the same size, not with factorials or binomial products.

A random integer of size about

\[
\exp(\kappa n)
\]

has typical total number of prime factors about

\[
\log\log(\exp(\kappa n))\sim\log n.
\]

In the GCD-relevant window, the expected number of prime divisors is

\[
\sum_{\sqrt n<p\le n}\frac1p=\log2+o(1).
\]

This matches the observed constant-order behavior of the Apéry zero incidences. The Apéry numbers are defined by a sum of many terms, so divisibility is governed by cancellation modulo \(p\), and there is no evident product formula forcing every prime in a macroscopic interval to divide \(b_n\).

The empirical facts

\[
\text{mean }|Z_p|\approx1,
\]

reflection symmetry, and Poisson-like zero-pair statistics all support this random-integer model. They do not prove the required uniform anti-star theorem, because a sparse sequence of exceptional indices could still carry many aligned primes.

Thus the Apéry sequence has a credible reason to behave differently from \(n!\), \(\binom{2n}{n}\), and Catalan numbers: it is an additive period/diagonal sequence rather than a factorial product. But this is a heuristic distinction, not yet a theorem strong enough for \((A_{\rm rel})\).

# 9. Direct answers to (a)--(f)

## (a) Is Theorem A elementary compared with Theorem B?

Its statement and its implication for \(G_n\) are elementary, and it does not logically require sheaf theory. Its proof is not currently elementary: after squaring, it is a two-large-prime divisibility problem beyond the square-root level of distribution. The truncated version \((A_{\rm rel})\) is nevertheless more tractable than B because it only needs a little-o saving over the extremal row budget.

## (b) Can the explicit binomial formula constrain which primes divide \(b_n\)?

It gives the exact Lucas factorization

\[
b_{qp+r}\equiv b_qb_r\pmod p,
\]

which splits the problem into an inherited smaller-index branch and the original moving-zero branch. This is useful for quotient-cell decompositions and induction. It does not factor \(b_n\) over the integers, and modular cancellation prevents a termwise prime-divisor classification. No known argument from the explicit sum gives Theorem A.

## (c) Can Siegel or Roth exploit the irrationality approximation?

Not for Theorem A. Those theorems control reduced denominators, integral points, or fixed \(S\)-unit structures. Theorem A counts all primes dividing \(b_n\), most of which need not divide the corresponding numerator. Roth additionally requires algebraicity of the approximated number, which is unknown for \(\zeta(3)\). The Wronskian controls common factors, not the full prime support of \(b_n\).

## (d) Must the marked object be crystalline?

No. The repository's fixed toric/Kummer construction already gives a bounded-complexity exact marked coordinate reducing to \(b_j\). A crystalline or integral compatible-system refinement may be useful because the zero event is divisibility by the defining characteristic. Resultants, norms, and companion characteristic polynomials do not remove the moving-index and two-characteristic dispersion problem.

## (e) Is there a third route?

Yes: the most credible third route is a **hybrid anti-star theorem**—use the toric marker or integral transfer identities only to prove the weak truncated energy \((A_{\rm rel})\), rather than full FKM-style centered dispersion. Function-field, K3, and p-adic methods are valuable for constructing the marker and exposing structure, but none currently supplies the required horizontal upper-tail bound after specialization.

## (f) What is the simplest known prime-factor result for a holonomic sequence?

If \(\log|a_n|=O(n)\), then the elementary primorial bound gives

\[
\omega(a_n)=O(n/\log n)=o(n).
\]

So the literal question has many trivial affirmative examples, including the Apéry numbers. If the intended question is the much stronger

\[
\omega(a_n)=o(n/\log n),
\]

there are still elementary holonomic examples such as \(2^n(n+1)\), but no generic theorem for nondegenerate holonomic sequences. The claimed Fibonacci asymptotic is not known. The factorial and central-binomial examples have \(\Theta(n/\log n)\) because of their special product structure, not because they are holonomic.

# 10. Recommended next theorem

I would record the following as the primary target:

> **Relevant large-prime row-energy theorem.** Uniformly for dyadic \(I=(N,2N]\),
> \[
> \sum_{c\in I}
> \left(
> \#\{p:\sqrt N<p\le2N,\ p\mid b_c\}
> \right)^2
> =o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}\right).
> \]

Then split it by Lucas into remainder and inherited branches. Seek only enough cancellation to defeat a full row; do not initially demand the Poisson main term.

An even sharper program is to prove directly that a row of size \(\varepsilon N/\log N\) forces an integer determinant or minor of height \(e^{o(N)}\) to be divisible by \(e^{\Omega(N)}\). This would be a true many-prime forcing theorem and would bypass the entire high-moment hierarchy.

Theorem B should remain a long-range geometric program. Its marker component is now concrete; its dispersion component is the genuinely new theorem. It is not the shortest route presently visible.
