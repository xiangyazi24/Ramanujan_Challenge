## 1. Ordinary polynomial versus function identity

The operator is defined on functions on \(\mathbf F_p\). Therefore the statement
\[
Hc=-(\theta/2)\delta_0
\]
is a function equality. The degree-\(<p\) representative of \(\delta_0\) is
\[
\delta_0(t)=1-t^{p-1}.
\]
Consequently the canonical representative of the source is
\[
-(\theta/2)(1-t^{p-1}).
\]

However an ordinary polynomial representative of the same function is only determined modulo \(t^p-t\). Since the centered operator applied to a degree \(\le p-3\) polynomial can have degree at most \(p+1\), the full ordinary identity is
\[
Hc(t)= -\frac\theta2(1-t^{p-1}) +(t^p-t)(A t+B),
\]
for some scalars \(A,B\in\mathbf F_p\).

There are no higher multiples: \(\deg(Hc)\le p+1\), while a multiple of \(t^p-t\) of degree larger than one would already have degree at least \(p+2\).

Because \(c\) is even and the centered operator preserves the appropriate parity shift, the coefficient of the forbidden odd top term is constrained. Writing
\[
(t^p-t)(At+B)=A(t^{p+1}-t^2)+B(t^p-t),
\]
the possible top coefficients are therefore exactly:

* coefficient of \(t^{p+1}\): \(A\);
* coefficient of \(t^p\): \(B\);
* coefficient of \(t^{p-1}\): \(\theta/2\) from the source term.

No further coefficient is forced by the function equation alone.

## 2. The reciprocal assumption

Assume the hypothetical reciprocal polynomial exists:
\[
r=1/k,
\]
with even canonical degree at most \(p-3\). Since
\[
c=k-\theta d,
\]
and
\[
d(t)=\delta_1+\delta_{-1},
\]
pointwise one has
\[
cr=1-\theta dr.
\]
At \(t=\pm1\), \(k(\pm1)=3\), hence
\[
dr=\frac23d.
\]
Thus
\[
cr=1-\frac{\theta}{3}d
\]
as a function.

This identity gives a product relation only in the quotient ring of functions; it does not imply an ordinary polynomial identity without adding multiples of \(t^p-t\).

## 3. Attempted coefficient contradiction

The tempting comparison is to use
\[
\deg(c),\deg(r)\le p-3
\]
and conclude that the product has degree at most \(2p-6\). But the equality
\[
cr=1-\frac\theta3d
\]
is only modulo \(t^p-t\). Therefore the missing term is
\[
cr-(1-\theta d/3)=(t^p-t)Q(t),
\]
where
\[
\deg Q\le p-6.
\]
The high coefficients of \(cr\) can be absorbed by this unknown \(Q\). Hence the degree bounds alone do not force a contradiction.

The sparse equation for \(Hc\) similarly determines only the class modulo \((t^p-t)\). The coefficients \(A,B\) above are extra ordinary-polynomial data not fixed by the function equation.

## 4. Endpoint recurrence check

The endpoint rows of the recurrence determine the function values near the singular points and reproduce:

\[
k(0)=-1,\qquad k(\pm1)=3,
\]

and hence

\[
c(0)=-1,\qquad c(\pm1)=3+2\theta.
\]

These conditions constrain the low coefficients of the even representatives but do not determine the unknown quotient polynomial \(Q\) in
\[
cr-1+\theta d/3=(t^p-t)Q.
\]

Thus endpoint recurrence does not close the gap.

## 5. Conclusion

The exact ordinary identity is
\[
\boxed{
Hc(t)=-\frac\theta2(1-t^{p-1})+(t^p-t)(At+B)
}
\]
with the displayed top coefficients.

Combining this with the hypothetical low-degree reciprocal gives only the pair of quotient-ring identities
\[
\boxed{cr\equiv1-\theta d/3\pmod{t^p-t}}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{Hc\equiv-\theta(1-t^{p-1})/2\pmod{t^p-t}}.
\]

A genuine coefficient contradiction cannot be extracted from these statements alone. The obstruction is not a sign error in the source term; it is the undetermined ordinary-polynomial multiples of \(t^p-t\). Any successful nonvanishing proof must control those quotient terms (for example through an additional determinant, recurrence uniqueness statement, or a stronger ordinary-degree estimate).

Therefore the correct audit result is: **the proposed coefficient comparison is underdetermined; no contradiction follows without one more independent constraint.**
