# 2. Interval continuants and the exact transfer matrix

Put

\[
A_j(X):=P(X+j),
\qquad
B_j(X):=(X+j)^6,
\]

and define the interval continuant \(K_{a,b}(X)\) by

\[
K_{a,a-2}=0,
\qquad
K_{a,a-1}=1,
\]

\[
K_{a,b}=A_bK_{a,b-1}-B_bK_{a,b-2}
\quad(b\ge a).
\tag{2.1}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{N_r(X)=K_{1,r-1}(X).}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Let

\[
T_j(X):=
\begin{pmatrix}
 A_j(X)&-B_j(X)\\
 1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

A direct induction gives the exact matrix identity

\[
\boxed{
T_b\cdots T_a
=
\begin{pmatrix}
 K_{a,b}&-B_aK_{a+1,b}\\
 K_{a,b-1}&-B_aK_{a+1,b-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

Taking determinants yields the discrete Wronskian/Casoratian identity

\[
\boxed{
K_{a,b-1}K_{a+1,b}
-K_{a,b}K_{a+1,b-1}
=
\prod_{j=a+1}^{b}(X+j)^6.
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

This is the exact identity to test first.  It is important that its right-hand side is **not zero** away from the cut-edge columns.

The ordinary split identity for continuants is

\[
\boxed{
K_{a,b}
=K_{a,m-1}K_{m,b}
-B_mK_{a,m-2}K_{m+1,b}
}
\tag{2.5}
\]

for every \(a\le m\le b\).  Taking \(a=1\), \(m=h\), and \(b=h+k-1\) gives

\[
\boxed{
N_{h+k}(X)
=N_h(X)N_{k+1}(X+h-1)
-(X+h)^6N_{h-1}(X)N_k(X+h).
}
\tag{2.6}
\]

Splitting at \(m=h+1\) gives the complementary form

\[
\boxed{
N_{h+k}(X)
=N_{h+1}(X)N_k(X+h)
-(X+h+1)^6N_h(X)N_{k-1}(X+h+1).
}
\tag{2.7}
\]

These formulas involve no division and are valid over \(\mathbb Z[X]\), in every characteristic, and at cut edges.

# 3. What the Wronskian gives at a common root

Let \(x\in\mathbb F_p\) satisfy

\[
N_h(x)=0,
\qquad
N_k(x+h)=0.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Equations (2.6)--(2.7) give the genuine extra zero

\[
\boxed{N_{h+k}(x)=0.}
\tag{3.2}
\]

This is useful as a restart identity, but it does not yet create a smaller scalar.

Call the root unpolluted if

\[
x+j\ne0\pmod p
\qquad(1\le j\le h+k).
\tag{3.3}
\]

Applying (2.4) to the first and second blocks shows that at an unpolluted common root

\[
N_{h-1}(x),\quad N_{h+1}(x),
\quad N_{k-1}(x+h+1),
\quad N_{k+1}(x+h-1)
\]

are all nonzero.  In particular, the two transfer blocks have one-dimensional kernel lines and nonzero complementary entries.

Differentiate (2.6).  At a common root all terms containing \(N_h(x)\) or \(N_k(x+h)\) disappear, leaving

\[
\boxed{
N_{h+k}'(x)
=N_h'(x)N_{k+1}(x+h-1)
-(x+h)^6N_{h-1}(x)N_k'(x+h).
}
\tag{3.4}
\]

The two coefficients in (3.4) are units at an unpolluted root.  There is no recurrence identity forcing their two tangent terms to cancel or forcing either derivative to vanish.  Thus

```text
common root of N_h and shifted N_k
    => root of N_(h+k),

but not
    => multiple root of N_(h+k).
```

Consequently a discriminant of \(N_{h+k}\) is not automatically divisible by \(p\).  This is the first exact obstruction to the most natural Wronskian/discriminant attack.

There is an equally exact resultant version.  Reducing (2.6) modulo \(N_h\), then using multiplicativity of the resultant, gives

\[
\boxed{
\left|\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_{h+k})\right|
=
|N_h(-h)|^6
\left|\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_{h-1})\right|
\mathcal R_{h,k},
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

where

\[
\mathcal R_{h,k}
:=\operatorname{Res}_X(N_h(X),N_k(X+h))>0.
\tag{3.6}
\]

Signs depend on the standard resultant convention and disappear in (3.5).  Thus replacing the second block by the longer polynomial \(N_{h+k}\) does not compress the elimination integer: it multiplies the old resultant by an endpoint sixth power and an adjacent resultant.

# 4. Reflection symmetry

The cubic has the exact antisymmetry

\[
\boxed{P(-X-1)=-P(X).}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The symmetric tridiagonal determinant representation of \(N_r\), or induction using the reversed continuant recurrence, gives

\[
\boxed{
N_r(-X-r-1)=(-1)^{r-1}N_r(X).
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Hence

\[
x\longmapsto -x-h-k-1
\tag{4.3}
\]

is a bijection from the common roots for \((h,k)\) to those for \((k,h)\).  Therefore

\[
\boxed{c_p(h,k)=c_p(k,h).}
\tag{4.4}
\]

This is a valid exact identity.  It does not identify the prime families: family A for \((h,k)\) is not family B for \((k,h)\), and the corresponding upper primes are reflected to different affine forms.  Thus (4.4) saves at most a symmetry factor in an unrestricted sum, not an exponent or logarithm in the selected family sum.

# 5. Exact cut-edge factorization and the positive boundary theorem

Define

\[
a_n:=N_{n+1}(-1).
\tag{5.1}
\]

The recurrence at \(X=-1\) and the standard Apéry recurrence give

\[
\boxed{a_n=(n!)^3b_n,}
\tag{5.2}
\]

where equivalently

\[
b_n=
\sum_{r=0}^{n}
\binom nr^2\binom{n+r}{r}^2.
\tag{5.3}
\]

No asymptotic input is needed.  Formula (5.2) follows because both sides have initial values \(1,5\) and satisfy

\[
n^3b_n=P(n-1)b_{n-1}-(n-1)^3b_{n-2}.
\tag{5.4}
\]

## 5.1 Endpoint factorization

At \(X=-j\), the off-diagonal entry \((X+j)^3\) in the tridiagonal continuant vanishes, so the determinant splits into left and right blocks.  Reflection (4.2) then gives, for every \(1\le j\le r\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
N_r(-j)
&=(-1)^{j-1}N_j(-1)N_{r-j+1}(-1)\\
&=(-1)^{j-1}((j-1)!)^3b_{j-1}((r-j)!)^3b_{r-j}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.5}
\]

This proves (V3).

## 5.2 Charging every boundary root separately

Let \(x=-j\in\partial_{h+k}\).

- If \(1\le j\le h\), then a common root must in particular satisfy \(p\mid N_h(-j)\).  Since \(p>h+k\), (5.5) implies
  \[
  p\mid b_{j-1}b_{h-j}.
  \tag{5.6}
  \]
- If \(h<j\le h+k\), put \(\ell=j-h\).  Then \(1\le\ell\le k\), and the second common-root equation gives
  \[
  p\mid N_k(-\ell)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  p\mid b_{\ell-1}b_{k-\ell}.
  \tag{5.7}
  \]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
c_p^\partial(h,k)
\le{}&
\sum_{j=1}^{h}
 \mathbf1_{p\mid b_{j-1}b_{h-j}}\\
&+
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}
 \mathbf1_{p\mid b_{\ell-1}b_{k-\ell}}.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{5.8}
\]

The right side has one term for each possible boundary root.  If one prime divides several displayed products, it is counted several times, exactly as required.

Summing over any set of shell primes, including the three selected prime families, gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_p c_p^\partial(h,k)\log p
&\le
2\sum_{r=0}^{h-1}\log b_r
+2\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\log b_r.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.9}
\]

The elementary binomial estimates

\[
\binom nr\le2^n,
\qquad
\binom{n+r}{r}\le2^{n+r}\le4^n
\]

give

\[
1\le b_n\le(n+1)64^n.
\tag{5.10}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\sum_p c_p^\partial(h,k)\log p
\le{}&
\log64\,[h(h-1)+k(k-1)]\\
&+2\log(h!k!).
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{5.11}
\]

Since every selected \(p>T/7\), division by \(\log(T/7)\) proves (V2).

This is a genuine logarithmic improvement over the unsaturated resultant height for the cut-edge sector.  It is also immune to the known long polluted columns: if one Apéry prime divides many endpoint values, (5.8) records every root occurrence and (5.9) pays for every occurrence separately.

# 6. Exact center roots

The reflection identity says that the centered polynomial

\[
N_r\left(Y-\frac{r+1}{2}\right)
\]

is odd when \(r\) is even.  Hence, for even \(r\),

\[
\boxed{2X+r+1\mid N_r(X).}
\tag{6.1}
\]

For the first block, the possible central common root is

\[
x=-\frac{h+1}{2}
\quad(h\ \mathrm{even}).
\tag{6.2}
\]

For the second block it is

\[
x=-h-\frac{k+1}{2}
\quad(k\ \mathrm{even}).
\tag{6.3}
\]

These are distinct as rational points.  Over an odd shell prime they yield at most two residue classes.  Assigning any overlap with \(\partial_{h+k}\) to the boundary sector first gives

\[
c_p^{\rm ctr}(h,k)\le2.
\tag{6.4}
\]

More explicitly, if \(h\) is even, the first center is common precisely when

\[
p\mid
2^{3(k-1)}N_k\left(\frac{h-1}{2}\right),
\tag{6.5}
\]

and the symmetric statement holds when \(k\) is even.  These are the exact center factors in the current resultant ledger.  They may contain primes much larger than \(h+k\), so they cannot simply be divided out of the resultant; but their **root multiplicity contribution** is at most two and is therefore already controlled at support scale.

# 7. The full multiplicity-preserving resultant bound

The separated root strips are

\[
-h<\operatorname{Re}\alpha<-1
\quad(N_h(\alpha)=0),
\tag{7.1}
\]

and

\[
-h-k<\operatorname{Re}\beta<-h-1
\quad(N_k(\beta+h)=0).
\tag{7.2}
\]

They are disjoint.  Hence

\[
\mathcal R_{h,k}
=
\operatorname{Res}_X(N_h(X),N_k(X+h))
\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Let \(S_{h,k}\) be the nominal Sylvester matrix.  If the reductions have \(c_p(h,k)\) distinct common affine roots, their affine gcd has degree at least \(c_p(h,k)\), so

\[
\operatorname{nullity}_{\mathbb F_p}(S_{h,k}\bmod p)
\ge c_p(h,k).
\tag{7.4}
\]

If

\[
s_1\mid s_2\mid\cdots\mid s_N
\]

are its Smith invariants, then

\[
\#\{i:p\mid s_i\}
=\operatorname{nullity}(S_{h,k}\bmod p),
\]

whereas

\[
v_p(\det S_{h,k})=
\sum_i v_p(s_i).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{c_p(h,k)\le v_p(\mathcal R_{h,k}).}
\tag{7.5}
\]

This remains valid if leading coefficients vanish modulo \(p\); false points at infinity can only increase the Sylvester nullity and valuation.

Hadamard's inequality applied to the Sylvester matrix, or the root-strip product bound, gives

\[
\boxed{
\log\mathcal R_{h,k}
\ll hk\log(h+k+2).
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

Consequently

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\substack{T/7<p\le T/3\\p\ \mathrm{prime}}}
 c_p(h,k)\log p
&\le\log\mathcal R_{h,k},\\
\sum_{\substack{T/7<p\le T/3\\p\ \mathrm{prime}}}
 c_p(h,k)
&\ll
\frac{hk\log(h+k+2)}{\log(T/7)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.7}
\]

Imposing primality of \(q_1,q_2\) only decreases the sum.  This proves the first branch of (V1) with full occurrence multiplicity.

\tag{9.1}
\]

Thus the large-upper-gap condition merely imposes

\[
k>7D\ (A),
\qquad
h>7D\ (B),
\qquad
h+k>7D\ (C).
\tag{9.2}
\]

The product and balance cuts impose

\[
hk>\Psi,
\qquad
|h-k|>E.
\tag{9.3}
\]

None is an upper bound on \(\max(h,k)\).  For example, on any current threshold regime with

\[
M(T),E,D=o(T),
\qquad
\Psi=o(T^2),
\tag{9.4}
\]

the fixed-ratio box

\[
p\approx T/4,
\qquad
h\approx0.06T,
\qquad
k\approx0.08T
\tag{9.5}
\]

has \(h+k<p\), fixed positive balance defect, macroscopic product, and a macroscopic upper gap in each family.  The divisibility-by-seven conditions can be met by \(O(1)\) adjustments.  This is only a deterministic admissible parameter box; no unproved assertion about infinitely many simultaneous primes is being made.

On such a box the two branches of (V1) have scales

\[
\text{resultant: }T^2,
\qquad
\text{three-linear sieve: }T^2/L^3
\]

per \((h,k)\), up to singular-series factors.  The exact identities therefore do not improve the current sieve envelope on the generic macroscopic sector.

# 10. Audit of proposed smaller integers

## 10.1 Longer-gap resultant

Equation (3.5) shows that replacing \(N_k(X+h)\) by \(N_{h+k}(X)\) makes the integer larger by explicit factors.  This route is closed exactly, not heuristically.

## 10.2 Discriminant of \(N_{h+k}\)

Equation (3.4) shows that a common split root is not forced to be multiple.  Hence

\[
p\mid\operatorname{Disc}(N_{h+k})
\]

is not a consequence of the two common-root equations.

## 10.3 A reduced Bézout integer

Let \(g_{h,k}>0\) generate

\[
(N_h(X),N_k(X+h))\cap\mathbb Z.
\]

A common root modulo \(p\) implies \(p\mid g_{h,k}\).  But if there are \(c_p\) distinct common roots, evaluating one Bézout identity at all of them still gives only the single congruence \(p\mid g_{h,k}\).  It does not give \(p^{c_p}\mid g_{h,k}\).  Therefore the reduced Bézout integer is a support carrier and loses the requested occurrence multiplicity.

## 10.4 Principal subresultants

If the gcd degree is at least \(c\), then the first \(c\) principal subresultant coefficients vanish modulo \(p\).  Multiplying those coefficients can restore \(p^c\), but the product contains the ordinary resultant and additional factors, so its aggregate height is no smaller.  Selecting one subresultant again records only support or a threshold event.

## 10.5 Center and leading-coefficient saturation

The exact center factors and the leading-coefficient rank lattice account for real repeated factors of \(\mathcal R_{h,k}\).  However:

```text
- center factors may themselves contain primes p>h+k;
- leading-coefficient factors can coexist with genuine affine roots;
- the Smith invariant carrying an infinity contribution need not split
  integrally from the invariant carrying an affine contribution;
- dividing a visible factor from the determinant can therefore remove
  valuation needed for an actual common root.
```

The centered norm left after visible factors are removed is an uncontrolled algebraic norm.  The current theorem gives no upper bound for its \((h+k)\)-rough part smaller than the full

\[
O(hk\log(h+k))
\]

height.  Finite factor tables cannot supply such a theorem.

## 10.6 Why the full determinant is the present multiplicity carrier

In Smith form, \(c_p\) is bounded by the number of invariant factors divisible by \(p\).  The determinant pays at least one \(p\) for each such invariant factor.  A smaller Euclidean scalar usually keeps only the largest invariant factor or the common gcd of minors and therefore pays \(p\) once.  A genuine compression theorem would have to preserve the **number** of \(p\)-divisible invariant factors while proving a substantially smaller Archimedean product.  No current recurrence identity does this.

This is the first precise algebraic obstruction.

# 11. Audit of a second sieve retaining occurrences

Fix \((h,k)\) and a family.  Let

\[
w(n)
:=
\mathbf1_{L_{0,\sigma}(n)\ \mathrm{prime}}
\,c_{L_{0,\sigma}(n)}(h,k).
\tag{11.1}
\]

To sieve the two upper forms with occurrence multiplicity, one would need, for squarefree \(d\), estimates for

\[
A_d
:=
\sum_{\substack{n\ \mathrm{in\ the\ shell}\\
 d\mid L_{1,\sigma}(n)L_{2,\sigma}(n)}}
 w(n)
\tag{11.2}
\]

of the form

\[
A_d
=
\frac{\rho_\sigma(d)}{d}A_1+r_d,
\tag{11.3}
\]

with a summable remainder to a positive level of distribution.

The resultant provides only the total mass estimate

\[
A_1
\le
\frac{\log\mathcal R_{h,k}}{\log(T/7)}.
\tag{11.4}
\]

It gives no factor \(1/d\) in (11.2).  The large prime divisors of one integer may all lie in the residue classes which avoid the two forbidden upper-form roots modulo every small prime used by the sieve.  Total logarithmic height does not imply residue-class equidistribution.

Even if (11.3) were supplied, using only the trivial base mass

\[
A_1\ll mT/L
\tag{11.5}
\]

and then applying a two-dimensional sieve recovers

\[
A_1/L^2\ll mT/L^3,
\]

which is exactly the existing three-linear bound.  Thus a second sieve alone is not a strict improvement.  It must be accompanied by a genuine saving in the occurrence-weighted base mass or by an excess-multiplicity theorem.

# 12. The smallest new Apéry-specific input

Let

\[
\mathscr T_\sigma
=
\{(h,k,p):
(h,k)\ \mathrm{is\ in\ the\ exact\ residual},
\ p,q_1,q_2\ \mathrm{are\ prime\ in\ family}\ \sigma\}.
\]

Define selected support and selected second factorial moment by

\[
V_\sigma
:=
\sum_{(h,k,p)\in\mathscr T_\sigma}
\mathbf1_{c_p(h,k)>0},
\tag{12.1}
\]

\[
\mathcal M_{2,\sigma}
:=
\sum_{(h,k,p)\in\mathscr T_\sigma}
\binom{c_p(h,k)}2.
\tag{12.2}
\]

For every integer \(c\ge1\),

\[
c\le1+\binom c2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{(h,k,p)\in\mathscr T_\sigma}c_p(h,k)
\le V_\sigma+\mathcal M_{2,\sigma}.
}
\tag{12.3}
\]

The existing three-linear sieve already gives

\[
V_\sigma
\ll
\frac{T}{L^3}
\sum_{(h,k)\ \mathrm{in\ residual}}
\mathfrak S_\sigma(h,k),
\tag{12.4}
\]

with no \(m\)-factor.  Hence the clean sufficient new theorem is

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal M_{2,\sigma}
\ll
\frac{T}{L^3}
\sum_{(h,k)\ \mathrm{in\ residual}}
\mathfrak S_\sigma(h,k).
}
\tag{12.5}
\]

A little-\(o\) or power-saving version is even stronger.  Formula (12.5) is not a restatement of the desired first moment: it counts pairs of **distinct root witnesses** for one selected prime triple.

## 12.1 Exact double-incidence eliminant candidate

Put

\[
f(X)=N_h(X),
\qquad
g(X)=N_k(X+h),
\]

and

\[
\Theta_{h+k}(X)=\prod_{j=1}^{h+k}(X+j).
\]

The relevant off-diagonal unpolluted incidence scheme is obtained from

\[
(f(X),g(X),f(Y),g(Y))
\tag{12.6}
\]

by saturating with respect to

\[
(X-Y)\Theta_{h+k}(X)\Theta_{h+k}(Y).
\tag{12.7}
\]

Because the two root strips are disjoint in characteristic zero, an integral Macaulay/Fitting presentation of this saturated scheme is torsion.  A zero-th Fitting integer \(\mathfrak D_{h,k}^{(2)}\ne0\) can therefore be defined.  For every shell prime away from the saturation denominators, its \(p\)-adic length dominates the number of ordered distinct unpolluted root pairs:

\[
v_p(\mathfrak D_{h,k}^{(2)})
\ge
c_p^\circ(h,k)(c_p^\circ(h,k)-1).
\tag{12.8}
\]

This is the correct exact algebraic object for (12.2).  The obstruction is height: the generic Macaulay construction, or the exterior-compound construction from the Sylvester matrix, has height larger than the original resultant by a factor polynomial in \(h+k\).  It supplies no useful average bound.

A genuinely new Apéry-specific theorem would be a compressed-height estimate for these double-incidence Fitting factors, for example an aggregate bound at the support-sieve scale

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{(h,k)\ \mathrm{in\ residual}}
\log|\mathfrak D_{h,k}^{(2)}|
\ll
\frac{T}{L^2}
\sum_{(h,k)\ \mathrm{in\ residual}}
\mathfrak S_\sigma(h,k).
}
\tag{12.9}
\]

Dividing (12.8) by \(2\log(T/7)\) would then prove (12.5), after adding the already-controlled boundary and center pieces.

The literal weakest numerical input is a bound on

\[
\sum_{\mathscr T_\sigma}(c_p-1)_+,
\]

because the desired sum equals support plus this excess.  The second factorial moment (12.2) is the first robust algebraic moment which dominates that excess and has a concrete double-incidence interpretation.
