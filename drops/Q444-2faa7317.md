# Q444: Can we prove a power-saving bound for D_a(n)?

## Summary

The current unconditional information about Apéry numbers does **not** appear sufficient to prove

\[
D_a(n)=O(n^{1-\delta})
\]

for any fixed \(\delta>0\). The distinctness observation is correct and useful, but after exploiting it the remaining problem is essentially a shifted prime-divisor problem for many different Apéry numbers. Known recurrence/Casoratian properties do not create enough global sparsity.

The best unconditional bound available from standard Apéry facts is still essentially

\[
D_a(n)\le \pi(n)-\pi(\sqrt n)=O(n/\log n).
\]

A nontrivial improvement requires a new ingredient: distribution of the zero sets \(Z_p\), a sieve theorem for the trace sequence, or a strong bound on simultaneous divisibility of unrelated Apéry terms.

---

## 1. The distinct residue lemma is correct

For \(p>\sqrt n\), define

\[
m_p=n\bmod p.
\]

If \(m_p=m_q=m\), then both \(p,q\mid n-m\). Since

\[
0\le n-m<n
\]

and

\[
pq>\sqrt n\sqrt n=n,
\]

we get a contradiction unless \(p=q\).

Therefore the map

\[
p\mapsto m_p
\]

is injective on the primes counted by \(D_a(n)\).

This is a genuine structural fact.

---

## 2. Why growth of \(b_m\) does not help

For every counted prime,

\[
p\mid b_{m_p},\qquad p>\sqrt n.
\]

Hence

\[
b_{m_p}\ge p>\sqrt n.
\]

Using

\[
\log b_m=\Theta(m),
\]

we only obtain

\[
m_p\gg \log n.
\]

But there are still \(n-O(\log n)\) possible values of \(m\), so this removes only a negligible initial segment.

The distinctness of the \(m_p\)'s does not combine with exponential growth because the selected indices can lie anywhere in \([c\log n,n]\).

---

## 3. Product/valuation arguments are one-sided

The tempting estimate

\[
\prod_i p_i\mid \prod_i b_{m_i}
\]

is valid, but gives only

\[
K\frac{\log n}{2}\le \sum_i \log b_{m_i}
       \ll \sum_i m_i.
\]

The right side can be as large as \(O(Kn)\), because the distinct indices may be close to \(n\).

Thus this gives no upper bound on \(K=D_a(n)\).

The argument would become powerful only if one could prove that the selected residues \(m_i\) are typically small or have a density restriction. There is currently no such theorem.

---

## 4. Casoratian isolation is insufficient

The Casoratian property implies that for large primes,

\[
p\mid b_m \implies p\nmid b_{m+1}.
\]

Hence the zero set \(Z_p\) has no consecutive elements.

However, \(D_a(n)\) samples one zero from many different primes:

\[
p_i\mid b_{m_i}.
\]

The indices \(m_i\) can be far apart, and the isolation condition only controls pairs involving the same prime. It gives no restriction on

\[
\{m_i: p_i\text{ varies}\}.
\]

Therefore it cannot reduce \(D_a(n)\) below the trivial scale.

---

## 5. What stronger statement would close P3.2?

Any of the following would suffice:

### (A) Uniform bound on modular zeros

A theorem such as

\[
|Z_p|\le p^{1-\epsilon}
\]

is not directly enough, but combined with equidistribution of residues could give savings.

### (B) Large-prime divisor sparsity

A theorem of the form

\[
\#\{p\in(P,2P]:p\mid b_m\}\ll P^{1-\epsilon}
\]

uniformly for all relevant \(m\) would immediately imply the desired estimate.

### (C) Correlation/sieve input

The missing ingredient is a bound on the events

\[
b_{n\bmod p}\equiv0\pmod p
\]

across primes. This is exactly the type of information suggested by the observed bounded maximum \(D_a(n)\le5\).

---

## Conclusion

The new observation that the residues \(n\bmod p\) are distinct is important, but by itself it does not yield a power-saving bound. The current unconditional theory of Apéry numbers gives:

\[
\boxed{D_a(n)=O(n/\log n)}
\]

and no known route to

\[
\boxed{D_a(n)=O(n^{1-\delta})}
\]

without an additional distribution theorem.

The empirical boundedness of \(D_a\) strongly suggests that the missing theorem is not about individual Apéry divisors, but about **cross-prime independence/equidistribution of the maps \(p\mapsto n\bmod p\) and the modular zero sets \(Z_p\)**. This aligns with the other P3.2 discoveries (alpha equidistribution and Weyl-sum directions).
