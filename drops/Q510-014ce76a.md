# Poisson for most \(m\) does **not** imply the all-\(n\) P3.2 bound

## Verdict

No: ordinary Poisson convergence of the law of

\[
K_X(m)=\#\{X<p\le 2X:m\bmod p\in Z_p\},
\qquad 0\le m<X^2,
\]

under uniform random \(m\) does **not** imply a pointwise bound for every \(m\), and hence does not by itself imply

\[
D(n)=o(n/\log n)\qquad\text{for every }n.
\]

A single exceptional \(m\) has probability only \(X^{-2}\). Total-variation convergence \(d_{\rm TV}\to0\), even very convincing Poisson statistics for all typical \(m\), can completely miss such a singleton. The all-\(n\) theorem is precisely a **sub-singleton tail / pointwise derandomization** problem.

There is, however, an important positive statement:

> A sufficiently strong **factorial-moment** estimate does convert an average over \(m<X^2\) into a worst-case bound, because one exceptional point already contributes its full \(k\)-th factorial power to the moment sum.

This is exactly the role of \((\mathrm{HM})_j\) in the current repository. In fact, the current pointwise theorem needs **one fixed order \(j>6\)**, not the whole hierarchy. A Poisson theorem would close P3.2 only if it were strengthened enough to imply such factorial-moment control (or a still stronger exponential-tail bound). Weak convergence or total-variation convergence alone is insufficient.

## 0. Two preliminary corrections

### 0.1 The dyadic mean is not asymptotic to \(1\) when \(Z(p)=O(1)\)

By definition,

\[
\lambda_X=\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac{Z(p)}p.
\]

If \(Z(p)\) has bounded average, say heuristically \(Z(p)\approx c\), then

\[
\lambda_X
\sim c\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac1p
\sim \frac{c\log2}{\log X},
\]

so \(\lambda_X\to0\), not \(1\). What stays of constant size is the total mean over the full large-prime interval:

\[
\sum_{\sqrt n<p\le n}\frac{Z(p)}p
\approx c\bigl(\log\log n-\log\log\sqrt n\bigr)
=c\log2.
\]

This scaling correction does not change the most-versus-all obstruction. The obstruction persists even if one modifies the setup so that \(\lambda_X\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)\).

For the **proved** input in the repository, one only has the uniform zero-count bound \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\), which gives

\[
\lambda_X\ll \frac{X^{2/3}}{\log X}.
\]

That \(2/3\) is exactly why the current pointwise theorem requires a moment order \(j>6\).

### 0.2 P3.2 does not require every \(j>6\)

The theorem labeled `thm:hm-pointwise` in the P3.2 manuscript, and the corollary in `problems/3.2/new_sections.tex`, use **any one fixed** \(j>6\); for example \(j=8\). Thus direct Poisson convergence is not needed merely to avoid proving infinitely many separate hypotheses. Proving \((\mathrm{HM})_8\) already suffices.

## (A) Can Poisson convergence plus concentration give a pointwise bound?

### A.1 Ordinary convergence: no

Let

\[
\Omega_X=\{0,1,\dots,\lfloor X^2\rfloor-1\},
\qquad N_X=|\Omega_X|\asymp X^2.
\]

For a threshold \(T_X\), define

\[
B_X(T_X)=\{m\in\Omega_X:K_X(m)\ge T_X\}.
\]

Since the probability measure is uniform on \(N_X\) points,

\[
B_X(T_X)\ne\varnothing
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Pr(B_X(T_X))\ge \frac1{N_X}\asymp X^{-2}.
\]

Therefore, to deduce that **no** exceptional \(m\) exists, it is enough—and essentially necessary at this level—to prove

\[
\Pr(K_X\ge T_X)<X^{-2}.
\tag{1}
\]

A statement such as

\[
\Pr(K_X\ge T_X)=o(1)
\]

only says that the exceptional set has density zero. It may still contain one point, or \(X^{1.9}\) points, and neither possibility is compatible with an all-\(m\) conclusion.

Now suppose only that

\[
d_{\rm TV}\bigl(\mathcal L(K_X),\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda_X)\bigr)
=\delta_X\longrightarrow0.
\]

Then for every threshold \(T\),

\[
\Pr(K_X\ge T)
\le
\Pr(\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda_X)\ge T)+\delta_X.
\tag{2}
\]

To turn (2) into a pointwise result, one needs

\[
\Pr(\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda_X)\ge T_X)+\delta_X<X^{-2}.
\tag{3}
\]

Thus \(d_{\rm TV}=o(1)\) is nowhere near enough. The additive approximation error itself must be below the probability mass of a singleton, roughly \(X^{-2}\), unless one has some separate structural argument ruling out isolated atoms.

For a genuine Poisson variable \(Y\) and \(T\ge\lambda\), the Chernoff bound gives

\[
\Pr(Y\ge T)\le \left(\frac{e\lambda}{T}\right)^T.
\]

When \(\lambda=O(1)\), taking

\[
T\asymp \frac{\log X}{\log\log X}
\]

makes the Poisson tail smaller than \(X^{-2}\). Consequently, the following **would** give a pointwise bound:

\[
d_{\rm TV}=o(X^{-2})
\quad\text{together with the Poisson tail estimate.}
\]

It would imply

\[
M_X:=\max_{m<X^2}K_X(m)
=O\!\left(\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}\right).
\]

But an \(o(X^{-2})\) total-variation error is vastly stronger than ordinary Poisson convergence and much stronger than what the raw CRT endpoint error suggests.

### A.2 A direct concentration inequality can work—but only at sub-singleton scale

A concentration inequality for the **actual** \(K_X\), not merely for the approximating Poisson law, would be enough if it gave

\[
\Pr(K_X\ge T_X)<X^{-2}.
\]

For example, a Poisson-type exponential-moment bound valid in a sufficiently large positive-\(\theta\) range,

\[
\mathbb E e^{\theta K_X}
\le
\exp\!\bigl(C\lambda_X(e^\theta-1)\bigr),
\tag{4}
\]

would yield a Chernoff tail and hence a pointwise maximum bound after choosing \(T_X\) so that the resulting probability is \(<X^{-2}\).

However, (4) is not a consequence of pairwise correlations or of weak Poisson convergence. It controls all high-order clustering simultaneously. In the language of the current project, it is essentially a strong anti-tail theorem and would imply the desired \((\mathrm{AT}'')\)-type conclusion.

## A concrete counterexample: an anchored star is Poisson for most \(m\) but has a huge maximum

The cleanest way to see the logical gap is to remain inside the same residue-set model.

For every prime \(p\in(X,2X]\), take

\[
Z_p=\{0\}.
\]

Then

\[
K_X(m)=\#\{p\in(X,2X]:p\mid m\}.
\]

Let

\[
P_X=\#\{p:X<p\le2X\}\sim \frac{X}{\log X}.
\]

At the single point \(m=0\),

\[
K_X(0)=P_X\sim\frac{X}{\log X}.
\tag{5}
\]

For every \(0<m<X^2\), however, \(K_X(m)\le1\). Indeed, if distinct \(p,q>X\) both divided \(m\), then \(pq\mid m\), but \(pq>X^2>m\), impossible.

Thus the random law under uniform \(m<X^2\) is concentrated on \(0\) and \(1\), apart from one atom of probability \(X^{-2}\) at the huge value \(P_X\). Its mean parameter is

\[
\lambda_X\sim\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac1p
\sim\frac{\log2}{\log X}\to0.
\]

Since both this law and \(\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda_X)\) converge in total variation to the point mass at \(0\), one has

\[
d_{\rm TV}\bigl(\mathcal L(K_X),\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda_X)\bigr)\to0,
\]

while (5) is as large as the trivial maximum.

This example also shows exactly where higher moments enter. Since \(K_X(m)\le1\) away from \(m=0\), for every \(j\ge2\),

\[
\frac1{X^2}\sum_{m<X^2}(K_X(m))_j
=\frac{(P_X)_j}{X^2}.
\]

Hence

\[
\frac1{X^2}\sum_m(K_X(m))_2
\asymp \frac1{(\log X)^2},
\]

which is still of the natural second-moment scale, whereas for \(j\ge3\),

\[
\frac1{X^2}\sum_m(K_X(m))_j
\asymp \frac{X^{j-2}}{(\log X)^j},
\]

which explodes. Pair statistics can tolerate the star; higher factorial moments detect it immediately.

This is also a useful response to the hypothesis \(\lambda_X\sim1\). Start with any hypothetical residue family whose count converges to \(\operatorname{Pois}(1)\), and add one common anchored residue \(m_*\bmod p\) to every \(Z_p\). For uniform \(m<X^2\), the probability that any newly added residue is hit is at most

\[
\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac1p=O(1/\log X),
\]

so the limiting Poisson law is unchanged. But at \(m=m_*\), all the added residues fire and

\[
K_X(m_*)\ge P_X\sim X/\log X.
\]

Therefore even a nondegenerate Poisson limit with mean \(1\) cannot rule out a worst-case anchored star.

For the genuine Apéry sets, \(0\notin Z_p\) because \(b_0=1\), so this is not being proposed as an Apéry construction. It is a logical countermodel showing that **no theorem based only on the distribution for random \(m\)** can prove the all-\(m\) statement. One must use additional Apéry-specific structure to exclude analogous high-incidence atoms.

## A.3 Total variation does not automatically give moment convergence

The statement

> “Poisson convergence gives \(\mathbb E[K_X^j]\sim\mathbb E[Y_X^j]\) for every fixed \(j\)”

is false without uniform integrability or an equivalent tail condition.

Total variation controls expectations of bounded test functions. The function \(k\mapsto(k)_j\) is unbounded, and the deterministic bound

\[
K_X\le P_X\asymp X/\log X
\]

grows with \(X\).

If \(Y_X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda_X)\) and

\[
d_{\rm TV}(\mathcal L(K_X),\mathcal L(Y_X))=\delta_X,
\]

then truncation only yields a bound of the shape

\[
\mathbb E(K_X)_j
\le
\mathbb E(Y_X)_j
+O\!\left(P_X^j\delta_X\right)
=\lambda_X^j+O\!\left(P_X^j\delta_X\right).
\tag{6}
\]

Thus, in the empirical scaling \(P_X\asymp X/\log X\) and \(\lambda_X\asymp1/\log X\), transferring the \(j\)-th factorial moment from total variation would require roughly

\[
\delta_X\ll X^{-j+o(1)},
\]

not merely \(\delta_X=o(1)\). The singleton-star example is exactly the failure of uniform integrability behind (6).

There are three materially different statements:

1. **Distributional/TV Poisson convergence:** controls bounded events; insufficient for P3.2.
2. **Poisson convergence of factorial moments:**
   \[
   \mathbb E(K_X)_j=(1+o(1))\lambda_X^j
   \]
   for fixed \(j\); this does imply \((\mathrm{HM})_j\).
3. **Uniform probability-generating-function or exponential-moment convergence in a neighborhood extending past \(1\):** this controls the far tail and can imply all fixed moments, sometimes even a pointwise maximum.

Only the latter two are strong enough for the project. Proving them is not a free consequence of Chen–Stein total variation; they contain precisely the high-clustering information that is currently missing.

## The exact bridge already present in the repository: \((\mathrm{HM})_j\Rightarrow\) pointwise

Write

\[
\Sigma_j(X)=\sum_{0\le m<X^2}(K_X(m))_j.
\]

The hypothesis \((\mathrm{HM})_j\) is of the form

\[
\Sigma_j(X)
\le X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^j.
\tag{HM_j}
\]

Let

\[
M_X=\max_{m<X^2}K_X(m).
\]

If \(M_X\ge j\), choose an \(m\) attaining the maximum. Then

\[
(M_X-j+1)^j
\le (M_X)_j
\le \Sigma_j(X).
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
M_X
\le j-1+X^{2/j+o(1)}\lambda_X.
}
\tag{7}
\]

This is the precise average-to-worst-case mechanism. A singleton with value \(T\) contributes \((T)_j\) to the unnormalized moment sum, so a high moment cannot hide it.

Using the proved zero-count input

\[
\lambda_X\ll \frac{X^{2/3}}{\log X},
\]

(7) gives

\[
M_X
\ll
\frac{X^{2/3+2/j+o(1)}}{\log X}+O_j(1).
\tag{8}
\]

After dyadic decomposition of \((\sqrt n,n]\) and a harmless constant-factor enlargement of the interval \(m<X^2\) at the bottom dyadic block,

\[
D(n)
\ll
\frac{n^{2/3+2/j+o(1)}}{\log n}+O_j(\log n).
\tag{9}
\]

Therefore

\[
2/3+2/j<1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
j>6
\]

implies

\[
D(n)=o(n/\log n)
\]

for **every** \(n\). Equivalently, the repository's `thm:hm-pointwise` gives

\[
\log G_n\ll n^{2/3+2/j+o(1)}=o(n).
\]

So the most-versus-all gap is fillable by a high moment. But that high moment is exactly the nontrivial input; it does not follow from ordinary Poisson convergence.

If one could first prove the much stronger uniform estimate \(Z(p)=O(1)\), then \(\lambda_X\ll1/\log X\), and the simplified argument would need only one fixed \(j>2\). The current rigorous exponent is \(2/3\), hence \(j>6\).

## (B) Why the Lovász local lemma does not address this direction

The Lovász local lemma is an existence theorem. In a suitable probability space it can show

\[
\Pr(\text{no bad event occurs})>0,
\]

and hence that **there exists** an assignment or object avoiding all bad events.

Here the desired statement is the opposite type:

\[
\text{for every }m<X^2,\quad K_X(m)<T_X.
\]

Equivalently, the deterministic bad set \(B_X(T_X)\) must be empty. A local lemma cannot turn “bad events are rare” into “no point is bad.” There is no reverse LLL of the required form: one can always concentrate all rare failures on one exceptional assignment.

At most, an LLL or union-bound argument in a random model of the residue sets could prove that **some randomly generated residue family** has no bad \(m\). That would not prove the statement for the fixed Apéry zero sets \(Z_p\).

The relevant integer-valued principle here is much simpler:

\[
|B_X(T_X)|=X^2\Pr(K_X\ge T_X).
\]

To force \(|B_X(T_X)|=0\), prove the right-hand side is \(<1\). This is why the necessary probability scale is \(X^{-2}\).

## (C) Is the gap genuinely harder than average statements?

Yes. It is a genuine arithmetic derandomization problem.

The distinction is:

- **Annealed / average statement:** choose \(m\) uniformly and show \(K_X(m)\) usually has Poisson behavior.
- **Quenched / pointwise statement:** prove that no deterministic \(m\) supports an abnormally large cluster of congruence hits across primes.

A set of one or finitely many exceptional integers is invisible to every \(o(1)\)-level distribution theorem but is fatal to an all-\(n\) result. The current repository identifies this exact issue as the anti-tail or atom problem, with

\[
(\mathrm{AT}''):\qquad
M_X\ll \lambda_X X^{o(1)}.
\]

The anchored-star construction above is the canonical obstruction: pair incidences may all be of the right size while many pair incidences are concentrated on the same single \(m\). Excluding that concentration requires higher-order information or a direct structural theorem about the Apéry recurrence.

### What would be sufficient?

Any one of the following would close the gap:

1. **One factorial-moment estimate \((\mathrm{HM})_j\) for a fixed \(j>6\).** This is already enough for the current P3.2 pointwise theorem.
2. **Poisson convergence in factorial moments** for every fixed \(j\), uniformly with the normalization required by \((\mathrm{HM})_j\). Then choose \(j=7\) or \(8\).
3. **A total-variation/local approximation with sub-singleton error**, together with a Poisson far-tail estimate:
   \[
   d_{\rm TV}=o(X^{-2}).
   \]
4. **A direct exponential-moment or Chernoff bound** for \(K_X\) strong enough to make the bad-set probability \(<X^{-2}\).
5. **The direct anti-tail statement \((\mathrm{AT}'')\).** The moment-collapse proposition in `problems/3.2/new_sections.tex` then gives all \((\mathrm{HM})_j\) from the already proved second moment.

Items 2–4 may be described as “strong Poisson theorems,” but they are substantially stronger than convergence in law. They already encode the difficult control of rare high-multiplicity atoms.

## A correction to the Chen–Stein formulation

The standard Arratia–Goldstein–Gordon local-dependence theorem does not set

\[
b_2=\sum_{i\ne j}\mathbb E[X_iX_j]
\]

over all pairs. Instead, one chooses dependency neighborhoods \(B_i\) and uses quantities of the form

\[
b_1=\sum_i\sum_{j\in B_i}p_ip_j,
\qquad
b_2=\sum_i\sum_{\substack{j\in B_i\\j\ne i}}\mathbb E[X_iX_j],
\]

plus, in the general nonlocal formulation, a term measuring residual dependence outside \(B_i\). For independent indicators one may take \(B_i=\{i\}\), so \(b_2=0\), not \(\sum_{i\ne j}p_ip_j\approx\lambda^2\).

In the present deterministic-residue model, all indicators are functions of the same random integer \(m\), and there is no evident sparse dependency graph. Raw pairwise CRT counts do not prevent all pair intersections from being concentrated at one common \(m\). Thus even a successful total-variation Chen–Stein theorem would still leave the pointwise issue unless it came with the much stronger tail control described above.

The phrase “two moments suffice” in the Chen–Stein literature refers to a Poisson-approximation theorem under an appropriate local-dependence/coupling framework; it does **not** mean that arbitrary pairwise correlations determine the extreme tail or the maximum over a finite sample space.

## Direct answers

### (A)

No pointwise bound follows from ordinary Poisson convergence or \(d_{\rm TV}=o(1)\). A pointwise bound follows only from a tail estimate below the singleton scale \(X^{-2}\), from a sufficiently high factorial moment, or from an equivalent strong exponential-moment estimate. In particular,

\[
(\mathrm{HM})_j
\Longrightarrow
M_X\le j-1+X^{2/j+o(1)}\lambda_X,
\]

and one fixed \(j>6\) closes the current P3.2 theorem.

### (B)

The Lovász local lemma points in the wrong logical direction. It can establish existence of a good assignment; P3.2 requires absence of every bad \(m\). It cannot remove a singleton exceptional integer.

### (C)

The gap is genuine. Poisson for most \(m\) is an average theorem; all-\(n\) P3.2 is a pointwise anti-clustering theorem. It is fillable only by an additional quantitative tail or structural input. A genuinely strong Poisson theorem—factorial-moment convergence, uniform generating-function control, or \(o(X^{-2})\) local/TV error—would close P3.2, but ordinary Poisson convergence does not bypass the \((\mathrm{HM})_j\)/\((\mathrm{AT}'')\) bottleneck.

## Bottom line

\[
\boxed{
\text{Poisson in distribution for random }m
\not\Rightarrow
D(n)=o(n/\log n)\text{ for all }n.
}
\]

What is needed is not merely “Poisson for most \(m\),” but **Poisson with uniform integrability/high moments**, or a direct **sub-singleton anti-tail bound**. In the current repository architecture, the shortest sufficient target remains either one \((\mathrm{HM})_j\) with \(j>6\), or the stronger pointwise hypothesis \((\mathrm{AT}'')\).
