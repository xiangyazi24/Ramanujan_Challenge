# Second-order recurrences modulo primes and the Apéry zero set

## Short answer

The recurrence viewpoint is useful, but classical Skolem–Mahler–Lech theory does **not** directly prove the desired bound.

The reason is that Skolem–Mahler–Lech studies a **fixed** linear recurrence over a field of characteristic zero (or a fixed algebraic dynamical system), whereas the Apéry recurrence modulo p is a different recurrence for every prime p, with coefficients varying with r modulo p.

The problem is closer to studying rational points of a varying family of algebraic dynamical systems over finite fields.

## 1. Projective recurrence interpretation

The Apéry recurrence

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}
\]

can be rewritten as a first-order recurrence on the projective ratio

\[
x_n=b_n/b_{n-1}\in \mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p).
\]

Away from singular indices, x_n satisfies a Möbius-type update

\[
x_{n+1}=A_n-\frac{B_n}{x_n},
\]

where A_n,B_n are rational functions of n modulo p.

A zero b_n=0 corresponds to the orbit hitting the point x_n=0 (or infinity depending on normalization).

Thus Z_p is an orbit-hitting problem for a non-autonomous dynamical system on P^1(F_p).

## 2. Why Skolem–Mahler–Lech does not apply directly

For a constant-coefficient recurrence

\[
u_{n+d}=c_{d-1}u_{n+d-1}+\cdots+c_0u_n,
\]

Skolem–Mahler–Lech says the zero set is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

The Apéry recurrence is different:

* coefficients depend on n;
* the recurrence degenerates at n=p-1;
* the characteristic polynomial changes with p;
* we need a uniform average over primes.

There is no fixed eigenvalue ratio whose multiplicative order controls the zeros.

## 3. The right finite-field analogue

The natural object is not a linear recurrence sequence, but a rank-2 difference equation with varying coefficients.

The recurrence defines a matrix cocycle

\[
\binom{b_{n+1}}{b_n}=M(n)\binom{b_n}{b_{n-1}}.
\]

Over F_p, the zero problem asks how often this cocycle sends the initial vector into a coordinate hyperplane.

A possible route would be:

1. prove strong monodromy of the cocycle;
2. show orbit equidistribution in P^1(F_p);
3. deduce that hitting 0 has frequency about 1/p.

Since there are p possible positions n, this predicts |Z_p|=O(1).

However, this requires an effective equidistribution theorem for the recurrence cocycle, which is not currently known.

## 4. Relation to the existing P3.2 geometry

The existing bound

\[
|Z_p|\ll p^{2/3}
\]

comes from algebraic restrictions (gap polynomials / Frobenius geometry), not from recurrence dynamics.

The desired M1 estimate

\[
\sum_{p\le N}|Z_p|\ll \pi(N)
\]

would require showing that the orbit has random-like hitting statistics for most primes.

This is analogous to a finite-field Sato–Tate statement for the recurrence cocycle.

## 5. Could the recurrence help?

Yes, but likely through a different theorem than Skolem–Mahler–Lech.

Promising directions:

* p-adic/difference Galois theory for the Apéry recurrence;
* monodromy of the associated Picard–Fuchs connection;
* expansion properties of the induced action on P^1(F_p);
* trace-function estimates after expressing the recurrence solution geometrically.

The recurrence may provide a conceptual explanation for why zeros are sparse, but the missing ingredient is a uniform finite-field orbit-distribution theorem.

## Conclusion

The answer is:

* A second-order recurrence interpretation is real and useful.
* Classical Skolem–Mahler–Lech does not give an average zero bound because the recurrence varies with p and with n.
* The correct analogue is an orbit-hitting/equidistribution problem for a finite-field matrix cocycle.
* Proving M1 from this route would require new effective monodromy or expansion results, beyond standard linear recurrence theory.
