# Newton polygons for the odd Apéry gap factors

## Executive verdict

The ordinary one-edge Newton-polygon strategy is **not** a viable all-
\(k\) proof.  In fact, the first nontrivial calibration already gives a
rigorous obstruction:

> The degree-six polynomial \(f_5\) is irreducible over \(\mathbf Q\), but
> no prime \(p\) and no integer shift \(u\mapsto u+c\) can give a full
> Newton-polygon side of coprime slope and horizontal length six.

Thus neither unshifted Dumas nor shifted Eisenstein/Dumas can prove the
whole family.  They do work sporadically: \(f_3\) is irreducible over
\(\mathbf Q_3\) by a single side of slope \(1/3\).

The only genuinely live p-adic direction is more sophisticated:

1. factor the reduction of the primitive polynomial at a structural prime;
2. discard the prime immediately if the reduction has two coprime residual
   blocks, because Hensel already makes the polynomial reducible over
   \(\mathbf Q_p\);
3. when the reduction is one power \(\phi^e\), run the
   \(\phi\)-adic Newton polygon and then the higher-order Montes/Ore
   algorithm;
4. if no single prime gives local irreducibility, intersect the possible
   factor-degree sets supplied by several primes.

This **Montes plus multi-prime degree sieve** is the most realistic local
route.  It is still not a solved black-box template: the missing theorem
would be a uniform description, in \(k\), of the residual factors and
higher-order polygons.  The known mod-\(p\) DDF certificates solve each
individual \(k\), but not that quantifier problem.

There is also an important correction to the proposed endpoint analysis:

\[
p>n\quad\not\Longrightarrow\quad v_p(b_n)=0.
\]

For example,

\[
b_3=1445=5\cdot17^2,
\]

and both \(5\) and \(17\) exceed \(3\).  Integrality means
\(v_p(b_n)\ge0\), not that \(b_n\) is a \(p\)-adic unit.

## 1. Primitive normalization and notation

Put

\[
k=2n+1,\qquad d=\deg f_k=3n,
\]

and write

\[
f_k(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_i u^i.
\]

Before drawing any polygon, replace \(f_k\) by its primitive part

\[
F_k(u)=\frac{f_k(u)}{\operatorname{cont}(f_k)}.
\]

Dividing by the content translates every valuation point vertically by the
same amount, so it does not change slopes, but it is essential when applying
primitive irreducibility criteria and when interpreting endpoint valuations.
In the recurrence normalization \(\widetilde W_0=0\),
\(\widetilde W_1=-1\), the raw centered polynomial already has nontrivial
content at some small indices; for example the raw \(f_9\) has content
\(3\).

The leading coefficients satisfy

\[
L_{j+1}=34L_j-L_{j-1},\qquad L_0=0,\quad L_1=-1,
\]

hence

\[
L_j=-\frac{(17+12\sqrt2)^j-(17-12\sqrt2)^j}{24\sqrt2}.
\]

For odd \(j\), this is also the leading coefficient in the centered
variable \(u\).  Thus the leading coefficients form a Lucas sequence.  A
prime divisor of \(L_j\), even a primitive divisor, only makes the top
coefficient vanish modulo \(p\); it gives no automatic divisibility of the
other coefficients.

The constant term is

\[
a_0=(-1)^{n+1}(n!)^6b_n^2,
\]

so, before removal of content,

\[
v_p(a_0)=6v_p(n!)+2v_p(b_n).
\]

For \(p>n\), this reduces to

\[
v_p(a_0)=2v_p(b_n),
\]

which can be positive and is always even.

## 2. What a full one-edge polygon actually requires

For a primitive polynomial

\[
F(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_i u^i\in\mathbf Z[u],
\]

the \(p\)-adic Newton polygon is the lower convex hull of

\[
(i,v_p(a_i)),\qquad 0\le i\le d,
\]

with zero coefficients assigned valuation \(+\infty\).

Suppose that the whole polygon is the single segment joining
\((0,A)\) and \((d,B)\).  If

\[
\gcd(d,A-B)=1,
\]

then the slope has reduced denominator \(d\).  Every irreducible
\(\mathbf Q_p\)-factor has degree divisible by \(d\), so \(F\) is
irreducible over \(\mathbf Q_p\), and hence over \(\mathbf Q\).

If \(B=0<A\), the exact coefficient inequalities are

\[
v_p(a_i)\ge
\left\lceil\frac{A(d-i)}d\right\rceil
\qquad(0\le i\le d).
\]

In particular **every nonleading coefficient is divisible by \(p\)**.  If
\(A=2\) and \(d\) is odd, then the coefficients in the lower half must in
fact be divisible by \(p^2\), while every remaining nonleading coefficient
must be divisible by \(p\).  Thus a large prime divisor of \(b_n\) does not
come close to supplying a one-edge polygon unless it also divides a long
block of other coefficients.

The same statement at the other end says that if \(A=0<B\), then every
nonconstant coefficient is divisible by \(p\).

### A universal, very cheap obstruction

For the primitive polynomial define the two one-sided contents

\[
C_k^-:=\gcd(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{d-1}),
\qquad
C_k^+:=\gcd(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_d).
\]

**Lemma.** If \(C_k^-=C_k^+=1\), then no prime gives a full nonzero-slope
Newton polygon for the unshifted polynomial.

**Proof.** Because \(F_k\) is primitive, some coefficient has valuation
zero.  On a single segment of nonzero slope, the minimum occurs at exactly
one endpoint; an interior valuation-zero point would lie below the segment.
If the right endpoint is the minimum, all coefficients except the leading
one have positive valuation, so \(p\mid C_k^-\).  If the left endpoint is
the minimum, then \(p\mid C_k^+\).  Both are impossible when the two gcds
are one.  \(\square\)

This should be the first computation, before constructing a single Newton
polygon.

An exact recurrence computation gives

\[
(C_3^-,C_3^+)=(1,3),
\]

whereas

\[
C_k^-=C_k^+=1
\]

for every odd \(k=5,7,\ldots,31\).  The raw contents in this range are one,
except

\[
\operatorname{cont}(f_9)=3,
\quad
\operatorname{cont}(f_{15})=5,
\quad
\operatorname{cont}(f_{21})=7,
\quad
\operatorname{cont}(f_{27})=9;
\]

after primitive normalization the two one-sided contents are still one.
This finite calculation is not an all-\(k\) proof, but it strongly suggests
the useful negative lemma

\[
C_k^-=C_k^+=1\qquad(k\ge5\text{ odd}).
\]

Proving that lemma from recurrences for the outer coefficients would dispose
of the unshifted one-edge route completely.

## 3. Three exact calibrations

### 3.1. The sporadic success: \(k=3\)

The centered factor is

\[
f_3(u)=25-219u+765u^2-1155u^3.
\]

At \(p=3\), the coefficient valuations are

\[
0,1,2,1.
\]

All points lie on or above the segment from \((0,0)\) to \((3,1)\).
Its slope is \(1/3\), so the reduced denominator is three.  Hence

\[
f_3\text{ is irreducible over }\mathbf Q_3.
\]

Thus the method is not vacuous; it is merely nonuniform.

### 3.2. Large primes dividing the central value do not help: \(k=7\)

Here \(n=3\), \(d=9\), and

\[
a_0=97418894400,
\qquad
a_1=-1025477702064.
\]

Since \(b_3=5\cdot17^2\),

\[
v_5(a_0)=2,\qquad v_{17}(a_0)=4.
\]

But

\[
a_1\equiv1\pmod5,
\qquad
a_1\equiv9\pmod{17},
\]

and the leading coefficient is a unit at both primes.  Consequently the
lower polygons are

\[
(0,2)\longrightarrow(1,0)\longrightarrow(9,0)
\quad(p=5),
\]

and

\[
(0,4)\longrightarrow(1,0)\longrightarrow(9,0)
\quad(p=17).
\]

The nonzero side has horizontal length one, hence gives no irreducibility
information.  This is the concrete obstruction to using a prime
\(p>n\) dividing \(b_n\).

### 3.3. A death certificate for all full-edge shifts: \(k=5\)

The primitive degree-six polynomial is

\[
\begin{aligned}
f_5(u)={}&-341056+3428592u-14483556u^2+30911271u^3\\
&-26385381u^4+9763599u^5-1332869u^6.
\end{aligned}
\]

It satisfies

\[
\gcd(a_0,\ldots,a_5)=1,
\qquad
\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_6)=1,
\]

so no unshifted prime gives a full nonzero edge.

More strongly, no integer shift can do so.  Its discriminant has

\[
v_2(\operatorname{disc}f_5)=22,
\qquad
v_3(\operatorname{disc}f_5)=12,
\qquad
v_5(\operatorname{disc}f_5)=2,
\]

and every remaining prime divisor occurs with exponent one.  Since
\(d-1=5\), only \(2\) and \(3\) can possibly support a degree-six totally
ramified Dumas side.  But

\[
f_5(u)\equiv u^3(u+1)^3\pmod2,
\]

and

\[
f_5(u)\equiv(u-1)^3(u+1)^3\pmod3.
\]

At each candidate prime the reduction has two distinct residual roots.
Translation permutes these roots but cannot merge them.  Hence no
\(f_5(u+c)\) reduces to a sixth power of one linear polynomial.

A full coprime-slope side of horizontal length six would make the generated
\(p\)-adic extension totally ramified of degree six, forcing

\[
v_p(\operatorname{disc}f_5)\ge5.
\]

The discriminant screen and the two displayed reductions therefore exhaust
all primes.  We conclude:

\[
\boxed{\text{No prime and no integer shift gives a full Dumas side for }f_5.}
\]

This single example rules out a universal theorem of the form “for every
odd \(k\), choose \(p,c\) so that \(f_k(u+c)\) is Eisenstein or one-edge
Dumas.”

## 4. Shifted Eisenstein and shifted Dumas: the exact test

Let \(F(u)=\sum_{i=0}^d a_i u^i\) be primitive and suppose
\(p\nmid a_d\).  Put

\[
G_c(x)=F(x+c)=\sum_{i=0}^d b_i x^i,
\qquad
b_i=\frac{F^{(i)}(c)}{i!}.
\]

A single side from \((0,s)\) to \((d,0)\), with \(\gcd(s,d)=1\), is
exactly the collection of inequalities

\[
v_p(F(c))=s,
\]

and

\[
d\,v_p\!\left(\frac{F^{(i)}(c)}{i!}\right)
\ge (d-i)s
\qquad(1\le i\le d).
\]

For \(s=1\), these are the usual shifted Eisenstein conditions.

Already modulo \(p\), a necessary condition is

\[
\overline F(u)=\overline{a_d}(u-\bar c)^d.
\]

Thus one should **not** loop over many shifts first.  Factor \(F\bmod p\):
if its squarefree support contains more than one point, no shift can be
Eisenstein or full-edge Dumas at that prime.

There is also a cheap coefficient invariant that finds all possible shift
primes without factoring the full discriminant.  If \(p\nmid d a_d\) and

\[
\overline F(u)=\overline{a_d}(u-c)^d,
\]

then for every \(2\le j\le d\),

\[
J_j:=d^j a_d^{\,j-1}a_{d-j}-\binom dj a_{d-1}^{\,j}
\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Therefore every such prime divides

\[
E(F):=\gcd(J_2,J_3,\ldots,J_d),
\]

apart from the finitely many primes dividing \(d a_d\), which can be tested
separately.  This “rational-normal-curve gcd” is a much better shifted-
Eisenstein census than trying random \(c\)'s.

The recommended test is:

1. compute the primitive \(F_k\);
2. compute \(E(F_k)\);
3. test only primes dividing \(d a_d E(F_k)\);
4. require the reduction to be one linear factor of multiplicity \(d\);
5. test the exact Taylor-valuation inequalities above.

The reciprocal polynomial handles a prospective side whose minimum is at
the constant endpoint rather than the leading endpoint.

## 5. The structural prime \(3\): why higher-order polygons are natural,
and why they are not an all-\(k\) solution

Modulo \(3\),

\[
A(x)=34x^3+51x^2+27x+5
\equiv x^3-1=(x-1)^3.
\]

Because Frobenius is additive in characteristic three, the gap recurrence
implies a cube recurrence.  If

\[
\widetilde W_j(r)\equiv R_j(r)^3\pmod3,
\]

then

\[
R_{j+1}(r)
=(r+j-1)R_j(r)-(r+j)^2R_{j-1}(r)
\]

satisfies

\[
\widetilde W_{j+1}(r)\equiv R_{j+1}(r)^3\pmod3.
\]

Thus the first reduction of the raw gap polynomial is a cube.  This is the
reason the existing campaign note
`problems/3.2/campaign3_questions/q23_montes.txt` correctly identifies
higher-order Montes analysis at \(3\) as a natural local experiment.  It is
also why an ordinary first-order polygon at \(3\) is usually too coarse.

However, before running a higher-order polygon, one must factor the
squarefree support of the cube root.  If

\[
\overline F=\phi_1^{e_1}\phi_2^{e_2}\cdots
\]

with at least two distinct coprime \(\phi_i\), Hensel lifts the coprime
blocks and \(F\) is already reducible over \(\mathbf Q_3\).  Higher-order
polygons refine each block; they cannot recombine distinct first-order
blocks.

For example,

\[
\overline f_5=(u-1)^3(u+1)^3
\]

has two blocks, so \(f_5\) factors over \(\mathbf Q_3\) into two degree-three
pieces.  Likewise

\[
\overline f_7=2u^6(u-1)^3
\]

has two distinct residual factors.  Therefore \(p=3\) cannot prove
irreducibility for all odd \(k\).

When the primitive reduction is one power

\[
\overline F=\phi^e
\]

with \(\phi\) irreducible, the correct next step is the
\(\phi\)-adic expansion

\[
F=A_0+A_1\phi+\cdots+A_e\phi^e,
\qquad \deg A_i<\deg\phi,
\]

followed by the \(\phi\)-Newton polygon and its residual polynomial.  If
that residual polynomial remains repeated, continue with a higher-order
Montes type.  A side of reduced denominator \(q\), with residual factor
degree \(f\), produces local factors whose degrees are multiples of

\[
qf\deg\phi.
\]

In the favorable cube case where \(\deg\phi=n\), a side of denominator
three and residual degree one could produce a single factor of degree
\(3n=d\).  The obstruction is that the cube root is not uniformly
irreducible, as \(k=5\) already shows.

If the raw polynomial has \(3\)-adic content, first divide by that content
and analyze the first nonzero correction modulo \(3\); reducing the raw
polynomial to zero contains no information.

The same preliminary support test should be run at \(2,5,17\).  There is no
reason to launch a full Montes computation when the first reduction already
contains two coprime blocks.

## 6. Multi-prime local degree sieve: the most realistic p-adic use

A polynomial can be irreducible over \(\mathbf Q\) while reducible over every
small \(\mathbf Q_p\) inspected.  Therefore demanding one locally
irreducible prime may be unnecessarily strong.

Suppose the factorization over \(\mathbf Q_p\) has irreducible factor
degrees

\[
d_{p,1},\ldots,d_{p,t_p}.
\]

Any rational factor of \(F\) becomes a product of some of these local
factors, so its degree belongs to the subset-sum set

\[
\mathcal D_p=
\left\{\sum_{i\in I}d_{p,i}:I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t_p\}\right\}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\bigcap_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathcal D_p=\{0,d\}
\]

for any finite set of primes \(\mathcal P\) proves irreducibility over
\(\mathbf Q\).

At a good squarefree reduction prime this is just the usual mod-\(p\)
factor-degree sieve.  At a bad structural prime, Montes supplies the genuine
\(p\)-adic factor degrees that ordinary DDF cannot read from a repeated
reduction.

This suggests the following realistic program.

1. For every odd \(k\le100\), compute complete local degree partitions at
   \(p=2,3,5,17\), after primitive normalization.
2. Add primes dividing the constant term, the leading term, and the small
   subresultants/discriminant factors.
3. Intersect the subset-sum sets, rather than requiring one partition to be
   \((d)\).
4. Search for a finite menu of patterns depending only on \(k\) modulo a
   small modulus, or for a deterministic rule choosing one or two primes
   from arithmetic data attached to \(k\).
5. Only after a pattern is visible, prove the residual and higher-order
   recurrences uniformly in \(k\).

This is p-adically stronger than the existing per-\(k\) DDF certificates,
but the all-\(k\) theorem still requires new structure.  There is no general
algebraic-number-theory theorem saying that an arbitrary growing family of
irreducible polynomials must admit a uniformly describable local certificate.

## 7. What primes dividing the leading coefficient can and cannot do

Because \(L_k\) is a Lucas sequence, primes dividing \(L_k\) are plentiful.
But if \(p\mid a_d\) and \(p\nmid a_0\), a full positive-slope segment would
require

\[
p\mid a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_d.
\]

Thus the relevant integer is \(C_k^+\), not \(a_d\) alone.

For \(k=5\),

\[
a_6=-1332869=-19\cdot29\cdot41\cdot59,
\]

but

\[
\gcd(a_5,a_6)=1.
\]

Every prime divisor of the leading coefficient leaves the next coefficient
a unit, so the terminal nonhorizontal side has horizontal length one.
Primitive-divisor theory for the Lucas sequence cannot repair this: it
controls only \(a_d\), not the other polynomial coefficients.

Likewise, a prime dividing one isolated intermediate coefficient usually
raises a point above the lower hull and does nothing.  Newton polygons need
systematic divisibility of an initial or terminal coefficient block.

## 8. Discriminants: useful sieve, not an irreducibility criterion

The discriminant has three legitimate uses here.

1. **Shift screen.** A full coprime-slope side of horizontal length \(d\)
   gives a totally ramified degree-\(d\) local extension, hence
   \(v_p(\operatorname{disc}F)\ge d-1\).  This sharply limits candidate
   shifted-Dumas primes, as in the exact \(f_5\) calculation.
2. **Bad-prime locator.** Primes dividing the discriminant are precisely the
   primes at which repeated residual factors and higher-order Montes data may
   occur, apart from leading-coefficient issues.
3. **Galois group after irreducibility.** Once transitivity is known, a
   nonsquare discriminant rules out containment in the alternating group.

Squarefreeness of the discriminant does **not** prove irreducibility.  If
\(F=GH\), then

\[
\operatorname{disc}(F)
=\operatorname{disc}(G)\operatorname{disc}(H)
 \operatorname{Res}(G,H)^2.
\]

A squarefree discriminant merely forces

\[
\operatorname{Res}(G,H)=\pm1
\]

and suitable coprimality of the factor discriminants.  Such reducible
examples exist; for instance

\[
u(u^2-u-1)
\]

is reducible and has discriminant \(5\), which is squarefree.

Moreover the Apéry gap family has repeated reductions at structural primes,
so high prime powers in the discriminant are expected rather than
exceptional; \(f_5\) already has \(2^{22}3^{12}5^2\) in its discriminant.
Thus “prove the discriminant squarefree” is neither plausible nor sufficient.

## 9. Exact computation to run next

### Phase A: kill or locate ordinary one-edge cases

For every odd \(k\le100\), and preferably \(k\le500\), output

\[
\operatorname{cont}(f_k),\quad C_k^-,\quad C_k^+.
\]

If the observed identity

\[
C_k^-=C_k^+=1\qquad(k\ge5\text{ odd})
\]

persists, derive recurrences for enough outer coefficients to prove it.  This
would be a clean theorem saying that no unshifted full nonzero edge exists.

### Phase B: exhaustive shifted-Dumas screen

For each primitive \(F_k\):

1. compute the gcd \(E(F_k)\) of the invariants \(J_j\) above;
2. add primes dividing \(d a_d\);
3. for each candidate prime, factor \(F_k\bmod p\);
4. retain only reductions with one linear factor of multiplicity \(d\);
5. test the Taylor valuations for all possible slopes \(s\) with
   \(\gcd(s,d)=1\).

This is exhaustive for integer shifts and far cheaper than trying random
primes and shifts.

### Phase C: Montes census

At \(p\in\{2,3,5,17\}\), and at selected endpoint/discriminant primes:

1. divide out content;
2. factor the squarefree support of \(F_k\bmod p\);
3. if there are at least two support factors, record the resulting local
   reducibility and stop;
4. if there is one support factor \(\phi\), compute the complete Montes type,
   slopes, residual polynomials, and final local factor degrees;
5. store only the degree partition and the type data needed to reproduce it.

At \(p=3\), separately factor the cube root of the raw reduction whenever it
is nonzero.  This immediately detects the indices for which \(3\)-adic
irreducibility is impossible.

### Phase D: degree-set intersection

Represent \(\mathcal D_p\) by a bitset and intersect it across primes.  Search
for:

- a fixed finite prime set that works for all computed \(k\);
- a congruence-class rule in \(k\);
- or an infinite subfamily admitting one uniform \(\phi\)-Eisenstein/Montes
  certificate.

A fixed-prime or congruence pattern is what could realistically be converted
into an all-\(k\) proof.

### Sage skeleton for the first two phases

```python
from sage.all import *

R.<r> = PolynomialRing(QQ)
Z.<z> = PolynomialRing(QQ)
U.<u> = PolynomialRing(ZZ)

def A(x):
    return 34*x^3 + 51*x^2 + 27*x + 5

def raw_gap_polys(K):
    W = [R(0), R(-1)]
    for j in range(1, K):
        W.append((A(r+j)*W[j] - (r+j)^6*W[j-1]).expand())
    return W

def primitive_centered_factor(W, k):
    assert k % 2 == 1
    g = Z(W[k](z - QQ(k+1)/2))
    assert all(g[i] == 0 for i in range(1, g.degree()+1, 2))
    coeffs = [ZZ(g[2*i]) for i in range(g.degree()//2 + 1)]
    content = gcd([abs(c) for c in coeffs])
    F = U([c // content for c in coeffs])
    return content, F

def one_sided_contents(F):
    a = F.list() + [0]*(F.degree()+1-len(F.list()))
    return gcd([abs(x) for x in a[:-1]]), \
           gcd([abs(x) for x in a[1:]])

def shifted_power_invariant(F):
    d = F.degree()
    ad = ZZ(F[d])
    a1 = ZZ(F[d-1])
    J = []
    for j in range(2, d+1):
        Jj = d^j * ad^(j-1) * ZZ(F[d-j]) - binomial(d,j)*a1^j
        if Jj != 0:
            J.append(abs(Jj))
    return gcd(J) if J else ZZ(0)

def dumas_shift_test(F, p, c):
    G = U(F(u + ZZ(c)))
    d = G.degree()
    if G[d] % p == 0:
        return False
    s = ZZ(G[0]).valuation(p)
    if s <= 0 or gcd(s,d) != 1:
        return False
    for i in range(d+1):
        vi = ZZ(G[i]).valuation(p) if G[i] != 0 else Infinity
        if d*vi < (d-i)*s:
            return False
    return True
```

For repeated reductions, use a genuine Montes implementation rather than
ordinary finite-field factorization alone; see Guàrdia--Montes--Nart,
*Newton polygons of higher order in algebraic number theory*, Trans. Amer.
Math. Soc. 364 (2012), 361--416.

## 10. Final ranking

1. **Most promising local route:** higher-order Montes at a prime for which
   the primitive reduction has a single irreducible support factor, combined
   across several primes by a factor-degree subset-sum sieve.
2. **Potentially useful for subfamilies:** a uniform \(\phi\)-Eisenstein
   pattern at \(3\), \(2\), \(5\), or \(17\), after classifying the residual
   support as a function of \(k\).
3. **Sporadic only:** an ordinary unshifted single edge.  It proves \(f_3\),
   but the one-sided-content data kill it for every tested odd \(k\ge5\).
4. **Not an all-\(k\) route:** shifted Eisenstein or shifted full-edge Dumas;
   \(f_5\) is an exact counterexample to the existence of such a certificate.
5. **Auxiliary only:** discriminant square class and valuations.  They locate
   local candidates and help determine the Galois group after irreducibility,
   but squarefreeness does not imply irreducibility.

So the answer to the main question is:

\[
\boxed{\text{ordinary Newton polygons are not the decisive all-}k\text{ lemma.}}
\]

The p-adic experiment worth pursuing is the **complete residual-support and
Montes degree-partition census**, followed by a search for a uniform
multi-prime degree obstruction.  If that census shows no stable pattern, the
all-\(k\) irreducibility proof will have to come from the recurrence's global
Jacobi/Galois structure rather than from endpoint valuations.
