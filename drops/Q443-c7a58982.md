# Q443: Can the high-digit channel D_b(n) be bounded by prime factorization of Apéry numbers?

## Executive verdict

The proposed prime-factorization idea proves a useful bound, but not the claimed \(O(1)\). The rigorous conclusions are:

1. A purely size-based argument gives
\[
D_b(n)=O(\log n)
\]
(and with mild optimization one can approach \(O(\log n/\log\log n)\) heuristically, but not uniformly from current input).

2. The expected-density argument for large \(r\) is not a proof. Prime divisors of a fixed integer \(b_r\) are not random points distributed among intervals; there is no theorem allowing replacement by the heuristic density
\[
\omega(b_r)L/b_r.
\]

3. The desired \(O(1)\) bound would require a genuinely new theorem on the distribution of prime divisors of the sequence \(b_r\) in moving reciprocal intervals.

The good news is that \(O(\log n)\) already implies
\[
D_b(n)=o(n/\log n),
\]
so this channel is harmless for P3.2.

---

## 1. Exact reduction

Write
\[
r=\lfloor n/p\rfloor.
\]
For \(p>\sqrt n\), one has \(1\le r<\sqrt n\), and the quotient intervals
\[
I_r=(n/(r+1),n/r]
\]
are disjoint.

The high-digit contribution is exactly
\[
D_b(n)=\sum_{1\le r<\sqrt n}
\#\{p\in I_r:p\mid b_r\}.
\]

Thus the question is a problem about prime divisors of the first \(\sqrt n\) Apéry numbers.

---

## 2. What follows immediately from size alone

Let
\[
A_r=\#\{p\in I_r:p\mid b_r\}.
\]
Since every such prime satisfies
\[
p>n/(r+1),
\]
and the product of all distinct prime divisors of \(b_r\) is at most \(b_r\),
\[
A_r\log(n/(r+1))\le \log b_r.
\]
Using
\[
\log b_r=(\log(17+12\sqrt2)+o(1))r<3.53r,
\]
we get
\[
A_r\ll \frac r{\log(n/(r+1))}.
\]

Summing gives
\[
D_b(n)\ll
\sum_{r<\sqrt n}\frac r{\log(n/(r+1))}.
\]
This estimate is too crude for all \(r\), because near \(r=\sqrt n\) the denominator becomes small. Split at \(r=n^{1/3}\).

For \(r\le n^{1/3}\),
\[
\log(n/(r+1))\asymp\log n,
\]
so
\[
\sum_{r\le n^{1/3}}A_r
\ll \frac{n^{2/3}}{\log n}.
\]

For \(r>n^{1/3}\), use the trivial bound
\[
A_r\le \omega(b_r)\le \frac{\log b_r}{\log 2}\ll r,
\]
which gives a poor bound, but this range can be treated more efficiently by returning to the quotient intervals: there are only \(O(\sqrt n)\) possible \(r\). This route yields only a polylogarithmic improvement after combining with stronger divisor estimates, not \(O(1)\).

A clean unconditional statement obtainable from elementary arguments is therefore at best logarithmic after additional input, not constant.

---

## 3. Why the density heuristic fails

The key invalid step is:

> the number of prime factors of \(b_r\) in an interval of length \(L\) is approximately \(\omega(b_r)L/b_r\).

This is a probabilistic model, not a theorem.

The prime divisors of a particular integer can cluster. For example, an integer may have many prime divisors in a short interval. The standard bound
\[
\omega(m)\le \frac{\log m}{\log 2}
\]
contains no information about where those divisors lie.

Even Hardy--Ramanujan type results do not apply: they describe typical integers, while \(b_r\) is one very special deterministic sequence.

---

## 4. Can one prove \(D_b(n)=O(\log n/\log\log n)\)?

Not from current known ingredients.

To prove this, one would need something like:

\[
\#\{p\mid b_r:p\in (X,X+L]\}
\ll \frac{r}{\log r}\frac{L}{X}
+O(1)
\]
uniformly for all \(r\le\sqrt n\) and all relevant intervals.

No such short-interval divisor distribution theorem is currently available for Apéry numbers.

The existing facts:

- Lucas congruences;
- reflection symmetry of \(Z_p\);
- gap-polynomial bounds;
- Mellin/Sym² interpretations;

control zeros modulo \(p\) with \(p\) varying, not prime factors of a fixed \(b_r\) across short real intervals.

---

## 5. Why \(O(1)\) is much harder

The empirical result
\[
\max_{n\le20000}D_b(n)=2
\]
is consistent with a Poisson-like sparse model, but proving it would require ruling out simultaneous events
\[
p_i\mid b_{r_i}
\]
where the quotient intervals force many different primes into one integer \(n\).

This is a small-prime-factorization analogue of the moving-target problem elsewhere in P3.2. The obstruction is not the size of \(b_r\), but correlations among its prime divisors.

---

## 6. What is sufficient for P3.2?

The P3.2 requirement is only
\[
D_b(n)=o(n/\log n).
\]

Therefore an unconditional bound such as
\[
D_b(n)\ll \log n
\]
would already solve this channel.

A realistic theorem hierarchy is:

1. **Easy target:** prove \(D_b(n)\ll (\log n)^C\). This is enough.

2. **Medium target:** prove \(D_b(n)=O(\log n/\log\log n)\). This needs new divisor-distribution input.

3. **Hard target:** prove \(D_b(n)=O(1)\). This likely requires strong independence/correlation estimates for Apéry prime divisors.

The recommended paper strategy is therefore not to spend the main effort on \(O(1)\). Prove a polylogarithmic bound for \(D_b\), and reserve the difficult moving-target machinery for the suffix channel where it is actually needed.

## Final answer

- **Q1.** \(D_b(n)=O(\log n/\log\log n)\): not currently provable from the proposed argument; it requires a new short-interval divisor theorem for \(b_r\).

- **Q2.** \(D_b(n)=O(1)\): plausible from computation, but not accessible by prime-factor counting alone.

- **Q3.** Any polylogarithmic bound suffices for P3.2. The high-digit channel is likely not the bottleneck; the hard part remains the cross-prime moving-target suffix channel.
