# 1. Exact centered-row model and exact-`g` collapse

Fix the quotient `q>=1` throughout.

For one `g`-slice, Q3044 uses candidate integers `N` for which

\[
p=1+gN
\]

is prime.  Let

\[
Z_{g,N}\subseteq (\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times
\]

be the actual normalized zero set, let

\[
z_{g,N}=|Z_{g,N}|,
\qquad
\rho_{g,N}=\frac{z_{g,N}}{\varphi(N)},
\]

and define the centered residue function

\[
F_{g,N}(a)=
\begin{cases}
1-\rho_{g,N},&a\in Z_{g,N},\\
-\rho_{g,N},&(a,N)=1,\ a\notin Z_{g,N},\\
0,&(a,N)>1.
\end{cases}
\tag{1.1}
\]

The fixed-`g` lifted row is

\[
f_{g,N}(h)=F_{g,N}(h-qN)
\]

on

\[
qN<h<(q+1)N,
\]

and is zero outside that interval.  Q3044 proves exactly

\[
\boxed{
\|f_{g,N}\|_2^2=z_{g,N}(1-\rho_{g,N})\le z_{g,N}.
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

For the actual outer integer `n`, put

\[
m=n-q.
\]

A `g`-slice can contribute only when `g|m`.  Write

\[
h=\frac{m}{g},
\qquad
h=qN+a.
\]

Then

\[
m=g(qN+a)=qgN+ga=q(p-1)+ga,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{n=qp+r,\qquad r=ga.}
\tag{1.3}
\]

If the centered term is nonzero, then `(a,N)=1`.  Hence

\[
\gcd(r,p-1)
=\gcd(ga,gN)
=g\gcd(a,N)
=g.
\]

Thus any nonzero contribution automatically satisfies

\[
\boxed{
g=\gcd(r,p-1)=\gcd(n-q,p-1).}
\tag{1.4}
\]

This is the exact-`g` uniqueness statement needed for the global row: for fixed `(n,p)`, **at most one** divisor `g|p-1` can produce a nonzero centered coordinate.

Consequently, after summing over all `g`, there is no multiplicity coming from several factorizations of the same prime.  We may collapse the global centered row pointwise to distinct primes:

\[
\boxed{
S(n)=\sum_p x_p(n),
}
\tag{1.5}
\]

where `x_p(n)` is the unique nonzero exact-`g` centered contribution of `p`, if one exists, and is zero otherwise.

This collapse is useful, but it does not by itself bound how many **different** primes can occur at one `n`.

# 2. Raw hit multiplicity and exact centering identity

For each prime coordinate define

\[
I_p(n)=
\mathbf 1_{\{\text{the unique exact-}g\text{ residue is in }Z_{g,N}\}},
\]

and write its corresponding density as `rho_p(n)` when the exact-`g` coordinate is active, zero otherwise.  Then

\[
x_p(n)=I_p(n)-\rho_p(n).
\]

Define

\[
K(n)=\sum_p I_p(n),
\qquad
\mu(n)=\sum_p\rho_p(n).
\tag{2.1}
\]

Therefore, **row by row and with no approximation**,

\[
\boxed{S(n)=K(n)-\mu(n).}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Let the total positive incidence mass over the packet be

\[
\boxed{T=\sum_n K(n).}
\tag{2.3}
\]

For a full lifted packet, every column contributes `z_{g,N}` positive hits and total centering mass

\[
\rho_{g,N}\varphi(N)=z_{g,N}.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_n\mu(n)=T
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

for the full packet, and `<=T` for any truncation of its row domain.

The pointwise quantity one wants to rule out is a row with

\[
K(n)\asymp T
\]

and hence, once `mu(n)=o(T)`, also `|S(n)|\asymp T`.

# 3. Direct expansion of the global fourth moment

Start from

\[
M_4:=\sum_n S(n)^4
=\sum_n\left(\sum_p x_p(n)\right)^4.
\]

Grouping by the number of distinct prime moduli gives the exact ordered-sum decomposition

\[
\begin{aligned}
M_4
={}&
\sum_p\sum_n x_p^4\\
&+4\sum_{p\ne q}\sum_n x_p^3x_q\\
&+3\sum_{p\ne q}\sum_n x_p^2x_q^2\\
&+6\sum_{\substack{p,q,r\\\text{all distinct}}}\sum_n x_p^2x_qx_r\\
&+\sum_{\substack{p,q,r,s\\\text{all distinct}}}
\sum_n x_px_qx_rx_s.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.1}
\]

The configuration types are therefore exactly:

```text
1 distinct modulus: 4
2 distinct moduli: 3+1 and 2+2
3 distinct moduli: 2+1+1
4 distinct moduli: 1+1+1+1.
```

There are no additional same-prime/different-`g` cases hidden inside (3.1): exact-`g` uniqueness makes those products zero pointwise before this expansion.

The direct centered expansion is correct but hides positivity.  The raw multiplicity `K(n)` gives a cleaner formulation.

# 4. Exact factorial-collision expansion

For an integer `K>=0`, write

\[
(K)_d=K(K-1)\cdots(K-d+1).
\]

The elementary identities are

\[
K^2=K+(K)_2,
\]

\[
K^3=K+3(K)_2+(K)_3,
\]

and

\[
K^4=K+7(K)_2+6(K)_3+(K)_4.
\]

Substituting `S=K-mu` gives the exact rowwise identity

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
S^4
={}&(K)_4\\
&+(6-4\mu)(K)_3\\
&+(7-12\mu+6\mu^2)(K)_2\\
&+(1-4\mu+6\mu^2-4\mu^3)K\\
&+\mu^4.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Define the global distinct-prime factorial collision counts

\[
\boxed{
C_d:=\sum_n (K(n))_d,
\qquad d=2,3,4.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Thus:

- `C_2` counts ordered alignments of two distinct prime moduli at one outer `n`;
- `C_3` counts ordered alignments of three distinct primes;
- `C_4` counts ordered alignments of four distinct primes.

These are exactly the positive collision statistics relevant to the fourth moment.

# 5. At the `T^4` scale, only four-distinct-prime collisions survive

Let

\[
\rho_*:=\max_{g,N}\rho_{g,N}.
\]

In the fixed-quotient packet, the proved zero bound gives

\[
z_{g,N}\ll p^{2/3},
\qquad p=1+gN.
\]

Using the standard lower bound

\[
\varphi(N)\gg \frac{N}{\log\log N}
\]

and `N\asymp p/g`, one gets

\[
\rho_{g,N}
\ll g\,p^{-1/3}\log\log p.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Hence throughout the packet regime `g=p^{o(1)}`,

\[
\boxed{\rho_*=p^{-1/3+o(1)}=o(1).}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Only columns with `z_{g,N}>0` matter, and their number is at most `T`.  Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\mu_*:=\max_n\mu(n)\le \rho_*T=o(T).
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Also, trivially,

\[
C_2\le T^2,
\qquad
C_3\le T^3,
\qquad
C_4\le T^4.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Using `sum mu<=T`, `mu_*=o(T)`, and (5.4) in (4.1), every term other than `C_4` is `o(T^4)`:

\[
T=o(T^4),
\qquad
C_2=O(T^2)=o(T^4),
\qquad
C_3=O(T^3)=o(T^4),
\]

and every centering correction has at least one factor bounded by `mu_*=o(T)` and total remaining mass at most a fixed power of `T`.

Thus, whenever `T->infinity`, the exact fourth-moment expansion yields

\[
\boxed{
M_4=C_4+o(T^4).
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

This is the decisive reduction.  A second- or third-distinct-prime configuration cannot by itself sustain a `T`-sized spike at the fourth-moment scale.  The only possible `T^4` obstruction is an anchored cluster of four-or-more distinct primes, measured by `C_4`.

# 6. A quantitative fourth-factorial-moment theorem that would close the maximum

Let

\[
K_*:=\max_n K(n).
\]

If `K_*>=6`, then

\[
(K_*)_4
=K_*(K_*-1)(K_*-2)(K_*-3)
\ge \left(\frac{K_*}{2}\right)^4.
\]

Since the maximizing row contributes to `C_4`, we have

\[
\left(\frac{K_*}{2}\right)^4\le C_4.
\]

Thus in all cases

\[
\boxed{
K_*\le 5+2C_4^{1/4}.
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

From `S=K-mu`,

\[
|S(n)|\le K(n)+\mu(n),
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
\max_n|S(n)|
\le \mu_*+5+2C_4^{1/4}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Therefore:

> **Fourth-collision sufficient theorem.**  Suppose `T->infinity`, `mu_*=o(T)`, and
> \[
> C_4=o(T^4).
> \]
> Then
> \[
> \boxed{\max_n|S(n)|=o(T).}
> \]

A quantitative version is immediate.  If for some fixed `delta>0`

\[
C_4\ll T^{4-\delta},
\tag{6.3}
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
\max_n|S(n)|
\ll \mu_*+T^{1-\delta/4}.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

More generally, any vanishing factor is enough:

\[
C_4\le \frac{T^4}{L(T)},
\qquad L(T)\to\infty
\]

implies

\[
\max_n|S(n)|
\le \mu_*+O\!\left(\frac{T}{L(T)^{1/4}}\right)
=o(T).
\]

If the packet has `R` available outer rows, the independence-scale expectation would be

\[
C_4\asymp \frac{T^4}{R^3}.
\]

Even the much weaker estimate

\[
C_4\ll \frac{T^4}{R^{3-\varepsilon}}
\]

would give

\[
\max_n|S(n)|
\ll \mu_*+\frac{T}{R^{3/4-\varepsilon/4}}.
\]

So one does **not** need a Poisson theorem.  One needs merely any genuine four-prime anti-concentration giving `C_4=o(T^4)`.

The same statement survives logarithmic prime weights on a dyadic prime block because all weights `log p` are comparable there; only constants and powers of `log P` change.

# 7. Arithmetic meaning of a `d`-prime collision

Suppose distinct primes

\[
p_1,\ldots,p_d
\]

all give actual hits at one row `n`.  Write

\[
n=qp_i+r_i,
\qquad 0<r_i<p_i.
\]

Then

\[
p_i\mid b_{r_i}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Their unique exact-`g` labels are

\[
\boxed{
g_i=\gcd(r_i,p_i-1)=\gcd(n-q,p_i-1),}
\tag{7.2}
\]

\[
N_i=\frac{p_i-1}{g_i},
\qquad
a_i=\frac{r_i}{g_i},
\]

and all satisfy the common outer-row equation

\[
\boxed{
n-q=g_i(qN_i+a_i).}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Now use the multiplicative Apéry--Lucas congruence.  For fixed `q` and all sufficiently large `p_i` with `p_i\nmid b_q`,

\[
b_n=b_{qp_i+r_i}\equiv b_qb_{r_i}\pmod{p_i}.
\]

Hence

\[
p_i\mid b_{r_i}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p_i\mid b_n.
\tag{7.4}
\]

Thus a `d`-prime collision implies

\[
\boxed{p_1p_2\cdots p_d\mid b_n.}
\tag{7.5}
\]

Modulo the finite set of primes dividing fixed `b_q`, the collision statistic is therefore a factorial moment of the number of allowed large prime divisors of the **same integer** `b_n`:

\[
C_d
=\sum_n\bigl(\omega_{q,\mathrm{packet}}(b_n)\bigr)_d,
\tag{7.6}
\]

up to whatever harmless endpoint/truncation convention the packet uses.

This reformulation is important: the fourth-moment problem is no longer a same-characteristic zero-spacing problem.  It is a cross-characteristic large-prime-divisor problem for the integer sequence `b_n`.

# 8. What reflection actually buys — and why it stops at a constant factor

For one hit prime `p`, reflection gives

\[
p\mid b_r
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p\mid b_{p-1-r}.
\]

The reflected outer row is

\[
\begin{aligned}
n_p^\vee
&=qp+(p-1-r)\\
&=(2q+1)p-1-n.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.1}
\]

The exact-`g` label is preserved, because

\[
\gcd(p-1-r,p-1)=\gcd(r,p-1)=g.
\tag{8.2}
\]

In normalized coordinates this is simply

\[
a\longmapsto N-a.
\]

For distinct primes `p`, the reflected rows `n_p^vee` are distinct because (8.1) is injective in `p`.

If an anchor `n_0` carries `K` distinct hit primes, at most one of them can be centrally fixed by reflection: `n_p^vee=n_0` forces

\[
p=\frac{2n_0+1}{2q+1}.
\]

Therefore the reflected partners alone force at least `K-1` additional distinct positive incidences away from the anchor.  Consequently

\[
T\ge K+(K-1)=2K-1,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{K\le \frac{T+1}{2}.}
\tag{8.3}
\]

This is real information, but it is only a **constant-factor** anti-spike bound.  It is not `o(T)`.

In the common doublet regime, each prime is literally an edge

\[
\{n,n_p^\vee\}
\]

whose endpoint sum is

\[
n+n_p^\vee=(2q+1)p-1.
\tag{8.4}
\]

Two distinct prime edges cannot have both endpoints equal, because then their endpoint sums would coincide and (8.4) would force the primes equal.  Thus reflection gives a **simple graph**.

But a simple graph can be a star.  Pairwise codegree one does not bound the degree of one vertex.

# 9. No-adjacent-zero does not improve the star bound

The two reflected residues are

\[
r,\qquad p-1-r.
\]

Their difference is

\[
|p-1-2r|.
\]

For odd `p`, this difference is even.  Unless the root is central, it is therefore at least `2`.  So the reflected doublet automatically respects the no-consecutive-zero theorem.

In normalized exact-`g` coordinates the gap is

\[
g|N-2a|.
\]

If `g>=2`, it cannot equal `1`.  If `g=1`, then `N=p-1` is even, so a nonzero `|N-2a|` is again at least `2`.

Hence no-adjacent-zero removes no branch of the reflected anchored-star construction.  It gives no improvement over (8.3).

# 10. Exact-`g` uniqueness removes duplicates, not stars

Equation (1.4) is strong enough to prevent the same pair `(n,p)` from being counted under two different `g` labels.  Thus it eliminates a fake multiplicity in the global sum and is essential for writing the fourth moment over distinct primes.

But it does not constrain the number of **different** primes sharing the same row.

Indeed the obstruction below lives entirely in the single slice

\[
g=1.
\]

So no argument whose only cross-`g` input is exact-`g` uniqueness can turn the fourth moment into `o(T^4)`.

# 11. What the integer recurrence gives at a star

At an actual anchored collision, (7.5) says that every hit prime divides the same integer `b_n`.  Thus for `K` distinct hit primes all at least `P`,

\[
P^K\le |b_n|.
\tag{11.1}
\]

This is the first genuine cross-prime consequence of the **integer** sequence, as opposed to separate mod-`p` recurrence statements.

But the available height is much too large to yield `o(T)`.

From the positive Apéry formula

\[
b_n=\sum_{j=0}^n
\binom nj^2\binom{n+j}{j}^2,
\]

use

\[
\binom nj\le2^n,
\qquad
\binom{n+j}{j}\le2^{n+j}\le4^n.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{|b_n|\le(n+1)64^n.}
\tag{11.2}
\]

Combining (11.1) and (11.2),

\[
\boxed{
K\le
\frac{n\log64+\log(n+1)}{\log P}.
}
\tag{11.3}
\]

In one fixed quotient cell, `P\asymp_q n`, so

\[
K=O_q\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right).
\tag{11.4}
\]

That is the same order as the entire population of candidate primes in a fixed positive-width quotient cell.  It is not a vanishing fraction of the natural incidence scale.

In fact the product of **all** primes in a fixed cell has logarithm only `Theta_q(n)`, whereas the crude Apéry height budget in (11.2) is about

\[
(\log64)n\approx4.159n.
\]

So integer size alone does not even contradict the extreme event that essentially every candidate prime in one cell divides `b_n`.

The recurrence also says that if `p|b_n` then the neighboring value cannot vanish modulo `p`; but those neighbor-unit statements are characteristic by characteristic.  CRT combines them without creating a cross-prime exclusion.

Therefore the currently extracted recurrence consequences do not bound `C_4` below the `T^4` scale.

This is a **scoped** conclusion, not a theorem that no deeper recurrence argument can exist.  A successful recurrence attack would have to prove genuinely new information about the distribution/factorization of the integers `b_n` across several large prime moduli simultaneously.

# 12. Explicit reflected anchored-star obstruction

The following construction is abstract but exact.  It shows that reflection, no adjacency, exact-`g` uniqueness, centering, the proved zero-count size bound, and pairwise codegree one still permit a fourth-moment spike.

Fix `q`.  Let `H` be a large prime and set

\[
n_0=H+q,
\qquad
m_0=n_0-q=H.
\]

Work entirely in the slice

\[
g=1.
\]

Choose odd primes `p` for which, with

\[
N=p-1,
\]

we have

\[
qN<H<(q+1)N,
\tag{12.1}
\]

and exclude the at-most-one central value satisfying

\[
2H=(2q+1)N.
\]

For fixed `q`, the prime number theorem gives

\[
K\asymp_q \frac{H}{\log H}
\tag{12.2}
\]

such primes.

For each chosen `N`, put

\[
a_N=H-qN.
\]

Then `1<=a_N<=N-1` by (12.1).  Moreover

\[
\gcd(a_N,N)=\gcd(H-qN,N)=\gcd(H,N)=1,
\tag{12.3}
\]

because `H` is prime and `N<H`.

Now define the **synthetic** normalized zero set

\[
\boxed{Z_N=\{a_N,N-a_N\}.}
\tag{12.4}
\]

It has all the structural properties under audit:

1. `z_N=2`, hence certainly `z_N<=Cp^{2/3}`;
2. it is exactly reflection invariant;
3. it has no adjacent zeros, because `N=p-1` is even and the noncentral reflected gap is a nonzero even integer;
4. both roots have exact `g=1`, by (12.3) and the same identity for `N-a_N`;
5. each prime produces a doublet of outer rows.

The first member of every doublet is the common anchor:

\[
qN+a_N=H,
\]

so after adding back `q`, every chosen prime hits

\[
n_0=H+q.
\]

The reflected row is

\[
n_p^\vee=(2q+1)p-1-n_0,
\]

and these leaves are distinct for distinct `p`.

Thus the incidence graph is an exact `K`-edge star.  Pairwise codegree is one: two distinct prime columns share the anchor and no second row.

The centered density is

\[
\rho_N=\frac{2}{\varphi(N)}.
\]

At the anchor,

\[
S(n_0)
=\sum_{p}(1-\rho_N)
=K-\sum_p\frac{2}{\varphi(p-1)}.
\]

Since `p\asymp_q H` and

\[
\frac1{\varphi(p-1)}\ll\frac{\log\log H}{H},
\]

we get

\[
\sum_p\frac{2}{\varphi(p-1)}
\ll
K\frac{\log\log H}{H}
\ll_q\frac{\log\log H}{\log H}
=o(1).
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{S(n_0)=K-o(1).}
\tag{12.5}
\]

The total positive incidence mass is exactly

\[
\boxed{T=2K.}
\tag{12.6}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\max_n|S(n)|
\ge\left(\frac12-o(1)\right)T.
}
\tag{12.7}
\]

At every leaf the raw multiplicity is one; only the anchor has multiplicity `K`.  Consequently, for every `d>=2`,

\[
C_d=(K)_d.
\]

In particular,

\[
\boxed{
C_2\sim\frac{T^2}{4},
\qquad
C_3\sim\frac{T^3}{8},
\qquad
C_4\sim\frac{T^4}{16}.
}
\tag{12.8}
\]

And already from the single anchor,

\[
\boxed{
M_4=\sum_nS(n)^4
\ge(K-o(1))^4
=\left(\frac1{16}-o(1)\right)T^4.
}
\tag{12.9}
\]

This is the requested anchored-star obstruction.

Again: (12.4) is not claimed to be the zero set of the actual Apéry sequence.  It is a model satisfying exactly the listed currently available structural restrictions.  Therefore any proof using only those restrictions cannot prove `M_4=o(T^4)` or `max=o(T)`.

# 13. The deterministic fourth-moment inequality is just as vulnerable as `L^2`

The Q3044 second-moment estimate is the pointwise Cauchy bound

\[
\left|\sum_{j=1}^K f_j(n)\right|^2
\le K\sum_j|f_j(n)|^2.
\]

At fourth order, the analogous deterministic inequality is

\[
\left|\sum_{j=1}^K f_j(n)\right|^4
\le K^3\sum_j|f_j(n)|^4.
\tag{13.1}
\]

Because `|F_{g,N}|<=1`,

\[
\|f_{g,N}\|_4^4
\le\|f_{g,N}\|_2^2
\le z_{g,N}.
\tag{13.2}
\]

For the star above, every column has `z_N=2`, so (13.1) gives

\[
M_4\le K^3\sum_{j=1}^K2=2K^4=\frac18T^4.
\tag{13.3}
\]

The actual star lower bound (12.9) is

\[
M_4\ge\left(\frac1{16}-o(1)\right)T^4.
\]

So the fully deterministic fourth-moment estimate is sharp on this obstruction to within a factor `2`.

Likewise the Q3044-style `L^2` bound gives

\[
\|S\|_2^2\le K\sum_jz_j=2K^2=\frac12T^2,
\]

while the anchor alone contributes asymptotically `K^2=T^2/4`.

Thus the passage `L^2 -> L^4` does not, by itself, create any new anti-spike mechanism.

# 14. Why the 2-, 3-, and 4-prime pieces behave differently

The exact expansion gives a useful hierarchy.

## Two distinct primes

The raw pair statistic is

\[
C_2=\sum_n(K(n))_2.
\]

Reflection/simple-graph geometry only says that two prime columns cannot share two rows in the doublet model.  It does not prevent `Theta(T^2)` different ordered prime pairs from all meeting at one anchor.  The star has

\[
C_2\sim T^2/4.
\]

So no second-moment improvement follows.

## Three distinct primes

The triple statistic is

\[
C_3=\sum_n(K(n))_3.
\]

Pairwise codegree one still permits every triple of star edges to share the same vertex.  Hence

\[
C_3\sim T^3/8.
\]

No new obstruction appears merely because three moduli are present.

## Four distinct primes

The fourth statistic is

\[
C_4=\sum_n(K(n))_4.
\]

This is exactly the first term that can match a `T^4` fourth moment.  But the same star gives

\[
C_4\sim T^4/16.
\]

Thus fourth order is not qualitatively different unless one proves a **new four-prime arithmetic theorem**.

The positive lesson is that a new theorem need not estimate every mixed centered term in (3.1).  It is enough to attack `C_4` directly.

# 15. First theorem-shaped invariant that escapes the obstruction

For an actual fixed-`q` packet, define

\[
\omega_q(n)
=
\#\left\{
 p\text{ in the packet}:
 \begin{array}{l}
 n=qp+r,\ 0<r<p,\\
 p\mid b_r,\\
 g=\gcd(r,p-1)\text{ is an allowed exact-}g\text{ label}
 \end{array}
\right\}.
\]

Then `K(n)=omega_q(n)` and

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal F_{4,q}
:=\sum_n(\omega_q(n))_4=C_4.
}
\tag{15.1}
\]

Modulo the fixed finite set of primes dividing `b_q`, Lucas changes this into a same-integer divisor statistic:

\[
\mathcal F_{4,q}
=
\sum_n
\left(
\#\{p\text{ in the packet}:p\mid b_n\}
\right)_4.
\tag{15.2}
\]

The narrow theorem sufficient for the present goal is therefore:

> **Fixed-`q` four-prime anti-star theorem.**  Uniformly in the relevant packets,
> \[
> \boxed{\mathcal F_{4,q}=o(T^4).}
> \]

No stronger distribution statement is logically necessary for `max=o(T)`.

A natural independence-scale target, if there are `R` outer rows, is

\[
\mathcal F_{4,q}
\ll R^{o(1)}\frac{T^4}{R^3},
\tag{15.3}
\]

but (15.3) is much stronger than needed.  Even

\[
\mathcal F_{4,q}\le T^4/(\log T)
\]

would already give a pointwise saving by (6.2).

This invariant escapes the same-characteristic geometry because it asks for simultaneous divisibility of one **integer** `b_n` by four unrelated large primes.  Reflection, gap polynomials, and no-adjacent-zero live separately modulo each prime; they do not control this cross-characteristic factorial divisor moment.

# 16. Narrow theorem-grade conclusion

The source-faithful conclusion that can be proved from the current pushed packet is:

> **Theorem / reduction.**  Fix `q` and let `S(n)` be the global exact-`g` centered row obtained by summing the Q3044 centered columns over all allowed `g`, with total positive incidence `T`.  Assume the packet lies in the regime `g=p^{o(1)}`, so the proved bound `Z(p)=O(p^{2/3})` implies `mu_*:=max_n mu(n)=o(T)`.  Let
> \[
> C_4=\sum_n(K(n))_4
> \]
> be the ordered four-distinct-prime collision count.  Then
> \[
> \max_n|S(n)|\le \mu_*+5+2C_4^{1/4}.
> \]
> In particular `C_4=o(T^4)` implies `max_n|S(n)|=o(T)`.
>
> The presently proved reflection, no-consecutive-zero, exact-`g` uniqueness, and zero-count bounds do not imply `C_4=o(T^4)`: the `g=1` reflected-doublet star of Section 12 satisfies all of them and has `T=2K`, `C_4=(K)_4~T^4/16`, and `max|S|>=(1/2-o(1))T`.  The current integer-recurrence consequences add only the actual divisibility relation `prod p_i | b_n` and the exponential height bound on `b_n`, which yields at best `K=O_q(n/log n)` for primes `p_i\asymp_q n`, not a vanishing fraction of the packet prime population.
>
> Therefore a fourth-moment proof requires a genuinely new cross-prime divisor-distribution statement, such as `C_4=o(T^4)` itself or any stronger four-prime factorial-moment estimate.  Merely invoking fourth moments on top of the existing same-prime reflection/recurrence structure does not beat the deterministic `L^2` spike barrier.

That is the sharp scoped obstruction I can justify from the currently exposed source state.
