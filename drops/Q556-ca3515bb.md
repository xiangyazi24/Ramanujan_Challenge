# Q556: P^1 orbit equidistribution for Apéry transfer cocycle

## Executive verdict

The proposed interpretation

\[
|Z_p|=\#\{r:T_{1\to r}[5:1]=[0:1]\}
\]

is correct, but the hoped-for implication

\[
G_{geom}=SL_2 \Longrightarrow \text{P}^1(F_p)\text{-orbit equidistribution with }O(\sqrt p)\text{ error}
\]

does **not** follow from Deligne's trace equidistribution. The missing ingredient is a sheaf-theoretic description of the entire nonautonomous cocycle orbit, not merely the geometric monodromy of the underlying rank-2 local system.

The right framework is a **l-adic cocycle over the base variable r**, or equivalently a Frobenius orbit problem on a family over the affine line. For a general nonautonomous sequence of matrices there is no universal equidistribution theorem. One needs a large monodromy and non-degeneracy hypothesis for the induced sheaf on the orbit space.

For the Apéry recurrence this route is promising conceptually, but the required theorem is essentially an additional major input.

---

# 1. Autonomous versus nonautonomous dynamics on P^1

For a constant matrix $M\in PGL_2(F_p)$, the orbit

\[
x_r=M^r x_0
\]

lies in the finite cyclic subgroup generated by M. Its length divides the order of M, hence divides a divisor of

\[
|PGL_2(F_p)|=p(p^2-1).
\]

Therefore a single orbit cannot equidistribute on all of $P^1(F_p)$ except in special accidental cases.

The Apéry system is fundamentally different:

\[
x_{r+1}=M(r)x_r,
\]

where $M(r)$ changes with r. The relevant object is not a group action but a **transfer cocycle**.

Define

\[
T(r,s)=M(s-1)\cdots M(r).
\]

The orbit is generated by the sequence

\[
x_r=T(1,r)x_1.
\]

There is no finite subgroup whose orbit length controls this sequence.

---

# 2. What is the precise equidistribution theorem?

There is no theorem saying that every sufficiently varying nonautonomous cocycle on $P^1(F_p)$ equidistributes.

The closest analogue is a Deligne/Katz theorem for a family of Frobenius actions.

Suppose there exists a lisse sheaf $\mathcal F$ on a curve $U/\mathbb F_p$ whose Frobenius action gives the cocycle. Let

\[
\rho:\pi_1(U)\rightarrow GL_2(\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell)
\]

be the associated representation.

A typical equidistribution statement requires:

1. geometric monodromy is large (for example $SL_2$);
2. the sheaf is geometrically irreducible;
3. the relevant observable is a trace function or matrix coefficient of bounded conductor.

Then Deligne's Riemann Hypothesis gives square-root cancellation:

\[
\left|\sum_{t\in U(F_p)}\psi(\rho(\mathrm{Frob}_t))\right|
\ll C\sqrt p.
\]

However this is an average over Frobenius parameters $t$. It is not automatically a statement about the sequential orbit

\[
T(1,r)x_1,\quad r=1,\ldots,p.
\]

The missing bridge is a dynamical sheaf whose Frobenius trace equals the orbit indicator.

---

# 3. Why trace equidistribution does not immediately give entry equidistribution

The event of interest is

\[
(T_{1\to r})_{12}=0.
\]

Equivalently:

\[
T_{1\to r}[5:1]=[0:1].
\]

This is a matrix coefficient condition.

Deligne gives information about functions such as

\[
\mathrm{tr}(T_{1\to r}),
\]

because traces are conjugacy invariant and correspond naturally to characters of representations.

But

\[
(T_{1\to r})_{12}=0
\]

is not conjugacy invariant. It depends on the chosen basis.

To obtain entry-level equidistribution one needs the full matrix coefficient representation. Concretely, one needs estimates of the form

\[
\sum_r \mathbf 1_{T(r)x_0=y}
\]

or equivalently estimates for all functions on $P^1$:

\[
\sum_r f(T(r)x_0).
\]

Representation theoretically this requires controlling every nontrivial irreducible representation occurring in

\[
L^2(P^1(F_p)).
\]

Large monodromy helps, but it is not by itself sufficient.

# 4. Normalized Apéry transfer matrix

The recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=A(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1},
\]

with

\[
M(n)=\begin{pmatrix}
A(n)/(n+1)^3&-n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The determinant is

\[
\det M(n)=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}.
\]

Hence

\[
\det(T_{1\to r})=\prod_{n=1}^{r-1}\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}=\frac1{r^3}.
\]

A formal SL$_2$ normalization would be

\[
\widetilde M(n)=\frac{M(n)}{\sqrt{\det M(n)}}
=\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{3/2}M(n).
\]

The cocycle becomes

\[
\widetilde T_{1\to r}=r^{3/2}T_{1\to r}.
\]

Over $F_p$ this requires choosing square roots of n. Therefore the clean object is usually not an SL$_2$ cocycle over $F_p$, but a GL$_2$ cocycle with controlled determinant character.

Projectively this determinant disappears, so the natural group is PGL$_2$.

# 5. Would a Weil bound give |Z_p|=1+O(sqrt(p))?

The desired statement is

\[
\left|\#\{r:T(1,r)x_0=x\}-\frac{p-3}{p+1}\right|
\ll \sqrt p.
\]

For a genuine algebraic family this is exactly the expected scale.

But the Apéry cocycle has two obstacles:

## Obstacle 1: the parameter r is the time variable

The matrices are evaluated at consecutive integers:

\[
M(1),M(2),\ldots,M(p-2).
\]

This is not the same as evaluating a fixed sheaf at all points of an algebraic variety.

## Obstacle 2: conductor growth

Encoding the whole product

\[
M(r-1)\cdots M(1)
\]

as a Frobenius trace generally creates a sheaf whose conductor grows with the length of the product. A square-root estimate with uniform constant requires bounded conductor.

This is exactly the obstruction encountered in the sheaf approach to AKD3: the relevant object is not a fixed low-complexity sheaf.

---

# 6. What would be sufficient for M1?

The full $O(\sqrt p)$ orbit theorem is stronger than necessary.

For the Apéry GCD conjecture we only need

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|=O(1).
\]

Possible weaker targets:

1. **Average cocycle mixing:**

\[
\sum_{p\le X}\left||Z_p|-1\right|\ll \pi(X).
\]

2. **Second moment bound:**

\[
\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|^2\ll \pi(X).
\]

3. **A large-sieve estimate for the cocycle values.**

These avoid proving pointwise Weil bounds.

---

# 7. Final assessment for Apéry

The projective orbit formulation is mathematically natural:

\[
Z_p=\{r:x_r=[0:1]\}.
\]

However:

- $G_{geom}=SL_2$ is evidence of randomness of the Frobenius representation, not a proof of sequential orbit mixing.
- Trace equidistribution does not imply matrix-entry equidistribution.
- A square-root Weil bound would require a new bounded-conductor cocycle sheaf.
- The normalized SL$_2$ formula exists formally, but the projective cocycle is the cleaner object.

The likely correct theorem to target is not

\[
|Z_p|=1+O(\sqrt p),
\]

but rather an averaged cocycle dispersion statement:

\[
\boxed{\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|^2=O(\pi(X))}
\]

or a two-prime correlation bound. Such a statement would match the M1 requirement and is closer to what the available monodromy information can realistically support.
