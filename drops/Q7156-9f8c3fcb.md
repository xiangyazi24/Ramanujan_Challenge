# P32 weak-DPLS audit

## 1. Variance reduction and exact target

Write
\[
M_p=\sum_q C_p(q),\qquad
\sum_q C_p(q)^2=M_p+2\sum_{q} {C_p(q)\choose 2}.
\]
The identity is exact because each projective state q contributes one diagonal ordered pair for every primitive chain start and two orientations for every unordered pair of distinct starts.

From
\[
\sum_p A_p\leq \sum_p M_p/p+\pi(X)^{1/2}\left(\sum_p V_p\right)^{1/2},
\]
the off-diagonal term must satisfy
\[
\pi(X)^{1/2}(\sum_pV_p)^{1/2}\ll X^{3/2+o(1)}/\log X.
\]
Since \(\pi(X)\asymp X/\log X\), the weakest sufficient condition is
\[
\boxed{\sum_{X<p\le2X}V_p\ll X^{2+o(1)}/\log X}.
\]
The relative estimate \(\sum V_p\ll X^{o(1)}\sum M_p\) is stronger than necessary.

## 2. What the pair-root cap gives unconditionally

The universal continuant estimate gives, for every gap d,
\[
\#\{x:\pi_x=\pi_{x+d}\}\le3(d-1).
\]
For short gaps \(d\le H=\sqrt p\), summing over d gives at most
\[
\sum_{d\le H}O(d)=O(H^2)
\]
returns from one state.

For a fixed prime, a crude second moment bound is therefore
\[
\sum_q C_p(q)^2
\le M_p+O(M_pH^2),
\]
because a chain start can have O(H^2) possible partners after only using the pair cap.
Thus
\[
V_p\ll M_pH^2+M_p^2/p.
\]

With H=sqrt(X), this gives an aggregate loss of two powers of H. It is not close enough by itself.

## 3. Exact missing factor

The desired absolute variance requires
\[
\sum_pV_p\ll X^{2+o(1)}/\log X.
\]
The pair cap alone naturally gives a bound larger by roughly
\[
H^2=X
\]
relative to the needed off-diagonal saving.

Equivalently, one needs the average number of compatible short-chain partners of a primitive start to be O(X^{o(1)}), not O(H^2). This is precisely the missing DPLS input.

## 4. Reflection and exceptional pairs

Reflection gives
\[
z\mapsto p-1-z.
\]
It identifies possible paired starts but does not reduce the number of projective collisions: reflected pairs are one deterministic exceptional family. Splitting pairs into:

1. overlapping chains;
2. reflected chains;
3. separated chains;

only removes the first two classes. The separated class still has the full H^2 combinatorial possibility under the pair-degree hypothesis.

## 5. Can requiring two four-return chains help?

Suppose both starts support primitive four-return chains. Then one obtains two low-degree continuant certificates. However, without an additional resultant/gcd estimate, this only says two independent polynomial systems vanish modulo p.

The missing statement would have to be a genuine codimension estimate:
\[
\#\{(p,x,y): x,y\text{ both support short chains and }\pi_x=\pi_y\}
\]
with a saving beyond the trivial product of two pair caps.

No such consequence follows from the known pair-root cap alone. The overlap and reflection cases can be handled separately, but the far-separated case remains open.

## 6. Fourier/energy viewpoint

Writing the projective-state indicator as a function on \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\), the variance is an additive energy quantity. The available degree bounds control individual shifts, not the Fourier spectrum. Parseval therefore gives only the same second-moment size as the combinatorial estimate.

A nontrivial Fourier estimate would itself be a new large-sieve theorem for the Apéry orbit.

## 7. Conclusion

The exact weakest sufficient hypothesis is
\[
\boxed{\sum_{X<p\le2X}V_p\ll X^{2+o(1)}/\log X}.
\]
The known universal gap-root bound and reflection imply only a much weaker estimate with an unavoidable H^2 loss. Imposing two primitive four-return chains does not currently remove that loss: it creates a plausible route to a new codimension/resultant theorem, but no proved theorem supplies it.

Therefore weak-DPLS is genuinely weaker than relative DPLS, but it is still beyond the currently established continuant degree bounds. The exact missing factor is the elimination of the O(H^2) possible compatible partners per primitive chain start.
