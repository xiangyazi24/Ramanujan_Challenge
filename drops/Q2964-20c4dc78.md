# P3.2 bad-row propagation: exact adjacent anti-propagation

## Verdict

The desired propagation does **not** follow from the current Jacobi Smith-border, determinant, or Apéry recurrence identities. In fact the strongest exact statement goes in the opposite direction.

Fix `q >= 1` and write

\[
\mathcal T_q(n)=
\left\{p\text{ prime}:\frac n{q+1}<p\le \frac nq,
\ p\mid b_{n-qp}\right\},
\qquad
R_q(n)=\prod_{p\in\mathcal T_q(n)}p.
\]

For every target prime `p in T_q(n)`, the same prime is **not a target at either adjacent size** `n-1` or `n+1`, for any quotient class at all. More precisely,

\[
\boxed{
\gcd\!\left(R_q(n),R_{q'}(n-1)R_{q'}(n+1)\right)=1
\quad\text{for every }q'.
}
\tag{A}
\]

At the Jacobi level this is exactly the statement that the target corank jump disappears at both borders:

\[
\boxed{
c_n(p)=q+1,\qquad c_{n-1}(p)=c_{n+1}(p)=q.}
\tag{B}
\]

Thus a target prime is present in the `q+1`-threshold Smith factor at size `n`, but absent from every adjacent `q+1`-threshold target factor. It survives only in the **universal q-corank baseline** at the neighboring matrices. This is precisely the wrong direction for exceptional-row propagation.

Using the banked normalized Jacobi factorization

\[
H_q(n)=\eta_q(n)^3E_q(n),
\qquad
R_q(n)\mid E_q(n)\mid R_q(n)^3,
\qquad
\eta_q(n)\mid n,
\tag{C}
\]

one obtains the quantitative anti-propagation statement: for every fixed `Q`,

\[
\boxed{
\gcd\!\left(
R_q(n),
\prod_{1\le q'\le Q}H_{q'}(n-1)H_{q'}(n+1)
\right)
\mid (n-1)^{3Q}(n+1)^{3Q}.}
\tag{D}
\]

Hence, if

\[
\log R_q(n)\ge \delta n,
\]

then all but `O_Q(log n)` of that logarithmic prime mass is absent from the bounded-`q` adjacent quotients:

\[
\boxed{
\log\frac{R_q(n)}{\gcd\left(R_q(n),
\prod_{q'\le Q}H_{q'}(n-1)H_{q'}(n+1)\right)}
\ge \delta n-O_Q(\log n).}
\tag{E}
\]

So there is no adjacent propagation inequality of the form

\[
\log H_q(n)\gg n
\Longrightarrow
\max_{|h|\le C,\ q'\le Q}\log H_{q'}(n+h)\gg n
\]

that can be obtained by transporting the same target prime factors. The exact source identities delete those factors at `h=+-1`.

For larger fixed offsets, a return of the same prime is an **additional gap-polynomial condition**, not a consequence of the original target. If `p | b_r`, `r+h<p`, then the continuant addition identity gives

\[
p\mid b_{r+h}
\iff
p\mid N_h(r).
\tag{F}
\]

Here `N_1(r)=1`, which is the no-consecutive-zero theorem. At distance two,

\[
\boxed{
p\mid b_{r+2}\iff p\mid P(r+1),}
\tag{G}
\]

and similarly backwards. Thus even a two-step return needs a new congruence; it is not forced by `p | b_r`. Current gap/resultant machinery gives upper bounds on such repeated returns, not the lower bound required for propagation.

There are exact long-range transports, but they disperse a bad row rather than copy it:

- `p`-spaced transport: `p | b_r` at `n=qp+r` also gives the same target prime at `n+kp=(q+k)p+r`, but the displacement is `Theta_q(n)` for fixed nonzero `k`;
- reflection: `b_{p-1-r} == b_r (mod p)` gives the same prime at
  \[
  n_p^*=(2q+1)p-n-1,
  \]
  again generally at linear distance from `n`.

For distinct target primes these destination rows are distinct. Hence the guaranteed propagated mass is only one `O(log n)` prime per destination row, not a comparable `Theta(n)` row mass. The polylogarithmic exceptional-set theorem therefore gives no contradiction.

**Bottom line:** the bad-row propagation attack does not close all indices. The current exact structure exhibits a sharper obstruction: **target mass is adjacent-anti-persistent.** Any successful closure from exceptional-set sparsity would require a genuinely new cross-prime/cross-row theorem forcing *new, different primes* to assemble into comparable mass near `n`; Smith borders, determinants, the Apéry three-term recurrence, reflection, and the present gap identities do not do that.

# 2. Exact adjacent anti-propagation for the target radical

Let

\[
p\in\mathcal T_q(n).
\]

Write

\[
n=qp+r,
\qquad
0\le r<p,
\qquad
p\mid b_r.
\tag{2.1}
\]

Two endpoint facts immediately sharpen the range of `r`.

First,

\[
b_0=1,
\]

so `r != 0`.

Second,

\[
\boxed{b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Indeed in

\[
b_{p-1}
=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}
\binom{p-1}{k}^2
\binom{p-1+k}{k}^2,
\]

the `k=0` term is one and every `k>=1` term has a factor `p^2`.

Therefore every target has

\[
\boxed{1\le r\le p-2.}
\tag{2.3}
\]

The Apéry recurrence is

\[
(j+1)^3b_{j+1}=P(j)b_j-j^3b_{j-1},
\qquad
P(j)=34j^3+51j^2+27j+5.
\tag{2.4}
\]

No two consecutive Apéry values below `p` can both vanish modulo `p`. If

\[
p\mid b_j,
\qquad
p\mid b_{j-1},
\qquad
1\le j<p,
\]

then (2.4), run backwards, forces `p | b_0`, impossible. Applying the same statement to `j+1` gives

\[
\boxed{
p\nmid b_{r-1},\qquad p\nmid b_{r+1}.}
\tag{2.5}
\]

Now use (2.3). At the lower neighboring row,

\[
n-1=qp+(r-1),
\qquad 0\le r-1<p.
\tag{2.6}
\]

At the upper neighboring row,

\[
n+1=qp+(r+1),
\qquad 0<r+1\le p-1.
\tag{2.7}
\]

Thus in both cases the Euclidean quotient by `p` remains exactly `q`. By (2.5), neither neighboring remainder is an Apéry zero. Hence

\[
p\notin\mathcal T_q(n-1),
\qquad
p\notin\mathcal T_q(n+1).
\tag{2.8}
\]

Could `p` move to another quotient class `q'` at the neighboring size? No: the quotient in Euclidean division is unique and is still `q`. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
p\notin\mathcal T_{q'}(n-1)\cup\mathcal T_{q'}(n+1)
\quad\text{for every }q'.}
\tag{2.9}
\]

Taking all prime factors of `R_q(n)` proves (A):

\[
\boxed{
\gcd\left(R_q(n),R_{q'}(n-1)R_{q'}(n+1)\right)=1
\quad\forall q'.}
\tag{2.10}
\]

This is stronger than failure of a lower bound. The current target support is **exactly disjoint** from every adjacent target support.

# 5. The Apéry recurrence does not start a two-step chain

One might hope that although the exact target dies at `n+-1`, the recurrence forces it back at `n+-2`, allowing an iterative fixed-width propagation.

It does not.

Assume first that the indices stay in the nonwrapping range. Since

\[
p\mid b_r,
\qquad
p\nmid b_{r+1},
\]

the recurrence at `r+1` gives

\[
(r+2)^3b_{r+2}
\equiv P(r+1)b_{r+1}\pmod p.
\]

Both `(r+2)^3` and `b_{r+1}` are units when `r+2<p`, so

\[
\boxed{
p\mid b_{r+2}
\iff p\mid P(r+1).}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Likewise, when `r>=2`, the recurrence at `r-1` gives

\[
\boxed{
p\mid b_{r-2}
\iff p\mid P(r-1).}
\tag{5.2}
\]

The right sides are new congruences. They are not consequences of `p | b_r`.

More generally the exact continuant addition identity is

\[
N_{x+1+h}(-1)
=N_{x+2}(-1)N_h(x)
-(x+1)^6N_{x+1}(-1)N_{h-1}(x+1).
\tag{5.3}
\]

Using

\[
N_{x+1}(-1)=(x!)^3b_x,
\]

and imposing `p | b_x`, one obtains in the nonwrapping range `x+h<p`

\[
((x+h)!)^3b_{x+h}
\equiv
((x+1)!)^3b_{x+1}N_h(x)\pmod p.
\tag{5.4}
\]

All factorials and `b_{x+1}` are `p`-units. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
p\mid b_{x+h}\iff p\mid N_h(x).}
\tag{5.5}
\]

This is the exact fixed-offset obstruction.

- `h=1`: `N_1=1`, so return is impossible.
- `h=2`: `N_2(x)=P(x+1)`, giving (5.1).
- `h>=2`: return requires a new zero of a gap continuant.

The existing gap/resultant theory is useful precisely because it can **upper-bound** how often such double returns occur. It supplies no lower bound saying that every selected zero `b_x=0` must produce one of them in a fixed or sublinear window.

Thus there is no recurrence-based iterative mechanism of the form

```text
bad at n
  => bad at some n+h, 1<=|h|<=C
  => bad again nearby
  => ...
```

The first step already fails at `h=1`, and every larger fixed `h` asks for an additional independent return condition `N_h(r)=0`.

# 6. The exact transports that do exist are long-range and dispersive

There are two genuine ways to reuse the same target prime. Neither gives nearby comparable row mass.

## 6.1 `p`-spaced quotient transport

If

\[
n=qp+r,
\qquad p\mid b_r,
\]

then for every integer `k` for which the quotient remains meaningful,

\[
n+kp=(q+k)p+r.
\]

The low digit is unchanged, so

\[
p\in\mathcal T_{q+k}(n+kp).
\tag{6.1}
\]

This is exact. But for fixed `q`, every candidate prime satisfies `p asymp_q n`, so for any fixed nonzero `k`,

\[
|kp|\asymp_q n.
\tag{6.2}
\]

It is not a fixed-length or sublinear displacement.

Moreover, if `p` varies through the `Theta(n/log n)` targets of a bad row, the destinations `n+kp` are distinct. Thus the guaranteed output is one transported prime per destination row, contributing only

\[
\log p=O(\log n)
\]

to that row.

## 6.2 Reflection transport

The Apéry reflection congruence gives

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
\tag{6.3}
\]

Hence a target at

\[
n=qp+r
\]

produces the same prime at the reflected row

\[
\begin{aligned}
n_p^*
&=qp+(p-1-r)\\
&=(2q+1)p-n-1.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.4}
\]

The map

\[
p\mapsto n_p^*
\]

is injective, apart from the harmless possibility that one prime is the midpoint fixed point. Its image lies in a constant-factor neighborhood:

\[
\frac{q}{q+1}n+O(1)
<n_p^*
<\frac{q+1}{q}n+O(1).
\tag{6.5}
\]

But the displacement

\[
n_p^*-n=p-1-2r
\]

can have order `n`; nothing forces it to be sublinear.

Again distinct target primes go to distinct reflected rows, so the guaranteed mass per new row is only one `O(log n)` prime. Reflection converts one dense row into many sparse singleton rows; it does not create many dense rows.

This is exactly compatible with the exceptional-set theorem.

# 8. Why a different-prime propagation theorem would be genuinely new

One logical possibility remains: perhaps the disappearance of the `p`-targets at `n+-1` forces **different primes** `ell` to become targets there, so that the total row mass stays large even though the supports are disjoint.

Nothing in the present identities gives such a statement.

The reason is structural.

1. The Apéry recurrence reduced modulo a prime `p` transports the `p`-adic state `(b_{j-1},b_j)` in the same characteristic. It does not create a divisibility condition modulo an unrelated prime `ell`.
2. Smith/Fitting border identities are also prime-local after localization at a DVR. They explain how the `p`-corank changes under bordering; they do not turn a large set of `p`-local jumps into `ell`-local jumps for different primes.
3. The determinant continuant recurrence is an integer identity, but for a current target prime it propagates only the fact that the adjacent determinant remains divisible by `p` through the baseline corank. That does not imply a new target zero, and it contains no mechanism selecting another prime.
4. The generic adjacent-quotient height no-go makes the same point algebraically: low-rank bordering does not control the near-tail Smith quotient by fixed local border data. The interior Fitting factors can carry arbitrarily large independent information.

Thus a theorem

\[
\log R_q(n)\gg n
\Longrightarrow
\log R_{q'}(n+1)\gg n
\]

with disjoint prime supports would be a new horizontal/cross-characteristic theorem, not a consequence of the current Smith-border or recurrence package.

This is the precise surviving gap.

   in the appropriate forward/backward normalization; it is not implied by `p|b_r`.
