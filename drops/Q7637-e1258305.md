# Proof-grade audit of the Apéry transverse series \(g\) and \(X\)

## Executive verdict

Put
\[
\theta=t\frac d{dt},\qquad D=1-34t+t^2,\qquad A=t(t-17),
\]
and
\[
F(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nt^n,\qquad
g(t)=\frac1{F(t)^2\sqrt D}=\sum_{n\ge0}g_nt^n,
\]
with the square-root branch equal to \(1\) at \(t=0\).  Define
\[
\Xi_r=-1-5\sum_{m=1}^r g_m b_{m-1},\qquad
X(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}\Xi_rt^r.
\]

The clean conclusions are:

1. The exact coefficientwise object behind \(X\) is
   \[
   \boxed{(1-t)X(t)=-1-5\bigl(g\odot tF\bigr)(t).}
   \tag{E1}
   \]
   Equivalently,
   \[
   g\odot tF
   =\operatorname{CT}_z\!\left[g(z)\frac tzF\!\left(\frac tz\right)\right]
   =\operatorname{Diag}_{x,y}\bigl(g(x)yF(y)\bigr).
   \tag{E2}
   \]
   This is a diagonal of a **non-rational** input, not a rational-diagonal representation.

2. There is an exact inhomogeneous Apéry companion \(K(t)=\sum\kappa_rt^r\):
   \[
   \boxed{\mathcal LK=-5(g-1)-t,\qquad \kappa_0=0,\quad\kappa_1=-36,}
   \tag{E3}
   \]
   and
   \[
   \boxed{\Xi_r=r^3\bigl(\kappa_rb_{r-1}-\kappa_{r-1}b_r\bigr)\quad(r\ge1).}
   \tag{E4}
   \]
   Hence also
   \[
   \boxed{X+1=\theta^3\bigl((tF)\odot K-F\odot(tK)\bigr).}
   \tag{E5}
   \]

3. The logarithmic derivative
   \[
   u=\frac{\theta g}{g}
   \]
   satisfies the exact Riccati equation
   \[
   \boxed{
   4D^2\theta u
   =D^2u^2-2ADu+t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28).
   }
   \tag{R}
   \]
   Equivalently \(g\) satisfies the order-two algebraic differential equation
   \[
   \boxed{
   4D^2g\,\theta^2g-5D^2(\theta g)^2
   +2ADg\,\theta g
   -t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28)g^2=0.
   }
   \tag{G}
   \]
   Using the stated differential-Galois hypothesis that the rank-two equation for \(y\) has a group containing \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), (R) is minimal of differential order one for \(u\), and (G) is minimal of differential order two for \(g\) among algebraic differential equations over \(\mathbf C(t)\).

4. The expected orbit argument is valid: after adjoining \(\sqrt D\), the \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) unipotents produce
   \[
   D^{-1/2}(y_1+\lambda y_2)^{-4},\qquad \lambda\in\mathbf C,
   \]
   and arbitrarily large finite subfamilies are linearly independent. Therefore
   \[
   \boxed{g\text{ satisfies no nonzero scalar linear ODE over }\mathbf C(t).}
   \tag{ND-g}
   \]
   In particular \(g\) is not D-finite.

5. **No corresponding theorem for \(X\) follows from this.** In fact
   \[
   X\text{ is D-finite}\iff g\odot tF\text{ is D-finite},
   \]
   and there is no inverse-Hadamard closure theorem allowing one to recover D-finiteness of \(g\) from that of \(g\odot tF\).  Coefficientwise division by \(b_{r-1}\) is not a holonomic closure operation.  Thus non-D-finiteness of \(X\) remains unproved from the stated data.

6. In the standard level-6 eta normalization, \(g(t(\tau))=1/(E(\tau)H(\tau))\) is a meromorphic modular form of weight \(-4\) on \(\Gamma_0(6)\), with normalized Fricke eigenvalue \(-1\).  It has four simple poles on \(X_0(6)\): the two cusps \(1/2,1/3\), and the two Fricke ramification points whose \(t\)-values are the roots of \(D\).  It has no zeros.  Consequently it cannot itself be a finite-rank **linear** algebraic period or a rational diagonal, because either would be D-finite.  This does not deny that \(g\) is an algebraic/rational expression in a genuine period; it is exactly an inverse fourth power of one, up to \(D^{-1/2}\).

7. For every odd prime \(p\), writing
   \[
   A_p(t)=F_{<p}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rt^r,
   \]
   the assumed Apéry Lucas factorization gives the exact Cartier multiplier
   \[
   \boxed{
   g(t)\equiv R_p(t)g(t^p)\pmod p,
   \qquad
   R_p(t)=\frac{D(t)^{(p-1)/2}}{A_p(t)^2}.
   }
   \tag{C1}
   \]
   Thus, for \(0\le r<p\),
   \[
   \boxed{\Lambda_rg=(\Lambda_rR_p)g.}
   \tag{C2}
   \]
   The \(g\)-module is rank one over \(\mathbf F_p(t)\) under Cartier.  But the exact law for \(X\) contains \((\Lambda_rR_p)g\) inside a Hadamard product with \(F\), so it is not a scalar Lucas law for \(\Xi\).  For each fixed \(p\), \(X\bmod p\) is nevertheless algebraic (hence has a finite Cartier automaton); the rank/state complexity is \(p\)-dependent and no uniform finite-rank law follows.

8. Finally, \(p\mid b_r\) and \(p\mid\Xi_r\) are, on the present evidence, **two coefficient/jet functionals at the cusp**, not intersections of two fixed divisors on one fixed finite-type algebraic family.  The mod-\(p\) Kummer model itself depends on \(p\) through \(A_p\) and the exponent \(p-1\), and the relevant jet order is \(r\).  Calling their simultaneous vanishing “geometric transversality” would therefore require an additional theorem that has not been proved.

## 2. Exact Hadamard, constant-term, and diagonal formulas for \(X\)

Write
\[
B(t)=tF(t)=\sum_{r\ge1}b_{r-1}t^r.
\]

\[
\Xi_r-\Xi_{r-1}=-5g_rb_{r-1}\qquad(r\ge1).
\tag{2.1}
\]
Let \(\odot\) denote Hadamard product:
\[
\left(\sum a_rt^r\right)\odot\left(\sum c_rt^r\right)
=\sum a_rc_rt^r.
\]
Then
\[
(g\odot B)(t)=\sum_{r\ge1}g_rb_{r-1}t^r.
\tag{2.2}
\]
Taking first differences of the coefficients of \(X\) gives
\[
\boxed{
(1-t)X(t)=-1-5(g\odot tF)(t).
}
\tag{2.3}
\]
The constant term is correct because the Hadamard product has zero constant coefficient and \(\Xi_0=-1\); the coefficient of \(t\) is
\[
\Xi_1-\Xi_0=-35=-5g_1b_0.
\]

### 2.1 Constant-term realization

Expanding both factors shows
\[
\boxed{
(g\odot tF)(t)
=\operatorname{CT}_z
\left[g(z)\frac tzF\!\left(\frac tz\right)\right].
}
\tag{2.4}
\]
Indeed the constant term forces the exponent of \(z\) from \(g(z)\) to be one more than that from \(F(t/z)\), giving exactly \(g_rb_{r-1}t^r\).

### 2.2 Diagonal realization

\[
\operatorname{Diag}_{x,y}Q

\boxed{

\tag{2.5}

This formula is exact but must not be misread: the input \(g(x)yF(y)\) is not rational.  Hence (2.5) is **not** a rational-diagonal certificate.

## 3. The inhomogeneous companion and the discrete Casoratian

\[
\boxed{\mathcal LK=-5(g-1)-t.}
\tag{3.1}

\boxed{
r^3\kappa_r-P(r-1)\kappa_{r-1}+(r-1)^3\kappa_{r-2}=-5g_r.}

W_r=r^3(\kappa_rb_{r-1}-\kappa_{r-1}b_r).

W_1=\kappa_1b_0-\kappa_0b_1=-36=\Xi_1.

## 4. Modular meaning of \(g\) on \(X_0(6)\)

### 4.1 Dependency of this subsection

The question supplies the modular identity
\[
g(t(q))=\frac1{E(q)H(q)}.
\tag{4.1}
\]
To discuss weight, Fricke sign, and the divisor, one needs the standard level-6 normalization used for the Apéry family:
\[
t(\tau)=
\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)\eta(6\tau)}
{\eta(2\tau)\eta(3\tau)}\right)^{12},
\tag{4.2}
\]
\[
E(\tau)=
\frac{\eta(2\tau)^7\eta(3\tau)^7}
{\eta(\tau)^5\eta(6\tau)^5},
\qquad
H=\delta\log t,
\qquad
\delta=q\frac d{dq}.
\tag{4.3}
\]
These normalization identities are logically separate from the differential-algebraic arguments in §§2–3 and §§5–8.  If (4.2)–(4.3) are not admitted, the modular divisor assertions below should be read as conditional on this standard exact normalization; the rest of the memo is unaffected.

The eta quotient \(E\) has weight \(2\) and trivial nebentypus on \(\Gamma_0(6)\).  Since \(t\) has weight zero, differentiating its modular invariance shows that \(H=\delta\log t\) has weight \(2\).  Hence
\[
\boxed{g=\frac1{EH}\text{ has weight }-4.}
\tag{4.4}
\]
It is a meromorphic modular form, not a modular function/unit.

### 4.2 Fricke character

Let \(W_6\tau=-1/(6\tau)\), with the usual normalized weight slash.  The eta transformation
\[
\eta(-1/\tau)=\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\eta(\tau)
\]
gives directly
\[
t(W_6\tau)=t(\tau).
\tag{4.5}
\]
For \(E\), the powers in (4.3) give
\[
E(W_6\tau)=-6\tau^2E(\tau),
\]
so
\[
E|_2W_6=-E.
\tag{4.6}
\]
Differentiating (4.5) gives
\[
H(W_6\tau)=6\tau^2H(\tau),
\]
therefore
\[
H|_2W_6=+H.
\tag{4.7}
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{g|_{-4}W_6=-g.}
\tag{4.8}
\]
The minus sign is easy to lose: it comes from the factor \((-i\tau)^2=-\tau^2\) in the eta transformation of \(E\).

### 4.3 Exact poles/divisor

The eta-quotient cusp orders are

| cusp | \(\operatorname{ord}(t)\) | \(\operatorname{ord}(E)\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(\infty\) | \(+1\) | \(0\) |
| \(0\) | \(+1\) | \(0\) |
| \(1/2\) | \(-1\) | \(+1\) |
| \(1/3\) | \(-1\) | \(+1\) |

These follow directly from the eta exponents (or Ligozat’s cusp-order formula).  Eta has no zeros in the upper half-plane, so \(E\) has no interior zero.

At every cusp, \(t\) is a local parameter to exponent \(\pm1\), hence \(H=\delta\log t\) has nonzero constant term in the corresponding local trivialization.  Thus \(H\) has no cusp zero.

The identity in the question gives locally
\[
H=E\sqrt D,
\]
and hence globally after squaring,
\[
\boxed{H^2=E^2D(t).}
\tag{4.9}
\]
The two roots of \(D\) are
\[
\alpha_\pm=17\pm12\sqrt2.
\]
Because a square has even local order, (4.9), together with the fact that \(E\) is nonzero in the interior, forces the zeros of \(D(t)\) to occur with even order on \(X_0(6)\).  Equivalently the two values \(\alpha_\pm\) are the two branch values of the Fricke quotient map.  At the corresponding points \(P_\pm\),
\[
\operatorname{ord}_{P_\pm}(t-\alpha_\pm)=2,
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_{P_\pm}H=1.
\tag{4.10}
\]
There are no other zeros of \(H\): weight \(2\) on \(\Gamma_0(6)\) has total divisor degree \(2\) (index \(12\), no elliptic points), and (4.10) already accounts for both.

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{div}_\infty(g)
=(1/2)+(1/3)+P_++P_-,
}
\tag{4.11}
\]
with every pole simple.  There are no zeros.  In the weight \(-4\) line bundle the divisor degree is \(-4\), exactly as the four simple poles require.

### 4.4 What “period” can and cannot mean here

A component of a fixed finite-rank Gauss–Manin system of an algebraic family satisfies a scalar linear Picard–Fuchs equation after elimination.  A diagonal of a rational function in finitely many variables is likewise D-finite in characteristic zero.  Section 6 below proves that \(g\) satisfies **no** nonzero scalar linear ODE over \(\mathbf C(t)\).  Hence
\[
\boxed{
g\text{ is neither a finite-rank linear algebraic period nor a rational diagonal.}}
\tag{4.12}
\]
This statement is deliberately scoped.  It does **not** say that \(g\) has no geometric origin.  On the contrary,
\[
g=D^{-1/2}y^{-4}
\]
is an algebraic/rational expression in a genuine rank-two period.  What fails is linear finite-rank period behavior for \(g\) itself.

For \(X\), no analogous exclusion is currently proved: since non-D-finiteness of \(X\) is open from the stated data, rational-diagonal or finite-rank linear-period status for \(X\) is not ruled out by this argument.

## 5. The exact differential-algebraic equation for \(g\)

Let
\[
v=\frac{\theta y}{y}.
\]
Dividing
\[
4D\theta^2y+4A\theta y+t(t-10)y=0
\]
by \(4Dy\), and using \(\theta^2y/y=\theta v+v^2\), gives
\[
\boxed{
\theta v+v^2+\frac ADv+\frac{t(t-10)}{4D}=0.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]
Since
\[
\frac12\frac{\theta D}{D}=\frac AD,
\]
and \(g=D^{-1/2}y^{-4}\),
\[
\boxed{
u:=\frac{\theta g}{g}=-\frac AD-4v.}
\tag{5.2}
\]
Thus
\[
v=-\frac14\left(u+\frac AD\right).
\]
Substitution into (5.1) yields
\[
4\theta u
=u^2-2\frac ADu-3\frac{A^2}{D^2}
-4\theta\left(\frac AD\right)
+4\frac{t(t-10)}D.
\tag{5.3}
\]
A direct rational simplification uses
\[
\theta\left(\frac AD\right)
=\frac{t(-17t^2+2t-17)}{D^2}
\]
and gives
\[
-3A^2-4D^2\theta(A/D)+4t(t-10)D
=t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28).
\]
Hence the exact Riccati equation is
\[
\boxed{
4D^2\theta u
=D^2u^2-2ADu+t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28).
}
\tag{5.4}
\]
As a low-order check, \(g=1+7t+O(t^2)\), so \(u=7t+O(t^2)\); the linear term of (5.4) is \(28t=4\cdot7t\).

Now
\[
\theta u=\frac{\theta^2g}{g}-\left(\frac{\theta g}{g}\right)^2.
\]
Multiplying (5.4) by \(g^2\) therefore gives
\[
\boxed{
4D^2g\,\theta^2g
-5D^2(\theta g)^2
+2ADg\,\theta g
-t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28)g^2=0.
}
\tag{5.5}
\]
This is an order-two, degree-two algebraic differential equation over \(\mathbf C(t)\).

## 6. Minimality and the \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) orbit proof

### 6.1 Base extension

Work first over
\[
K=\mathbf C(t,\sqrt D).
\]
This is only a finite algebraic extension of \(\mathbf C(t)\).  Passing to it does not change the identity component of the differential Galois group.  Thus the stated hypothesis that the rank-two group contains \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) still supplies the \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) unipotents needed below.

### 6.2 No algebraic order-one equation for \(g\)

The two fields generated by first jets are algebraically equivalent:
\[
g=D^{-1/2}y^{-4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 y^4=\frac{D^{-1/2}}g,
\tag{6.1}
\]
so \(y\) is algebraic over \(K(g)\).  Moreover (5.2) gives
\[
\frac{\theta y}{y}=-\frac14\left(\frac{\theta g}{g}+\frac AD\right),
\tag{6.2}
\]
so \(\theta y\) is algebraic over \(K(g,\theta g)\).  Conversely \(g\) and \(\theta g\) are rational in \(y,\theta y\) over \(K\).  Hence
\[
\operatorname{trdeg}_K K(g,\theta g)
=\operatorname{trdeg}_K K(y,\theta y).
\tag{6.3}
\]

Under \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), the orbit of a nonzero solution vector has dimension \(2\): the stabilizer of a nonzero vector is one-dimensional unipotent.  The differential-Galois orbit therefore gives
\[
\operatorname{trdeg}_K K(y,\theta y)\ge2.
\]
The reverse inequality is automatic because there are only two generators.  Thus
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{trdeg}_K K(g,\theta g)=2.}
\tag{6.4}
\]
Any nonzero algebraic first-order differential relation
\[
P(t,g,\theta g)=0
\]
would force this transcendence degree to be at most \(1\), contradiction.  Therefore (5.5) is minimal of differential order \(2\) for \(g\).

### 6.3 Minimality of the Riccati equation for \(u\)

Equation (5.4) is order one in \(u\).  To reduce it to order zero, \(u\) would have to be algebraic over \(K\).  If it were, (5.2) would make \(v=\theta y/y\) algebraic over a finite algebraic extension of \(K\).  Then the rank-two equation would acquire a one-dimensional differential submodule after finite algebraic base change: equivalently its Riccati equation would have an algebraic solution.

That is incompatible with an identity-component \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), which has no invariant line in its standard representation.  Hence \(u\) is not algebraic and
\[
\boxed{\text{(5.4) is minimal of differential order one for }u.}
\tag{6.5}
\]

This is the precise scope of the minimality claim.  It uses the usual Picard–Vessiot interpretation of “the differential Galois group contains \(\mathrm{SL}_2\).”  If that phrase in the source is intended over a different algebraic base, the claim should first be transported to \(K\); a finite algebraic extension does not alter the identity component.

### 6.4 The infinite linear orbit

Take a fundamental basis \(y_1,y_2\), with the chosen \(y=y_1\).  The unipotent subgroup of \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) sends
\[
y_1\longmapsto y_1+\lambda y_2,
\qquad
\lambda\in\mathbf C.
\]
Since \(\sqrt D\in K\) is fixed, the conjugates of \(g\) include
\[
\boxed{
g_\lambda=D^{-1/2}(y_1+\lambda y_2)^{-4}.}
\tag{6.6}
\]
Suppose distinct \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N\) admitted a constant linear relation
\[
\sum_{i=1}^Nc_i g_{\lambda_i}=0.
\]
Factor out \(D^{-1/2}y_2^{-4}\) and put \(z=y_1/y_2\).  Then
\[
\sum_{i=1}^N c_i(z+\lambda_i)^{-4}=0.
\tag{6.7}
\]
The ratio \(z\) is nonconstant because \(y_1,y_2\) are independent.  Clearing denominators in (6.7) gives a polynomial identity in the nonconstant element \(z\) with constant coefficients, so it must be the zero rational function.  Looking at the distinct fourth-order poles \(z=-\lambda_i\) forces each \(c_i=0\).  Thus every finite subfamily with distinct parameters is linearly independent.

If \(g\) satisfied a scalar linear differential equation of order \(N\) over \(\mathbf C(t)\), every differential-Galois conjugate \(g_\lambda\) would satisfy the same equation, whose solution space has dimension at most \(N\) over \(\mathbf C\).  This contradicts the arbitrarily large linearly independent families above.  Therefore
\[
\boxed{g\text{ satisfies no nonzero linear ODE over }\mathbf C(t).}
\tag{6.8}
\]
In particular \(g\) is not D-finite.

## 7. What can actually be proved about \(X\)?

Set
\[
h=g\odot tF.
\]
Equation (2.3) gives
\[
h=-\frac{1+(1-t)X}{5}.
\tag{7.1}
\]
Since multiplication by a polynomial and addition of a rational function preserve D-finiteness,
\[
\boxed{X\text{ is D-finite}\iff h\text{ is D-finite}.}
\tag{7.2}
\]

The known closure theorem is one-way:

> if both factors are D-finite, then their Hadamard product is D-finite.

Here one factor, \(g\), is **not** D-finite.  There is no converse theorem.  Even though all Apéry coefficients \(b_{r-1}\) are nonzero in characteristic zero and the map
\[
(a_r)\longmapsto (b_{r-1}a_r)
\]
is injective on sequences, its inverse is coefficientwise division by \(b_{r-1}\), and that is not a closure operation for P-recursive sequences.

Concretely, from \(X\) one recovers only
\[
\boxed{
g_r=-\frac{\Xi_r-\Xi_{r-1}}{5b_{r-1}}.}
\tag{7.3}
\]
A fixed-order polynomial recurrence for \(\Xi_r\) would make its first-difference sequence P-recursive, but (7.3) does not imply that \((g_r)\) is P-recursive.

This is the exact obstruction to transferring (6.8) to \(X\).

### 7.1 The companion \(K\) is non-D-finite

One nearby statement *does* follow.  If \(K\) were D-finite, then \(\mathcal LK\) would be D-finite.  From (3.1),
\[
g=1-\frac{\mathcal LK+t}{5},
\]
so \(g\) would be D-finite, contradicting (6.8).  Hence
\[
\boxed{K\text{ is not D-finite}.}
\tag{7.4}
\]
But (3.7) is again a Hadamard/Casoratian transform, so (7.4) still does not imply non-D-finiteness of \(X\).

4D^2\theta u=D^2u^2-2ADu+t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28),
\tag{7.7}

\mathcal LK=-5(g-1)-t.
\tag{7.8}
\]
What does **not** close as an ordinary finite differential system is the last map
\[
X+1=\theta^3\bigl((tF)\odot K-F\odot(tK)\bigr).
\tag{7.9}
\]
The Hadamard operator remains external.

In the canonical level-6 period ratio \(q\), there is a useful additional modular gauge.  If \(E=F(t(q))\) and the standard logarithmic second solution is normalized so that \(E,E\log q,E(\log q)^2\) are a basis for the symmetric-square equation, then for any \(U(q)\)
\[
\boxed{\mathcal L(EU)=g\,\delta^3U.}
\tag{7.10}
\]
The leading coefficient is
\[
\frac{ED}{H^3}=\frac1{E^2\sqrt D}=g,
\]
and the lower \(\delta\)-terms vanish because the conjugated operator annihilates \(1,\log q,(\log q)^2\).  Therefore, for \(K=EU\),
\[
\boxed{
\delta^3\left(\frac KE\right)=(5-t)EH-5.
}
\tag{7.11}
\]
So the inhomogeneous companion itself has an Eichler-type modular description.  Importantly, (7.11) still does not remove the Hadamard product from (7.9); it is not a proof that \(X\) belongs to a finite differential field.

### 7.3 A concrete identity that would close the question

A genuinely useful next target is to prove or refute an identity of the form
\[
\boxed{
(g\odot tF)(t)
=R\bigl(t,F,\theta F,\theta^2F,g,\theta g,K,\theta K,\theta^2K\bigr)
}
\tag{7.12}
\]
with \(R\) algebraic/rational over \(\mathbf C(t)\), or any finite differential-algebraic extension of this form.  Such an identity would convert the coefficientwise transform into a fixed finite differential system.

Absent (7.12), the non-D-finiteness of \(X\) is not proved.  An equivalent missing holonomic input would be a D-finite Hadamard inverse for \(tF\), i.e. control of
\[
\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{t^r}{b_{r-1}},
\]
but no such property follows from the Apéry recurrence and it should not be assumed.

## 8. Exact Lucas/Cartier law for \(g\)

Let \(p\) be an odd prime.  Work in \(\mathbf F_p[[t]]\) and put
\[
A_p(t)=F_{<p}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rt^r,
\qquad A_p(0)=1.
\]
The assumed Lucas factorization is
\[
F(t)=A_p(t)F(t^p).
\tag{8.1}
\]
Since
\[
D(t^p)=D(t)^p
\]
and both square roots have constant term \(1\),
\[
\sqrt{D(t^p)}=\sqrt{D(t)}^{\,p}.
\tag{8.2}
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
g(t)
&=\frac1{A_p(t)^2F(t^p)^2\sqrt{D(t)}}\\
&=\frac{D(t)^{(p-1)/2}}{A_p(t)^2}
   \frac1{F(t^p)^2\sqrt{D(t^p)}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{
g(t)=R_p(t)g(t^p),\qquad
R_p(t)=\frac{D(t)^{(p-1)/2}}{A_p(t)^2}.}
\tag{8.3}
\]
This is the exact rational multiplier requested in the question.

Because coefficients lie in \(\mathbf F_p\), \(g(t^p)=g(t)^p\), and hence
\[
\boxed{
g(t)^{p-1}=\frac{A_p(t)^2}{D(t)^{(p-1)/2}}.}
\tag{8.4}
\]
So \(g\bmod p\) is algebraic over \(\mathbf F_p(t)\).

### 8.1 Cartier sections

For \(0\le r<p\), define
\[
\Lambda_r\left(\sum_{n\ge0}c_nt^n\right)
=\sum_{n\ge0}c_{pn+r}t^n.
\]
The elementary semilinearity rule is
\[
\Lambda_r\bigl(R(t)S(t^p)\bigr)
=(\Lambda_rR)(t)S(t).
\tag{8.5}
\]
Applying it to (8.3) gives
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_rg=(\Lambda_rR_p)g.
}
\tag{8.6}
\]
Rational power series are stable under Cartier, so \(\Lambda_rR_p\in\mathbf F_p(t)\).  Thus the Cartier orbit of \(g\) has rank one over the rational-function field, even though its scalar multipliers depend on the digit \(r\).

For comparison, (8.1) gives the classical scalar law
\[
\boxed{\Lambda_rF=b_rF.}
\tag{8.7}
\]
For \(B=tF\), the shift matters:
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_rB=b_{r-1}F\quad(1\le r<p),
}
\tag{8.8}
\]
while
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_0B=b_{p-1}\,tF.
}
\tag{8.9}
\]
Equation (8.9) follows because, for \(n\ge1\),
\[
b_{pn-1}=b_{p-1}b_{n-1}.
\]

### 8.2 Exact nonclosed Cartier law for the transverse Hadamard product

Let
\[
h=g\odot B.
\]
Cartier commutes with Hadamard product coefficientwise:
\[
\Lambda_r(A\odot B)=(\Lambda_rA)\odot(\Lambda_rB).
\tag{8.10}
\]
Combining (8.6), (8.8), and (8.9),
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_rh
=b_{r-1}\bigl(((\Lambda_rR_p)g)\odot F\bigr)
\quad(1\le r<p),
}
\tag{8.11}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_0h
=b_{p-1}\bigl(((\Lambda_0R_p)g)\odot tF\bigr).
}
\tag{8.12}
\]
The multiplier \(\Lambda_rR_p\) sits **inside one input of the Hadamard product**.  Therefore (8.11) is not a scalar law \(\Lambda_rh=c_rh\), and it does not yield one for \(X\).

The exact \(X\)-section equations follow from \((1-t)X=-1-5h\).  For \(1\le r<p\),
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_rX-\Lambda_{r-1}X=-5\Lambda_rh,
}
\tag{8.13}
\]
and for the wraparound digit,
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_0X-t\Lambda_{p-1}X=-1-5\Lambda_0h.
}
\tag{8.14}
\]
Equations (8.11)–(8.14) are the honest Cartier law produced by the data.  It is a nonclosed Hadamard law at the uniform level.

There is nevertheless a fixed-prime finiteness statement.  Equation (8.1) makes \(F\bmod p\) algebraic, because
\[
F^{p-1}=A_p^{-1}.
\]
Equation (8.4) makes \(g\bmod p\) algebraic.  Over a finite field, coefficient sequences of algebraic one-variable power series are \(p\)-automatic, and termwise products of automatic sequences are automatic.  Hence
\[
h=g\odot tF
\]
and therefore \(X\) are algebraic over \(\mathbf F_p(t)\) for each fixed \(p\).  So a finite Cartier automaton for \(X\bmod p\) **exists**.

What is missing is uniformity: the equation for \(g\) contains \(A_p\) of degree \(p-1\) and exponent \(p-1\), and the resulting Cartier state space may grow with \(p\).  This is not the fixed-rank Lucas law needed for a geometric transversality argument.

### 8.3 The range \(r<p\)

Since \(g(t^p)=1+O(t^p)\), equation (8.3) gives, for \(0\le m<p\),
\[
\boxed{g_m=[t^m]R_p(t).}
\tag{8.15}
\]
Thus for a target index \(r<p\),
\[
\boxed{
\Xi_r\equiv
-1-5\sum_{m=1}^r b_{m-1}[t^m]
\frac{D(t)^{(p-1)/2}}{A_p(t)^2}\pmod p.
}
\tag{8.16}
\]
This is exact, but still a coefficient functional rather than an evaluation of a fixed algebraic function at a point.

For \(p=5\), one has the particularly simple consequence
\[
\Xi_r\equiv-1\pmod5
\]
for every \(r\), directly from the definition.  Thus \(5\) can never divide \(\Xi_r\).  No scan is involved.

## 9. Why simultaneous coefficient zeros are not yet divisor intersections

The two target congruences are
\[
p\mid b_r,
\qquad
p\mid\Xi_r.
\]
In characteristic zero they are literally jet coordinates:
\[
b_r=[t^r]F(t)
=\operatorname{Res}_{t=0}\frac{F(t)}{t^{r+1}}\,dt,
\tag{9.1}
\]
\[
\Xi_r=[t^r]X(t)
=\operatorname{Res}_{t=0}\frac{X(t)}{t^{r+1}}\,dt.
\tag{9.2}
\]
The pole order in the testing differential grows with \(r\).  Thus these are linear functionals on an order-\(r\) jet, not values of fixed functions at a moving algebraic point.

Modulo \(p\), the first coefficient has the unusually strong interpretation
\[
\Lambda_rF=b_rF.
\tag{9.3}
\]
Since \(F(0)=1\),
\[
p\mid b_r\iff \Lambda_rF=0.
\tag{9.4}
\]
But this is vanishing of an **\(r\)-labelled Cartier operator output**, not vanishing of \(F\) along a divisor.  For \(X\), even this scalar eigenlaw is absent; its exact section formula is (8.11)–(8.14).

There is a second, genuinely geometric obstruction to a fixed-family interpretation.  For each prime \(p\), equation (8.4) places \(g\bmod p\) on the Kummer cover
\[
Y^{p-1}D(t)^{(p-1)/2}=A_p(t)^2.
\tag{9.5}
\]
Both the exponent and the polynomial \(A_p\) depend on \(p\).  Thus these covers do not arise, from the current formulas, as fibers of one fixed finite-rank algebraic family with bounded defining complexity.  Likewise, the jet order needed to read the target coefficient is \(r\).

A genuine fixed-divisor reduction would require an additional construction of the following kind:

- a fixed finite-type scheme or algebraic stack \(\mathscr V/\mathbf Z[1/N]\);
- two fixed rational sections/functions \(B,\mathcal X\) on \(\mathscr V\);
- points \(P_{p,r}\in\mathscr V(\mathbf F_p)\) of uniformly controlled complexity;
- exact equivalences
  \[
  b_r\equiv0\pmod p\iff B(P_{p,r})=0,
  \qquad
  \Xi_r\equiv0\pmod p\iff \mathcal X(P_{p,r})=0;
  \]
- and enough height/degree control to make intersection theory nonvacuous.

Nothing in (2.3), (3.6), or (8.3) supplies such an object.  One can package all coefficients in an infinite jet/pro-algebraic space, but that is precisely not the fixed finite-type setting needed for a Bezout or transversality bound.

Therefore the correct present statement is
\[
\boxed{
\text{the pair }(b_r,\Xi_r)\text{ remains a pair of coefficient functionals,}
}
\tag{9.6}
\]
not a proved intersection of two divisors on a fixed algebraic family.

This also explains why the modular divisor of \(g\) in §4 does not by itself control primes dividing \(g_r\), \(b_r\), or \(\Xi_r\): zeros/poles of a modular form as a function on \(X_0(6)\) and zero congruences of its **Taylor coefficients at a cusp** are different geometric questions.

## 10. Reproducible symbolic checks (no finite scan)

The following checks are algebraic identities, not searches over indices.

### Check A: initial terms

Use only
\[
F=1+5t+O(t^2),\qquad
D=1-34t+t^2.
\]
Then
\[
F^{-2}=1-10t+O(t^2),
\qquad
D^{-1/2}=1+17t+O(t^2),
\]
so
\[
g=1+7t+O(t^2),
\]
and
\[
\Xi_0=-1,\qquad \Xi_1=-1-5\cdot7=-36.
\]

### Check B: Riccati reduction

Starting from
\[
4D(\theta v+v^2)+4Av+t(t-10)=0,
\]
substitute
\[
v=-\frac14\left(u+\frac AD\right).
\]
After multiplying by \(D^2\), the constant numerator is exactly
\[
-3A^2-4D^2\theta(A/D)+4t(t-10)D
=t(t^3-6t^2+489t+28).
\]
This reproduces (5.4) without computing any coefficient beyond \(g_1\).

### Check C: Casoratian

Subtract
\[
\kappa_{r-1}\bigl(r^3b_r-P(r-1)b_{r-1}+(r-1)^3b_{r-2}\bigr)=0
\]
from
\[
b_{r-1}\bigl(r^3\kappa_r-P(r-1)\kappa_{r-1}+(r-1)^3\kappa_{r-2}\bigr)
=-5g_rb_{r-1}.
\]
The result is exactly
\[
W_r-W_{r-1}=-5g_rb_{r-1}.
\]
Together with \(W_1=-36\), this proves (3.6) for all \(r\), with no scan.

### Check D: Cartier multiplier

In \(\mathbf F_p[[t]]\), simply divide
\[
F(t)=A_p(t)F(t^p)
\]
inside
\[
g(t)=F(t)^{-2}D(t)^{-1/2},
\]
and use
\[
D(t^p)^{1/2}=D(t)^{p/2}
\]
in the branch with constant term \(1\).  The quotient is exactly
\[
R_p=D^{(p-1)/2}/A_p^2.
\]
No coefficient experiment is involved.

## 11. What is proved, what is not, and the next sharp target

### Proved from the stated data (plus the explicitly isolated standard level-6 eta normalization in §4)

- the Hadamard/constant-term/diagonal identities (2.3)–(2.5);
- the exact inhomogeneous companion and Casoratian (3.1)–(3.7);
- the Riccati equation (5.4) and order-two algebraic differential equation (5.5);
- minimal differential order one for \(u\) and two for \(g\), under the stated \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) differential-Galois hypothesis;
- no nonzero scalar linear ODE for \(g\), hence non-D-finiteness of \(g\);
- non-D-finiteness of \(K\);
- weight \(-4\), Fricke eigenvalue \(-1\), and the four simple poles of \(g\) on \(X_0(6)\) under the standard eta normalization;
- the exact mod-\(p\) multiplier (8.3), Cartier law (8.6), and nonclosed Hadamard section law (8.11)–(8.14);
- fixed-\(p\) algebraicity/automaticity of \(X\bmod p\);
- the scoped obstruction to interpreting simultaneous coefficient zeros as intersections of fixed divisors.

### Not proved

- that \(X\) is non-D-finite or that \((\Xi_r)\) has no fixed-order polynomial recurrence;
- a uniform finite-rank Cartier/Lucas law for \(\Xi\) independent of \(p\);
- any geometric transversality theorem for the coefficient zeros \(b_r\equiv\Xi_r\equiv0\pmod p\);
- a fixed finite-type algebraic family whose divisor intersection is equivalent to those two coefficient congruences.

### Smallest concrete next identity

The most decisive next step is not another recurrence guess for \(\Xi\).  It is to attack the Hadamard bottleneck directly:
\[
\boxed{
H_X(t):=(g\odot tF)(t)
=\operatorname{CT}_z\left[g(z)\frac tzF(t/z)\right].
}
\]
Either:

1. prove a finite differential-algebraic telescoping identity expressing \(H_X\) in the differential field generated by \(F,g,K\) and finitely many derivatives, as in (7.12); or
2. prove that no such finite-rank telescoper exists, by transporting the \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) orbit through the constant-term functional and proving that infinitely many transformed constant terms remain linearly independent.

The second route is exactly where a new theorem is needed: the constant-term/Hadamard functional can annihilate large parts of an infinite Galois orbit, so the linear independence of the functions \((y_1+\lambda y_2)^{-4}\) **before** applying the transform does not imply linear independence **after** applying it.  Establishing that nondegeneracy is the missing bridge to a proof that \(X\) is non-D-finite.
