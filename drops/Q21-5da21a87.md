# Selberg sieve, GPY, and pointwise control of Apéry bad primes

## Executive summary

The Selberg upper-bound sieve does not run in reverse. It can estimate the measure of the exceptional set
\[
\{n:K(n)>0\},
\]
but it does not control the largest value of the counting function
\[
K(n)=\sum_{N<p\le n}1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}.
\]
A bound
\[
\max K(n)=N^{o(1)}
\]
is a high-moment / discrepancy statement, not a first-moment sieve statement.

The correct analogue is closer to proving that the random variable K has Poisson-type tails:
\[
\#\{n:K(n)\ge r\}\ll N\frac{(C\lambda)^r}{r!},
\]
uniformly for r growing slowly. This requires controlling r-fold correlations of the CRT events.

The GPY philosophy is relevant, but its usual mechanism is not directly applicable: GPY finds existence of unusually small weighted sums, whereas we need exclusion of unusually large sums.

---

# 1. Why Selberg sieve alone cannot bound max K

Write
\[
f_p(n)=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}.
\]
Then
\[
K(n)=\sum_p f_p(n).
\]
The Selberg sieve controls
\[
\sum_n \prod_{p\in P}(1-f_p(n)),
\]
which is the zero-hit population.

More generally, the upper-bound sieve controls sets defined by requiring many forbidden residue conditions. For example
\[
\#\{n:f_p(n)=1\ \text{for all }p\in S\}
\]
can be estimated.

However max K requires controlling the union of all large intersections:
\[
\{K\ge r\}=\bigcup_{|S|=r}\{f_p=1\ \forall p\in S\}.
\]
The naive union bound gives
\[
\sum_{|S|=r}\prod_{p\in S}\frac{|Z_p|}{p},
\]
which is the correct Poisson main term, but loses the factorial cancellation needed for sharp tails.

Thus Selberg gives distributional upper bounds only if combined with high-order correlation estimates.

---

# 2. The right sieve object: factorial moments

Define
\[
(K)_r=K(K-1)\cdots(K-r+1).
\]
Then
\[
\mathbb E(K)_r
=\sum_{p_1<\cdots<p_r}
\Pr(f_{p_1}=\cdots=f_{p_r}=1).
\]

If CRT independence holds strongly enough,
\[
\Pr(f_{p_1}=\cdots=f_{p_r}=1)
\approx \prod_i\frac{|Z_{p_i}|}{p_i},
\]
and therefore
\[
\mathbb E(K)_r\approx \lambda^r.
\]

Markov gives
\[
\Pr(K\ge r)\le \frac{\mathbb E(K)_r}{r!}
\approx \frac{\lambda^r}{r!}.
\]
Choosing
\[
r=C\log N/\log\log N
\]
would already imply
\[
\max K(n)=O(\log N/\log\log N)
\]
with overwhelming probability.

Therefore the missing theorem is not Selberg; it is a high-order CRT correlation theorem.

---

# 3. Can GPY be adapted?

The GPY quadratic form is
\[
\sum_n\left(\sum_p\lambda_p f_p(n)-1\right)^2.
\]
Expanding gives first and second moments:
\[
\sum_p\lambda_p^2\sum_n f_p(n)
+\sum_{p\ne q}\lambda_p\lambda_q\sum_n f_p(n)f_q(n).
\]

This is exactly a pair-correlation object.

For Apéry zeros, pair CRT independence already gives the second-order estimate:
\[
\sum_n f_p(n)f_q(n)
=\frac{|Z_p||Z_q|}{pq}N+O(1).
\]

But max K requires moments of all orders:
\[
\sum_n f_{p_1}(n)\cdots f_{p_r}(n).
\]
A GPY quadratic form cannot see r-fold clustering.

So GPY gives an HM_2 type statement, while the desired result requires HM_r for growing r.

---

# 4. What would a successful GPY-style replacement look like?

The analogue would be a Selberg polynomial weight designed to penalize large K:

\[
W(n)=\left(\sum_p a_p f_p(n)-L\right)^2.
\]

If one could prove
\[
\sum_n W(n)=o(N),
\]
then almost every n has K near its expected value.

But to prove a pointwise bound, one needs weights involving higher powers:

\[
\sum_n (K(n))_r.
\]

The natural object is therefore a higher-dimensional Selberg sieve, not GPY.

---

# 5. Buchstab and combinatorial sieve

Buchstab's identity decomposes
\[
\{K\ge r\}
\]
into nested prime selections. Schematically:

\[
\#\{K\ge r\}
\approx
\sum_{p_1<\cdots<p_r}
\#\{n:f_{p_i}(n)=1\ \forall i\}.
\]

This is useful because CRT gives the inner count:

\[
=\frac{N\prod |Z_{p_i}|}{p_i}+O(1).
\]

The obstruction is the accumulated CRT discrepancy:

\[
\sum_{p_1<\cdots<p_r}O(1).
\]

For r=3 this is exactly the triple discrepancy problem identified in the LGTQ-3 analysis.

---

# 6. Selberg sieve interpretation of the Apéry problem

The Apéry-specific input needed is not the sieve weights but cancellation of the CRT errors.

A sufficient theorem would be:

For every fixed r,
\[
\sum_{p_1<\cdots<p_r}
\left(
\#\{n:n\bmod p_i\in Z_{p_i}\}-N\prod_i\frac{|Z_{p_i}|}{p_i}
\right)
\ll N^{1/2+o(1)}.
\]

Then
\[
\mathbb E(K)_r=\lambda^r+o(1),
\]
and the Poisson tail follows.

This is the true Apéry analogue of the large sieve.

---

# 7. Spectral gap viewpoint

The CRT map
\[
\mathbb Z/Q\mathbb Z\to\prod_{p|Q}\mathbb F_p
\]
is an isomorphism. As a finite abelian group it has no intrinsic mixing: translation acts as a permutation.

Therefore there is no spectral gap for the CRT map itself.

A spectral gap can only arise after introducing an additional averaging operator, for example:

- averaging over primes;
- averaging over Apéry zero sets;
- averaging over multiplicative characters.

This becomes a large-sieve/Deligne-type problem, not a pure CRT problem.

---

# 8. Comparison with BV

Bombieri-Vinogradov succeeds because the von Mangoldt function has analytic structure and the large sieve controls averages over many moduli.

Here:

- the moduli are not varying;
- the translate variable is the averaging parameter;
- the CRT modulus is enormous:
\[
Q=\prod p\approx e^{X},
\]
while the interval length is only
\[
M=X^2.
\]

The ratio M/Q is extremely small. This helps CRT independence but hurts cancellation: each individual CRT class appears zero or one times.

Thus the problem is not lack of independence; it is cancellation of the signed discrepancy errors.

---

# 9. Final answer

(a) A Vaughan/BV decomposition is unlikely to be the right tool. The natural decomposition is factorial moments, not bilinear sums.

(b) The large sieve analogue is a multi-dimensional CRT discrepancy inequality controlling
\[
\sum_S |D_S|^2.
\]
This is currently the missing ingredient.

(c) Being far below the BV modulus barrier helps pairwise independence but does not solve the accumulated ±1 CRT errors.

(d) Motohashi, Vinogradov, and Elliott-Halberstam methods address different averaging structures and do not directly apply.

(e) A fixed modulus has no BV theorem because there is no modulus averaging. One needs translate averaging plus character cancellation.

(f) The CRT product itself has no spectral gap. Any useful spectral gap must come from arithmetic structure of Apéry zero sets.

(g) Selberg sieve alone cannot prove max K. It can prove tail bounds once high-order correlation estimates are available.

The decisive theorem is therefore:

**Apéry high-moment CRT dispersion:** for r up to c log N/loglog N,
\[
\mathbb E(K)_r\le (C\lambda)^r.
\]

Proving this is essentially equivalent to proving the desired
\[
\max_n K(n)=N^{o(1)}.
\]
The bottleneck is not classical sieve technology; it is an Apéry-specific replacement for the large sieve controlling r-dimensional CRT discrepancy.
