# Q451 — Dwork truncation, Hasse factorization, roots, and coefficient support

## Executive verdict

Write

\[
H_p(t):=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rt^r\in\mathbf F_p[t].
\]

This is the polynomial denoted `A_p` in the current P3.2 repository. Its factorization is now known exactly:

\[
\boxed{
H_p(t)=\Delta(t)^{\varepsilon_p}B_p(t)^2,
\qquad
\Delta(t)=t^2-34t+1,
\qquad
\varepsilon_p=\frac{1-\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)}2.
}
\tag{E1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\varepsilon_p=
\begin{cases}
0,&p\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{24},\\
1,&p\equiv13,17,19,23\pmod{24}.
\end{cases}
\tag{E2}
\]

The normalized factor satisfies \(B_p(0)=1\) and

\[
\boxed{
\deg B_p=\frac{p-1-2\varepsilon_p}{2}.
}
\tag{E3}
\]

This immediately answers the main geometric questions, but it does **not** bound the coefficient-zero set

\[
Z_p=\{r:b_r=0\pmod p\}.
\]

The reason is a dual-space distinction:

- roots of \(H_p(t)\) are evaluation zeros in the geometric parameter \(t\);
- \(Z_p\) is the set of zero **monomial coefficients** of \(H_p\), equivalently zero finite-Mellin moments of its value function.

The factorization controls the first object. It does not prevent cancellations among the convolution coefficients of \(B_p^2\).

The direct answers are:

1. **Factorization:** (E1) is the precise theorem. The factor \(B_p\) has degree approximately \(p/2\), not bounded degree.
2. **Number of roots:** over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\), the root set is \(V(B_p)\cup V(\Delta)\) when \(arepsilon_p=1\). If \(B_p\) is squarefree and coprime to \(\Delta\), the smooth factor has exactly \(\deg B_p\) distinct geometric roots. Over \(\mathbf F_p\), the count is the number of linear factors of \(B_p\), plus any rational discriminant roots; there is no universal formula.
3. **Coefficient support versus roots:** no useful deterministic implication follows in either direction. The exact relation is a quadratic convolution, not a root-count inequality.
4. **At \(p=19\):** \(H_{19}\) factors completely as displayed in Section 6 below and has exactly the two \(\mathbf F_{19}\)-roots \(7,11\), each of multiplicity two.
5. **At \(p=73\):** \(H_{73}=B_{73}^2\) with an explicit degree-36 polynomial \(B_{73}\). Its \(\mathbf F_{73}\)-roots are exactly \(9,65\), each of multiplicity two.
6. **Bounding \(|Z_p|\):** the square factorization alone does not improve the current recurrence/gap-polynomial bound. A bound on \(|Z_p|\) requires anti-cancellation information for the coefficients of \(B_p^2\), which is essentially another form of the original coefficient problem.

There are also three important corrections to the proposed geometric interpretation.

- For a K3 surface, **supersingular** means formal Brauer height \(h=\infty\), not height \(2\).
- The roots of \(\Delta\) are discriminant/bad-fiber parameters; they should not be counted as smooth supersingular K3 fibers.
- A Dwork unit-root formula is a limit of ratios of **higher truncations** after a choice of gauge. The rank-three Frobenius matrix is not literally \(H_p(t^p)/H_p(t)\).

## 1. The precise factorization of \(H_p\)

### 1.1 The theorem

For every prime \(p\ge5\), put

\[
\Delta(t)=t^2-34t+1,
\qquad
\varepsilon_p=\frac{1-\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)}2.
\]

Then there is a unique polynomial \(B_p\in\mathbf F_p[t]\), normalized by \(B_p(0)=1\), such that

\[
\boxed{H_p(t)=\Delta(t)^{\varepsilon_p}B_p(t)^2.}
\tag{1.1}
\]

The congruence classes are

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
p\bmod24&\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)&\varepsilon_p&\text{factorization}\\ \hline
1,5,7,11&1&0&H_p=B_p^2\\
13,17,19,23&-1&1&H_p=\Delta B_p^2.
\end{array}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Degree comparison gives

\[
\deg B_p=
\begin{cases}
(p-1)/2,&\varepsilon_p=0,\\
(p-3)/2,&\varepsilon_p=1.
\end{cases}
\tag{1.3}
\]

So the moving factor has degree \(\Theta(p)\). The fact that the underlying Picard–Fuchs family has fixed rank does not make its characteristic-\(p\) Hasse section a bounded-degree polynomial.

### 1.2 Explicit universal square-root truncations

The repository gives more than existence. Define rational sequences \(c_r,d_r\) by

\[
c_{-1}=d_{-1}=0,
\qquad
c_0=d_0=1,
\]

\[
4(r+1)^2c_{r+1}
=2(68r^2+34r+5)c_r-(2r-1)^2c_{r-1},
\tag{1.4}
\]

\[
4(r+1)^2d_{r+1}
=2(68r^2+102r+39)d_r-(2r+1)^2d_{r-1}.
\tag{1.5}
\]

In characteristic zero, if

\[
C_+(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}c_rt^r,
\qquad
C_-(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}d_rt^r,
\]

then

\[
\mathcal A(t):=\sum_{r\ge0}b_rt^r
=C_+(t)^2
=\Delta(t)C_-(t)^2.
\tag{1.6}
\]

The normalized finite factors are the truncations

\[
\boxed{
B_p(t)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{(p-1)/2}\overline{c_r}t^r,
&\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=1,\\[8pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{(p-3)/2}\overline{d_r}t^r,
&\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=-1.
\end{cases}}
\tag{1.7}
\]

Thus the factorization is not merely existential; \(B_p\) comes from one of two fixed second-order recurrences.

### 1.3 What is proved and what is only computationally observed

The published theorem proves (1.1). It does not by itself state that \(B_p\) is squarefree or coprime to \(\Delta\).

The repository's exact finite computation for all \(2260\) primes \(5\le p\le20000\) found:

```text
B_p squarefree;
gcd(B_p, Delta) = 1;
B_p divides t^(p^2)-t;
every irreducible factor of B_p has degree 1 or 2.
```

These are exact statements in that finite range, but they must not be promoted to an all-prime theorem without a proof or reference.

## 2. What Dwork's Frobenius formula actually says

Let

\[
F_s(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{p^s-1}b_nt^n.
\]

Thus \(F_1=H_p\). In a Dwork-compatible toric period normalization, the higher truncations satisfy congruences which make the ratios

\[
\frac{F_s(\widetilde t)}{F_{s-1}(\widetilde t^{\,p})}
\]

converge on the ordinary locus. Up to the conventional reciprocal and gauge, the unit-root function is

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal U_p(t)
=\lim_{s\to\infty}
\frac{F_s(\widetilde t)}{F_{s-1}(\widetilde t^{\,p})}.
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

Here \(\widetilde t\) is a \(p\)-adic lift of the geometric parameter. At a Teichmüller point, \(\widetilde t^p=\widetilde t\).

The formula proposed in the question,

\[
\Phi(t)=H_p(t^p)/H_p(t),
\]

is therefore not the precise statement for three reasons.

1. It uses only the first truncation rather than the convergent tower \(F_s\).
2. The ratio orientation depends on the Frobenius/gauge convention.
3. The Apéry Picard–Fuchs system has rank \(3\); its Frobenius is a matrix. \(H_p\) is a scalar Hasse–Witt coordinate controlling the ordinary locus, not the whole matrix.

The first truncation still has the key geometric meaning: where the scalar Hasse invariant vanishes, the unit-root formula ceases to be invertible in that coordinate.

## 3. Evaluation roots and the supersingular locus

### 3.1 Root divisor over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\)

Set-theoretically,

\[
V(H_p)=V(B_p)\cup
\begin{cases}
\varnothing,&\varepsilon_p=0,\\
V(\Delta),&\varepsilon_p=1.
\end{cases}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Every root of \(B_p\) occurs in \(H_p\) with even multiplicity. Every root of \(\Delta\) has its multiplicity shifted by one.

If \(B_p\) is squarefree and coprime to \(\Delta\), then the number of distinct geometric roots is

\[
\#V(H_p)(\overline{\mathbf F}_p)
=\deg B_p+2\varepsilon_p
=\frac{p-1+2\varepsilon_p}{2}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The smooth Hasse factor has

\[
\boxed{
\deg B_p=\frac{p-1-2\varepsilon_p}{2}\sim\frac p2.
}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Without squarefreeness, \(\deg B_p\) is a multiplicity count and only an upper bound for the number of distinct smooth parameters.

### 3.2 The discriminant roots are not supersingular K3 fibers

The polynomial

\[
\Delta(t)=t^2-34t+1
\]

is the singular-fiber discriminant appearing in the factorization. Its roots are bad parameters of the pencil. They should not be counted as smooth supersingular K3 surfaces.

Thus, in the intended geometric interpretation:

```text
B_p(t)=0       smooth nonordinary/supersingular locus;
Delta(t)=0     singular/bad fibers.
```

This distinction explains why the defect branch contains an extra quadratic factor.

### 3.3 Correction about K3 height

For K3 surfaces:

- ordinary means formal Brauer height \(h=1\);
- finite height \(h=2\) is nonordinary but **not** supersingular;
- Artin supersingular means \(h=\infty\), equivalently all crystalline \(H^2\)-slopes are \(1\).

For a finite-height K3 surface one has the inequality

\[
\rho\le22-2h.
\tag{3.4}
\]

In a smooth rank-19 lattice-polarized family, every fiber carries at least \(19\) algebraic classes. Hence a finite-height smooth fiber can only have \(h=1\). Consequently, once the geometric Hasse-invariant identification is fixed, a smooth nonordinary fiber in this rank-19 family is forced to be supersingular.

This argument does not apply at \(\Delta=0\), where the fiber is singular.

### 3.4 Number of roots in \(\mathbf F_p\)

The number of rational smooth roots is exactly

\[
\boxed{
s_p:=\#\{t\in\mathbf F_p:B_p(t)=0\}
=\deg\gcd(B_p(t),t^p-t).
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

There is no known closed formula for \(s_p\) from (1.1) alone.

The discriminant of \(\Delta\) is

\[
34^2-4=1152=24^2\cdot2.
\]

Therefore, for \(p\ge5\), \(\Delta\) has

\[
1+\left(\frac2p\right)
\]

roots in \(\mathbf F_p\): either \(0\) or \(2\).

If \(B_p\) is coprime to \(\Delta\), then

\[
\#V(H_p)(\mathbf F_p)
=s_p+\varepsilon_p\left(1+\left(\frac2p\right)\right).
\tag{3.6}
\]

The expectation “approximately \(p/12\)” is not supported by the factorization. Over the algebraic closure the smooth divisor has degree approximately \(p/2\). Over \(\mathbf F_p\), only the linear factors count, and their number can fluctuate substantially.

## 4. Coefficient support lives in the Mellin-dual space

Define

\[
Z_p=\{r\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}:b_r=0\pmod p\},
\qquad
S_p=p-|Z_p|.
\]

The number \(S_p\) is the monomial support size of \(H_p\). It is unrelated in any automatic way to the number of evaluation roots.

### 4.1 Exact finite-Mellin identity

For \(1\le j\le p-2\), multiplicative orthogonality gives

\[
\boxed{
 b_j=-\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}H_p(t)t^{-j}.
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Thus \(b_j=0\) means that the \(j\)-th multiplicative Fourier/Mellin coefficient of the value function \(t\mapsto H_p(t)\) vanishes.

This is the exact relationship between roots and coefficient zeros. It is global and Fourier-theoretic; it is not an identification of the two zero loci.

### 4.2 The convolution formula

Write

\[
B_p(t)=\sum_m\beta_mt^m,
\qquad
C_r:=\sum_{i+j=r}\beta_i\beta_j.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Then

\[
b_r=
\begin{cases}
C_r,&\varepsilon_p=0,\\
C_r-34C_{r-1}+C_{r-2},&\varepsilon_p=1,
\end{cases}
\tag{4.3}
\]

with missing-index terms interpreted as zero.

Therefore a zero coefficient is a quadratic additive cancellation among the coefficients of \(B_p\). Knowing the roots or irreducible degrees of \(B_p\) does not control these sums.

### 4.3 Why sparse-polynomial root bounds do not help

Finite-field lacunary-polynomial estimates usually bound the number of roots **from** the number and arithmetic arrangement of the exponents. They do not provide a useful reverse inequality bounding zero coefficients from the root count.

Here the support is already close to \(p\), so a generic bound involving \(S_p\) is nearly the trivial degree bound. Conversely, a polynomial can have a highly structured root divisor and no zero coefficients.

The smallest repository example is

\[
\begin{aligned}
H_7(t)
&=1+5t+3t^2+3t^3+3t^4+5t^5+t^6\\
&=(t-1)^2(t^2+1)^2\pmod7.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.4}
\]

All seven coefficients are nonzero, yet \(H_7\) has evaluation roots. Hence even exact squareness and an explicit root divisor do not force coefficient lacunarity.

## 5. Exact computation at \(p=19\)

Since \(19\equiv19\pmod{24}\), one has \(arepsilon_{19}=1\) and

\[
\Delta(t)=t^2+4t+1\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_{19}[t].
\]

The normalized degree-8 factor is

\[
\boxed{
B_{19}(t)
=1+10t+3t^2+5t^3+8t^4+5t^5+3t^6+10t^7+t^8.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

It factors over \(\mathbf F_{19}\) as

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
B_{19}(t)
={}&(t-7)(t-11)(t^2-5t+1)\\
&\cdot(t^2+17t+7)(t^2+16t+11).
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.2}
\]

The three displayed quadratic factors are irreducible over \(\mathbf F_{19}\). The last two form a reciprocal pair.

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
H_{19}(t)
={}&(t^2+4t+1)(t-7)^2(t-11)^2(t^2-5t+1)^2\\
&\cdot(t^2+17t+7)^2(t^2+16t+11)^2.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Expanded in the monomial basis,

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
H_{19}(t)={}&1+5t+16t^2+t^3+17t^4+10t^5+2t^6+4t^7\\
&+6t^9+4t^{11}+2t^{12}+10t^{13}+17t^{14}\\
&+t^{15}+16t^{16}+5t^{17}+t^{18}.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.4}
\]

The omitted coefficients are precisely

\[
[t^8]H_{19}=[t^{10}]H_{19}=0,
\]

so \(Z_{19}=\{8,10\}\).

The discriminant of \(t^2+4t+1\) is \(12\), a nonsquare modulo \(19\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{V(H_{19})(\mathbf F_{19})=\{7,11\},}
\tag{5.5}
\]

and both roots have multiplicity \(2\). Over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_{19}\), the smooth factor \(B_{19}\) has \(8\) distinct roots and the bad discriminant contributes two additional simple roots.

A reproducible Sage check is:

```sage
F = GF(19)
R.<t> = PolynomialRing(F)
B = 1 + 10*t + 3*t^2 + 5*t^3 + 8*t^4 + 5*t^5 + 3*t^6 + 10*t^7 + t^8
D = t^2 - 34*t + 1
H = D*B^2
print(H)
print(B.factor())
print(H.roots())
```

## 6. Exact computation at \(p=73\)

Since \(73\equiv1\pmod{24}\), one has \(arepsilon_{73}=0\), so

\[
H_{73}(t)=B_{73}(t)^2.
\]

The normalized degree-36 square root is

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
B_{73}(t)={}&1+39t+6t^2+14t^3+59t^4+33t^5+46t^6+10t^7\\
&+67t^8+69t^9+46t^{10}+61t^{11}+51t^{12}\\
&+14t^{14}+14t^{15}+27t^{16}+55t^{17}+19t^{18}\\
&+55t^{19}+27t^{20}+14t^{21}+14t^{22}\\
&+51t^{24}+61t^{25}+46t^{26}+69t^{27}+67t^{28}\\
&+10t^{29}+46t^{30}+33t^{31}+59t^{32}+14t^{33}\\
&+6t^{34}+39t^{35}+t^{36}.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{6.1}
\]

The coefficients of \(t^{13}\) and \(t^{23}\) in \(B_{73}\) are zero; they are simply omitted in (6.1). The requested truncation is explicitly

\[
\boxed{H_{73}(t)=B_{73}(t)^2\in\mathbf F_{73}[t].}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Expanding the square gives zero coefficients exactly at degrees \(2\) and \(70\), agreeing with

\[
Z_{73}=\{2,70\}.
\]

For an exact root audit, reciprocity permits the substitution \(x=t+t^{-1}\):

\[
B_{73}(t)=t^{18}Q_{73}(t+t^{-1}),
\tag{6.3}
\]

where

\[
\begin{aligned}
Q_{73}(x)={}&x^{18}+39x^{17}+61x^{16}+8x^{15}+25x^{14}+11x^{13}\\
&+28x^{12}+28x^{11}+21x^{10}+44x^9+43x^8+52x^7\\
&+42x^6+26x^5+50x^4+58x^3+38x^2+30x+52.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.4}
\]

Its roots in \(\mathbf F_{73}\) are

\[
\{1,7,18,22,26,30,36,40\}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

A value \(x\) produces an \(\mathbf F_{73}\)-root of \(B_{73}\) precisely when

\[
t^2-xt+1=0
\]

has a solution, equivalently when \(x^2-4\) is a square. Among the eight values in (6.5), this happens only for \(x=1\):

\[
1^2-4=-3=70=17^2\pmod{73}.
\]

Thus

\[
t=\frac{1\pm17}{2}=9,65.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{V(H_{73})(\mathbf F_{73})=\{9,65\},}
\tag{6.6}
\]

with both roots of multiplicity \(2\).

The exact factor-degree type is

```text
B_73: 2 linear factors and 17 irreducible quadratic factors;
H_73: the same factors, each with multiplicity 2.
```

In particular,

\[
B_{73}(t)=(t-9)(t-65)\prod_{i=1}^{17}Q_i(t),
\tag{6.7}
\]

where the \(Q_i\) are irreducible quadratics over \(\mathbf F_{73}\).

    + 55*t^17 + 19*t^18 + 55*t^19 + 27*t^20 + 14*t^21
    + 14*t^22 + 51*t^24 + 61*t^25 + 46*t^26 + 69*t^27

## 7. What is the degree of the supersingular factor?

The natural smooth factor is \(B_p\), not a hypothetical small factor \(U\). Its degree is

\[
\boxed{
\deg B_p=
\begin{cases}
(p-1)/2,&p\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{24},\\
(p-3)/2,&p\equiv13,17,19,23\pmod{24}.
\end{cases}}
\tag{7.1}
\]

So the degree is proportional to \(p\).

The ranks \(22\) and \(3\) of the full and moving \(H^2\)-pieces do not determine this degree. Those ranks describe the dimension of a cohomological fiber. The Hasse invariant is a section of a line bundle whose weight grows with \(p-1\); its divisor degree can therefore grow linearly with \(p\) even though the family and connection have fixed rank.

There is no \(p^{3/2}\) degree. The polynomial itself has degree \(p-1\), and the smooth square root has degree about \(p/2\).

## 8. Can the factorization bound \(|Z_p|\)?

### 8.1 What a successful argument would need

By (4.3), bounding \(|Z_p|\) from the factorization means bounding the number of indices \(r\) for which a quadratic convolution vanishes:

\[
\sum_{i+j=r}\beta_i\beta_j=0,
\]

or, in the defect branch,

\[
\sum_{i+j=r}\beta_i\beta_j
-34\sum_{i+j=r-1}\beta_i\beta_j
+\sum_{i+j=r-2}\beta_i\beta_j=0.
\]

Degree, squarefreeness, and irreducible-factor degrees do not control these additive cancellations.

One would need an additional theorem such as:

- additive energy/anti-concentration for the coefficient sequence \((\beta_i)\);
- a recurrence-based zero-spacing theorem for the convolution;
- a defining-characteristic Mellin large sieve;
- or a uniform bound on zero coordinates in the cover-adapted Franel basis.

None follows from (1.1) alone.

### 8.2 The Franel pullback does not remove the problem

Let

\[
K_p(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{p-1}f_mx^m,
\qquad
f_m=\sum_k\binom mk^3,
\]

and

\[
\phi(x)=\frac{x(1-8x)}{1+x},
\qquad
\Psi_{p,m}(x)=x^m(1-8x)^m(1+x)^{p-1-m}.
\]

The repository proves

\[
\boxed{
K_p(x)^2=(1+x)^{p-1}H_p(\phi(x))
=\sum_{m=0}^{p-1}b_m\Psi_{p,m}(x).
}
\tag{8.1}
\]

The \(\Psi_{p,m}\) are linearly independent. Hence

\[
b_m=[K_p^2]_{\Psi_{p,m}}.
\tag{8.2}
\]

This is an exact Apéry-specific reformulation, but the assertion that few of these coordinates vanish is precisely the coefficient-support problem again. A square in one basis need not have nonvanishing coordinates in another basis.

### 8.3 Current rigorous status

The main P3.2 draft obtains the unconditional bound

\[
|Z_p|=O(p^{2/3})
\]

from recurrence and gap-polynomial arguments. The Hasse-square factorization has not supplied a better bound.

The computations strongly suggest mean \(|Z_p|\approx1\), but they also suggest that \(|Z_p|\) is probably unbounded. Therefore an all-prime absolute bound should not be inferred from the factorization.

## 9. Direct answers to the six questions

### Question 1 — What is the factorization?

Exactly

\[
H_p(t)=\bigl(t^2-34t+1\bigr)^{\varepsilon_p}B_p(t)^2,
\qquad
\varepsilon_p=\frac{1-(-6/p)}2,
\]

with \(B_p(0)=1\) and degree given by (7.1). The polynomial \(B_p\) is an explicit truncation of one of two universal square-root series.

### Question 2 — How many roots are in \(\mathbf F_p\)?

There is no universal numerical formula. The exact answer is

\[
\deg\gcd(B_p,t^p-t)
\]

for the smooth factor, plus rational discriminant roots when \(arepsilon_p=1\). The degree of \(B_p\) is about \(p/2\), but only its linear factors produce \(\mathbf F_p\)-points.

### Question 3 — Is coefficient support related to root count?

Only through the full finite Mellin transform (4.1) or the convolution identities (4.3). There is no useful implication from the number of roots to \(p-|Z_p|\), and generic lacunary bounds are essentially vacuous here.

### Question 4 — What happens at \(p=19,73\)?

\[
V(H_{19})(\mathbf F_{19})=\{7,11\},
\qquad
V(H_{73})(\mathbf F_{73})=\{9,65\},
\]

all with multiplicity two. The exact polynomials and factorizations are in Sections 5 and 6.

### Question 5 — What is the degree of the supersingular factor?

Approximately \(p/2\), exactly (7.1). The quadratic \(\Delta\) is the bad-fiber factor, not an additional smooth supersingular divisor.

### Question 6 — Can this factorization bound \(|Z_p|\)?

Not by itself. It transforms the question into anti-concentration of quadratic convolution coefficients. That is a genuine additional arithmetic problem, and no present theorem converts the root divisor of \(B_p\) into a strong coefficient-support bound.

---

## 10. Bottom line for P3.2

The factorization is a major structural theorem:

\[
\boxed{
H_p=\Delta^{\varepsilon_p}B_p^2.
}
\]

It explains the evaluation-root geometry and shows that the smooth Hasse divisor has degree about \(p/2\). But it does not collapse the horizontal coefficient problem. The two statistics remain in dual spaces:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
B_p(t)=0
\quad\text{is an evaluation/supersingular-fiber condition},\\[3pt]
[t^r]H_p(t)=b_r=0
\quad\text{is a coefficient/Kummer–Mellin cancellation condition}.
\end{array}}
\]

For the Apéry GCD problem, the second condition is the relevant one. Existing Dwork and Hasse factorization theory organizes it beautifully, but a new defining-characteristic anti-concentration or horizontal Mellin-dispersion theorem is still required.
