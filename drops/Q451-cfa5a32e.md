# Q451 — Dwork truncation, Hasse factorization, roots, and coefficient support

## Executive verdict

Put

\[
H_p(t):=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rt^r\in\mathbf F_p[t].
\]

This is the polynomial called \(A_p\) in the current P3.2 repository. Its exact factorization is

\[
\boxed{
H_p(t)=\Delta(t)^{\epsilon_p}B_p(t)^2,
\qquad
\Delta(t)=t^2-34t+1,
\qquad
\epsilon_p=\frac{1-\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)}2.
}
\tag{E1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\epsilon_p=
\begin{cases}
0,&p\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{24},\\
1,&p\equiv13,17,19,23\pmod{24}.
\end{cases}
\tag{E2}
\]

The normalized factor satisfies \(B_p(0)=1\) and

\[
\boxed{
\deg B_p=\frac{p-1-2\epsilon_p}{2}.
}
\tag{E3}
\]

This is a strong theorem about the **evaluation-root divisor** of \(H_p\), but it does not directly bound

\[
Z_p:=\{r:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\},
\]

the set of zero **coefficients**. These are dual-space statistics:

- \(H_p(a)=0\) is an evaluation condition in the geometric parameter \(a\);
- \(b_r=[t^r]H_p(t)=0\) is a coefficient condition, equivalently a vanishing finite Mellin moment.

The main answers are:

1. **Exact factorization:** (E1), with a factor \(B_p\) of degree about \(p/2\).
2. **Roots over \(\mathbf F_p\):** the number is the number of linear factors of \(B_p\), plus any rational roots of \(\Delta\) in the defect branch. No universal numerical formula follows from (E1).
3. **Smooth supersingular factor:** after removing the bad discriminant locus, the relevant factor is \(B_p\), of degree \((p-1)/2\) or \((p-3)/2\), not \(p/12\).
4. **At \(p=19\):** \(H_{19}\) has exactly the roots \(7,11\in\mathbf F_{19}\), both double.
5. **At \(p=73\):** \(H_{73}\) has exactly the roots \(9,65\in\mathbf F_{73}\), both double.
6. **Connection to \(|Z_p|\):** the factorization turns \(b_r\) into a quadratic convolution coefficient. Bounding its zeros requires new anti-cancellation input; root degree and squarefreeness do not suffice.

Three corrections to the setup are load-bearing.

- For a K3 surface, **supersingular** means formal Brauer height \(h=\infty\), not height \(2\).
- The roots of \(\Delta\) are singular/bad-fiber parameters, not smooth supersingular K3 fibers.
- A Dwork unit-root formula is a limit of ratios of higher truncations after a gauge choice. The rank-three Frobenius matrix is not literally \(H_p(t^p)/H_p(t)\).

## 1. Exact factorization and the two square-root series

### 1.1 Factorization theorem

For every prime \(p\ge5\), there is a unique \(B_p\in\mathbf F_p[t]\), normalized by \(B_p(0)=1\), such that

\[
\boxed{H_p(t)=\bigl(t^2-34t+1\bigr)^{\epsilon_p}B_p(t)^2.}
\tag{1.1}
\]

The two branches are

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
p\bmod24&\epsilon_p& H_p(t)\\ \hline
1,5,7,11&0&B_p(t)^2\\
13,17,19,23&1&\Delta(t)B_p(t)^2.
\end{array}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Degree comparison gives

\[
\deg B_p=
\begin{cases}
(p-1)/2,&\epsilon_p=0,\\
(p-3)/2,&\epsilon_p=1.
\end{cases}
\tag{1.3}
\]

Thus the characteristic-\(p\) factor has linearly growing degree even though the Picard–Fuchs connection has fixed rank.

### 1.2 Explicit universal truncations

Define rational sequences \(c_r,d_r\) by

\[
c_{-1}=d_{-1}=0,
\qquad c_0=d_0=1,
\]

\[
4(r+1)^2c_{r+1}
=2(68r^2+34r+5)c_r-(2r-1)^2c_{r-1},
\tag{1.4}
\]

\[
4(r+1)^2d_{r+1}
=2(68r^2+102r+39)d_r-(2r+1)^2d_{r-1}.
\tag{1.5}
\]

If

\[
C_+(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}c_rt^r,
\qquad
C_-(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}d_rt^r,
\]

then in characteristic zero

\[
\sum_{r\ge0}b_rt^r=C_+(t)^2=\Delta(t)C_-(t)^2.
\tag{1.6}
\]

The finite factors are the corresponding truncations:

\[
\boxed{
B_p(t)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{(p-1)/2}\overline{c_r}t^r,
&\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=1,\\[8pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{(p-3)/2}\overline{d_r}t^r,
&\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=-1.
\end{cases}}
\tag{1.7}
\]

So \(B_p\) is explicit; it is not an unspecified factor chosen after factorization.

### 1.3 Theorem versus finite computation

The published factorization theorem proves (1.1). It does not, by itself, assert that \(B_p\) is squarefree or coprime to \(\Delta\).

The repository's exact finite audit for all \(2260\) primes \(5\le p\le20000\) found

```text
B_p is squarefree;
gcd(B_p, Delta) = 1;
B_p divides t^(p^2)-t;
every irreducible factor of B_p has degree 1 or 2.
```

Those are rigorous finite-range computations, not all-prime theorems.

## 2. The precise Dwork statement

For \(s\ge1\), define the higher truncations

\[
F_s(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{p^s-1}b_nt^n.
\tag{2.1}
\]

Then \(F_1=H_p\). In a Dwork-compatible toric normalization, congruences among the \(F_s\) make the ratios

\[
\frac{F_s(\widetilde t)}{F_{s-1}(\widetilde t^{\,p})}
\]

converge on the ordinary locus. Up to the conventional reciprocal and gauge, the unit-root function is

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal U_p(t)=
\lim_{s\to\infty}
\frac{F_s(\widetilde t)}{F_{s-1}(\widetilde t^{\,p})}.
}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Here \(\widetilde t\) is a \(p\)-adic lift of the geometric parameter. At a Teichmüller point, \(\widetilde t^p=\widetilde t\).

The formula

\[
\Phi(t)=H_p(t^p)/H_p(t)
\]

is not the full statement:

1. it omits the tower of truncations;
2. the ratio direction depends on the Frobenius convention;
3. the Apéry differential system has rank \(3\), so its Frobenius is a matrix, whereas \(H_p\) is one scalar Hasse–Witt coordinate.

The first truncation nevertheless detects the failure of ordinarity in the selected period coordinate.

## 3. Roots, Hasse divisor, and supersingular fibers

### 3.1 Geometric root divisor

Set-theoretically over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\),

\[
V(H_p)=V(B_p)\cup
\begin{cases}
\varnothing,&\epsilon_p=0,\\
V(\Delta),&\epsilon_p=1.
\end{cases}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Every root of \(B_p\) occurs with even multiplicity in \(H_p\). The discriminant roots contribute the odd factor in the defect branch.

If \(B_p\) is squarefree and coprime to \(\Delta\), then

\[
\#V(H_p)(\overline{\mathbf F}_p)
=\deg B_p+2\epsilon_p
=\frac{p-1+2\epsilon_p}{2}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The smooth factor has

\[
\boxed{
\deg B_p=\frac{p-1-2\epsilon_p}{2}\sim\frac p2.
}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Without squarefreeness, this is a degree counted with multiplicity, not the number of distinct parameters.

### 3.2 Bad discriminant roots

The roots of

\[
\Delta(t)=t^2-34t+1
\]

are singular parameters of the pencil. They must be removed before interpreting the remaining Hasse zeros as smooth supersingular fibers.

Thus the intended geometric dictionary is

```text
B_p(t)=0       smooth nonordinary/supersingular parameters;
Delta(t)=0     singular/bad parameters.
```

The algebraic factorization theorem itself is independent of this geometric labeling; the latter uses the standard Dwork/K3 identification of the truncation with the Hasse–Witt scalar.

### 3.3 Height correction

For a K3 surface:

- \(h=1\) means ordinary;
- finite \(h=2\) is nonordinary, but not supersingular;
- supersingular means \(h=\infty\), equivalently all crystalline \(H^2\)-slopes are \(1\).

For a finite-height K3 surface,

\[
\rho\le22-2h.
\tag{3.4}
\]

A smooth member of a rank-19 lattice-polarized family has \(ho\ge19\). Hence finite height forces \(h=1\). Therefore a smooth nonordinary fiber in this family is forced to be supersingular. This argument does not apply at \(\Delta=0\), where the fiber is singular.

### 3.4 Rational roots

The number of smooth roots in \(\mathbf F_p\) is exactly

\[
\boxed{
s_p:=\#\{a\in\mathbf F_p:B_p(a)=0\}
=\deg\gcd(B_p(t),t^p-t).
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

No universal formula for \(s_p\) follows from (1.1).

The discriminant of \(\Delta\) is

\[
34^2-4=1152=24^2\cdot2.
\]

Thus \(\Delta\) has

\[
1+\left(\frac2p\right)
\]

roots in \(\mathbf F_p\), namely either \(0\) or \(2\). If \(B_p\) is coprime to \(\Delta\), then

\[
\#V(H_p)(\mathbf F_p)
=s_p+\epsilon_p\left(1+\left(\frac2p\right)\right).
\tag{3.6}
\]

The analogy predicting approximately \(p/12\) rational roots is therefore misleading. The **geometric divisor degree** is approximately \(p/2\), while the number of \(\mathbf F_p\)-rational parameters depends on how that divisor splits.

## 4. Coefficient support is a Mellin-dual statistic

Let

\[
Z_p=\{r:b_r=0\pmod p\},
\qquad
S_p=p-|Z_p|.
\]

Then \(S_p\) is the monomial support size of \(H_p\). It is not the number of evaluation roots.

### 4.1 Exact finite Mellin relation

For \(1\le j\le p-2\), multiplicative orthogonality gives

\[
\boxed{
b_j=-\sum_{a\in\mathbf F_p^\times}H_p(a)a^{-j}.}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Thus \(b_j=0\) means that the \(j\)-th multiplicative Fourier coefficient of the function \(a\mapsto H_p(a)\) vanishes. This is the precise bridge between the two spaces.

### 4.2 Coefficient convolution

Write

\[
B_p(t)=\sum_m\beta_mt^m,
\qquad
C_r:=\sum_{i+j=r}\beta_i\beta_j.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
b_r=
\begin{cases}
C_r,&\epsilon_p=0,\\
C_r-34C_{r-1}+C_{r-2},&\epsilon_p=1,
\end{cases}}
\tag{4.3}
\]

with out-of-range terms equal to zero.

A zero coefficient is therefore a quadratic additive cancellation. Root degree, squarefreeness, and irreducible-factor degrees do not control how often these cancellations occur.

### 4.3 Why lacunary root bounds do not reverse

Finite-field lacunary-polynomial theorems generally bound evaluation roots from the number and arithmetic configuration of nonzero exponents. They do not yield a useful reverse bound on zero coefficients from a known root divisor.

Here \(S_p\) is already close to \(p\), so a generic support-based root bound is nearly the trivial degree bound.

The smallest repository example makes the mismatch concrete:

\[
\begin{aligned}
H_7(t)
&=1+5t+3t^2+3t^3+3t^4+5t^5+t^6\\
&=(t-1)^2(t^2+1)^2\pmod7.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.4}
\]

All seven coefficients are nonzero, although the polynomial has evaluation roots.

## 5. Exact computation at \(p=19\)

Since \(19\equiv19\pmod{24}\), one has \(\epsilon_{19}=1\) and

\[
\Delta(t)=t^2+4t+1\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_{19}[t].
\]

The normalized factor is

\[
\boxed{
B_{19}(t)=1+10t+3t^2+5t^3+8t^4+5t^5+3t^6+10t^7+t^8.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Its exact factorization is

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
B_{19}(t)
={}&(t-7)(t-11)(t^2-5t+1)\\
&\cdot(t^2+17t+7)(t^2+16t+11).
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.2}
\]

The three displayed quadratics are irreducible over \(\mathbf F_{19}\), and the last two are a reciprocal pair. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
H_{19}(t)
={}&(t^2+4t+1)(t-7)^2(t-11)^2(t^2-5t+1)^2\\
&\cdot(t^2+17t+7)^2(t^2+16t+11)^2.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Expanded in the monomial basis,

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
H_{19}(t)={}&1+5t+16t^2+t^3+17t^4+10t^5+2t^6+4t^7\\
&+6t^9+4t^{11}+2t^{12}+10t^{13}+17t^{14}\\
&+t^{15}+16t^{16}+5t^{17}+t^{18}.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.4}
\]

The missing terms are exactly

\[
[t^8]H_{19}=[t^{10}]H_{19}=0,
\]

so \(Z_{19}=\{8,10\}\).

The discriminant of \(t^2+4t+1\) is \(12\), a nonsquare modulo \(19\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{V(H_{19})(\mathbf F_{19})=\{7,11\},}
\tag{5.5}
\]

with both roots double. Over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_{19}\), \(B_{19}\) has eight distinct roots, while the bad discriminant contributes two additional simple roots.

```sage
F = GF(19)
R.<t> = PolynomialRing(F)
B = 1 + 10*t + 3*t^2 + 5*t^3 + 8*t^4 + 5*t^5 + 3*t^6 + 10*t^7 + t^8
Delta = t^2 - 34*t + 1
H = Delta*B^2
print(H)
print(B.factor())
print(H.roots())
```

## 6. Exact computation at \(p=73\)

Since \(73\equiv1\pmod{24}\), one has \(\epsilon_{73}=0\), so \(H_{73}=B_{73}^2\).

The normalized degree-36 square root is

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
B_{73}(t)={}&1+39t+6t^2+14t^3+59t^4+33t^5+46t^6+10t^7\\
&+67t^8+69t^9+46t^{10}+61t^{11}+51t^{12}\\
&+14t^{14}+14t^{15}+27t^{16}+55t^{17}+19t^{18}\\
&+55t^{19}+27t^{20}+14t^{21}+14t^{22}\\
&+51t^{24}+61t^{25}+46t^{26}+69t^{27}+67t^{28}\\
&+10t^{29}+46t^{30}+33t^{31}+59t^{32}+14t^{33}\\
&+6t^{34}+39t^{35}+t^{36}.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{6.1}
\]

The coefficients of \(t^{13}\) and \(t^{23}\) in \(B_{73}\) are zero. The requested truncation is explicitly

\[
\boxed{H_{73}(t)=B_{73}(t)^2\in\mathbf F_{73}[t].}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Its monomial coefficients vanish exactly at degrees \(2\) and \(70\), agreeing with \(Z_{73}=\{2,70\}\).

To audit the roots, use reciprocity:

\[
B_{73}(t)=t^{18}Q_{73}(t+t^{-1}),
\tag{6.3}
\]

where

\[
\begin{aligned}
Q_{73}(x)={}&x^{18}+39x^{17}+61x^{16}+8x^{15}+25x^{14}+11x^{13}\\
&+28x^{12}+28x^{11}+21x^{10}+44x^9+43x^8+52x^7\\
&+42x^6+26x^5+50x^4+58x^3+38x^2+30x+52.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.4}
\]

A direct exact evaluation gives

\[
V(Q_{73})(\mathbf F_{73})=\{1,7,18,22,26,30,36,40\}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

A value \(x\) yields an \(\mathbf F_{73}\)-root of \(B_{73}\) precisely when \(t^2-xt+1\) splits, equivalently when \(x^2-4\) is a square. Among the eight values in (6.5), this happens only for \(x=1\):

\[
1^2-4=-3=70=17^2\pmod{73}.
\]

Hence

\[
t=\frac{1\pm17}{2}=9,65.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{V(H_{73})(\mathbf F_{73})=\{9,65\},}
\tag{6.6}
\]

with both roots double.

The exact factor-degree type is

```text
B_73: 2 linear factors and 17 irreducible quadratic factors;
H_73: the same factors, each with multiplicity 2.
```

Thus

\[
B_{73}(t)=(t-9)(t-65)\prod_{i=1}^{17}Q_i(t),
\tag{6.7}
\]

where every \(Q_i\) is irreducible quadratic over \(\mathbf F_{73}\).

```sage
from collections import Counter
F = GF(73)
R.<t> = PolynomialRing(F)
B = (
    1 + 39*t + 6*t^2 + 14*t^3 + 59*t^4 + 33*t^5
    + 46*t^6 + 10*t^7 + 67*t^8 + 69*t^9 + 46*t^10
    + 61*t^11 + 51*t^12 + 14*t^14 + 14*t^15 + 27*t^16
    + 55*t^17 + 19*t^18 + 55*t^19 + 27*t^20 + 14*t^21
    + 14*t^22 + 51*t^24 + 61*t^25 + 46*t^26 + 69*t^27
    + 67*t^28 + 10*t^29 + 46*t^30 + 33*t^31 + 59*t^32
    + 14*t^33 + 6*t^34 + 39*t^35 + t^36
)
H = B^2
print(H.roots())
print(Counter(f.degree() for f, multiplicity in B.factor()))
```

The output is

```text
[(9, 2), (65, 2)]
Counter({2: 17, 1: 2})
```

## 7. Degree of the smooth supersingular factor

The natural smooth factor is \(B_p\). Its exact degree is

\[
\boxed{
\deg B_p=
\begin{cases}
(p-1)/2,&p\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{24},\\
(p-3)/2,&p\equiv13,17,19,23\pmod{24}.
\end{cases}}
\tag{7.1}
\]

So the degree is \(\Theta(p)\), specifically about \(p/2\).

The ranks \(22\) and \(3\) of the full and moving \(H^2\)-pieces describe cohomological fiber dimensions. They do not determine the degree of a Hasse section on the base. The weight/line bundle carrying that section grows with \(p-1\), allowing a divisor of degree proportional to \(p\).

There is no \(p^{3/2}\) degree: \(H_p\) has degree \(p-1\), and its smooth square root has degree about \(p/2\).

## 8. Why the factorization does not bound \(|Z_p|\)

By (4.3), a bound for \(|Z_p|\) would require control of the number of indices satisfying

\[
\sum_{i+j=r}\beta_i\beta_j=0
\]

or, in the defect branch,

\[
\sum_{i+j=r}\beta_i\beta_j
-34\sum_{i+j=r-1}\beta_i\beta_j
+\sum_{i+j=r-2}\beta_i\beta_j=0.
\]

Degree, root count, squarefreeness, and factor-degree patterns do not control these additive cancellations.

A successful argument would need extra input such as

- additive anti-concentration for the coefficient sequence \((\beta_i)\);
- a recurrence-based zero-spacing theorem for the convolution;
- a defining-characteristic Mellin large sieve;
- or a uniform zero-coordinate bound in the Franel pullback basis.

### 8.1 Franel pullback

Let

\[
K_p(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{p-1}f_mx^m,
\qquad
f_m=\sum_k\binom mk^3,
\]

\[
\phi(x)=\frac{x(1-8x)}{1+x},
\qquad
\Psi_{p,m}(x)=x^m(1-8x)^m(1+x)^{p-1-m}.
\]

The repository proves

\[
\boxed{
K_p(x)^2=(1+x)^{p-1}H_p(\phi(x))
=\sum_{m=0}^{p-1}b_m\Psi_{p,m}(x).
}
\tag{8.1}
\]

The \(\Psi_{p,m}\) are linearly independent, so

\[
b_m=[K_p^2]_{\Psi_{p,m}}.
\tag{8.2}
\]

This is an exact Apéry-specific restatement, but saying that few of these coordinates vanish is precisely the coefficient-support problem again. Squareness in one basis does not imply nonvanishing coordinates in another basis.

### 8.2 Current rigorous status

The main P3.2 draft obtains

\[
|Z_p|=O(p^{2/3})
\]

from the Apéry recurrence and gap polynomials. The factorization (1.1) has not supplied a stronger unconditional bound.

The computations support mean \(|Z_p|\approx1\), but also a Poisson model under which \(|Z_p|\) is probably unbounded. An all-prime \(O(1)\) bound should not be inferred from the Hasse square.

## 9. Direct answers to the six questions

### Question 1 — What is the factorization?

\[
H_p(t)=\bigl(t^2-34t+1\bigr)^{\epsilon_p}B_p(t)^2,
\qquad
\epsilon_p=\frac{1-(-6/p)}2,
\]

with \(B_p(0)=1\), degree (7.1), and the explicit truncation formula (1.7).

### Question 2 — How many roots are in \(\mathbf F_p\)?

The smooth count is

\[
\deg\gcd(B_p,t^p-t).
\]

Add the rational roots of \(\Delta\) in the defect branch, while remembering that those are bad fibers. The factorization gives no universal asymptotic for the number of linear factors.

### Question 3 — Is coefficient support controlled by the root count?

No useful deterministic implication is known. The exact connections are the Mellin identity (4.1) and convolution formula (4.3), both of which expose cancellation rather than support monotonicity.

### Question 4 — What are the explicit \(p=19,73\) examples?

\[
V(H_{19})(\mathbf F_{19})=\{7,11\},
\qquad
V(H_{73})(\mathbf F_{73})=\{9,65\},
\]

all roots double. Sections 5 and 6 give the exact polynomials and factorization data.

### Question 5 — What is the degree of the supersingular factor?

The smooth factor \(B_p\) has degree \((p-1)/2\) or \((p-3)/2\). The quadratic \(\Delta\) is the bad-fiber factor.

### Question 6 — Can the factorization bound \(|Z_p|\)?

Not by itself. It converts the problem into anti-concentration of quadratic convolution coefficients. That remains a genuine additional arithmetic theorem.

---

## 10. Bottom line for P3.2

The factorization

\[
\boxed{H_p=\Delta^{\epsilon_p}B_p^2}
\]

completely organizes the evaluation-root geometry and shows that the smooth Hasse divisor has degree about \(p/2\). But the P3.2 zero set remains a dual coefficient statistic:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
B_p(a)=0
\quad\text{is an evaluation/supersingular-fiber condition},\\[3pt]
[t^r]H_p(t)=b_r=0
\quad\text{is a coefficient/Kummer–Mellin cancellation condition}.
\end{array}}
\]

Existing Dwork and Hasse theory supplies the exact algebraic framework, but a new defining-characteristic anti-concentration or horizontal Mellin-dispersion theorem is still needed to bound \(|Z_p|\) sharply.
