# P2.7 versus AESZ #209: the exact relation, the scaling correction, and why none of (A)–(D) presently gives the target sequence

## Executive conclusion

There is an exact and useful relation involving AESZ #209, but it is **not** an identified pullback, symmetric square, Euler derivative, or finite cohomological projection producing the printed P2.7 denominator.

The exact known relation is

\[
F_{209}(z)
=
A_{\mathrm{in}}(z)\odot (1-4z)^{-1/2},
\tag{0.1}
\]

where

\[
A_{\mathrm{in}}(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n,
\qquad
a_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{n}\binom{n+2k}{n},
\]

\[
F_{209}(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}A_nz^n,
\qquad
A_n=\binom{2n}{n}a_n,
\]

and \(\odot\) denotes coefficientwise Hadamard product. This is precisely the construction described in the AESZ literature: Almkvist notes that eight fourth-order Calabi–Yau equations are obtained from third-order difference equations by multiplying their coefficients by \(\binom{2n}{n}\). See

- [Almkvist–van Enckevort–van Straten–Zudilin, *Tables of Calabi–Yau equations*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0507430), especially §2 and table entry 209;
- [Almkvist, *Calabi–Yau differential equations of degree 2 and 3 and Yifan Yang's pullback*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0612215), Introduction and §4;
- [Zudilin, *Approximations to -, di- and tri-logarithms*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0409023), for the inner four-term Apéry-like system.

For comparison with the **actual P2.7 recurrence in the repository**, two corrections are essential.

1. The actual P2.7 characteristic roots are
   \[
   r_j=\frac{\mu_j}{64},
   \qquad
   4\mu^3-220\mu^2+8\mu-1=0.
   \]
   The AESZ coefficients \(A_n\) have roots \(4\mu_j\). Therefore the pullback that aligns the coefficient-growth roots is
   \[
   \boxed{F_{209}(z/256),}
   \]
   not \(F_{209}(z/4)\).

2. A shift-order-three recurrence does **not** imply that its ordinary generating function satisfies a third-order differential equation. The P2.7 recurrence has polynomial coefficients of degree 12 in the displayed normalization, so its direct generating-function operator has differential order 12 before possible factorization. Thus the proposed “order 4 to order 3” rank reduction is based on conflating difference order with differential order.

With the correct scaling, there is always the tautological identity

\[
\boxed{
G(z)=F_{209}(z/256)\odot H(z),
\qquad
H(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}h_nz^n,
\qquad
h_n=\frac{256^nq_n}{A_n}.
}
\tag{0.2}
\]

But no simple D-finite, hypergeometric, or classical modular description of \(H\) is presently known. Equation (0.2) is a definition of the Hadamard quotient, not an explanation of P2.7.

The best current answer to the four proposed mechanisms is therefore:

| Proposal | Verdict |
|---|---|
| (A) Hadamard product | Exact only in the tautological form (0.2); no useful closed form for \(H\) is known |
| (B) Euler derivative/integral | The simple forms fail; a finite differential transform would amount to an Ore-module homomorphism and has not been found |
| (C) symmetric-square root | Impossible by an exact characteristic-root test |
| (D) fiber restriction/projection | No geometric correspondence or rank-three quotient is known; a base pullback alone cannot reduce rank |

The spectral level-11 identity does have an exact interpretation, but it is a **centered binomial trace transform**, not a symmetric square. It explains the cubic at infinity, not the full recurrence or its initial connection data.

# 1. The exact AESZ #209 Hadamard construction

Let

\[
B(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{1-4z}}
=
\sum_{n\ge0}\binom{2n}{n}z^n.
\]

Then coefficientwise multiplication gives

\[
F_{209}(z)=A_{\mathrm{in}}(z)\odot B(z).
\]

Equivalently, using

\[
\frac1\pi\int_0^1\frac{t^n}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}\,dt
=\frac1{4^n}\binom{2n}{n},
\]

one has the exact Euler integral

\[
\boxed{
F_{209}(z)
=
\frac1\pi\int_0^1
\frac{A_{\mathrm{in}}(4zt)}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}\,dt.
}
\tag{1.1}
\]

This is the genuine order-raising Hadamard operation behind the AESZ entry.

It can also be inverted coefficientwise. Since

\[
\frac1{\binom{2n}{n}}
=
\frac{(1)_n}{4^n(\frac12)_n},
\]

define

\[
D(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{\binom{2n}{n}}
={}_2F_1\!\left(\begin{matrix}1,1\\ \frac12\end{matrix};\frac z4\right).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{A_{\mathrm{in}}(z)=F_{209}(z)\odot D(z).}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Thus, if a transform from the AESZ period to P2.7 exists, the cleanest decomposition is

\[
F_{209}
\xrightarrow{\text{Hadamard deconvolution}}
A_{\mathrm{in}}
\xrightarrow{z\mapsto z/64}
A_{\mathrm{in}}(z/64)
\xrightarrow{\text{additional nonlocal transform}}
G.
\]

The first two arrows are explicit. The final arrow is the unresolved one.

# 2. The root-scaling correction: the factor is 256, not 4

Let \(\mu_0,\mu_+,\mu_-\) be the roots of

\[
P_{27}(\mu)=4\mu^3-220\mu^2+8\mu-1.
\]

Zudilin's inner sequence has exponential modes \(\mu_j^n\). The central binomial coefficient satisfies

\[
\binom{2n}{n}\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}},
\]

so the AESZ sequence has modes

\[
A_n\sim C_j(4\mu_j)^n\times\text{a power of }n.
\]

By contrast, the P2.7 recurrence in `problems/2.7/proof.tex` has actual roots

\[
r_j=\frac{\mu_j}{64}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{4\mu_j}{256}=\frac{\mu_j}{64}=r_j,
\]

and hence

\[
F_{209}(z/256)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{A_n}{256^n}z^n
\]

has the same three exponential coefficient scales as \(G(z)=\sum q_nz^n\).

The quotient used in the question was

\[
h_n^{(4)}=\frac{4^nq_n}{A_n}.
\]

The correctly root-balanced quotient is

\[
\widehat h_n=\frac{256^nq_n}{A_n}=64^nh_n^{(4)}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

This explains the observed decay. If the currently verified P2.7 formal powers are \(0\), while the AESZ sequence has dominant power \(-2\) (inner Zudilin power \(-3/2\), plus \(-1/2\) from the central binomial factor), then

\[
\boxed{
\widehat h_n\sim \kappa n^2,
\qquad
h_n^{(4)}\sim \kappa\frac{n^2}{64^n}.
}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\frac{h_{n+1}^{(4)}}{h_n^{(4)}}
=\frac1{64}\left(1+\frac2n+O(n^{-2})\right),
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

not \(1/4\). The supplied numerical values are consistent with convergence toward \(1/64=0.015625\).

The corrected normalized values begin approximately

\[
\frac{\widehat h_n}{\widehat h_0}
=
1,\ 15.7178504,\ 49.4904339,\ 102.52745,\ 174.83976,\ 266.43028,\ldots
\]

and therefore look asymptotically quadratic. They are not exactly a quadratic: the quadratic through the first three displayed values predicts about \(102.31775\) at \(n=3\), rather than \(102.52745\).

This already rules out the most naive identity

\[
q_n=P_2(n)\frac{A_n}{256^n},
\]

or equivalently

\[
G(z)=P_2(\theta)F_{209}(z/256)
\]

with a degree-two polynomial \(P_2\).

# 3. Proposal (A): Hadamard product

## 3.1 The exact but tautological answer

Define \(h_n\) by (0.2). Then

\[
G=F_{209}(z/256)\odot H
\]

holds identically. Every sequence admits such a termwise quotient when the denominator coefficients are nonzero.

The substantive question is whether \(H\) belongs to a recognizable class:

- rational;
- algebraic;
- hypergeometric;
- D-finite of low order;
- modular;
- or a period of a known rank-one system.

There is currently no evidence in the project that it does.

D-finite series are closed under Hadamard product, but they are **not** closed under Hadamard quotient. Thus D-finiteness of \(F_{209}\) and \(G\) does not imply D-finiteness of \(H\).

## 3.2 Why a simple hypergeometric quotient is unlikely

If \(h_n\) were hypergeometric, then

\[
\frac{h_{n+1}}{h_n}\in\mathbb Q(n).
\]

The exact sequence can be tested for such a rational ratio. Likewise, if \(H\) were a low-order D-finite function, \(h_n\) would satisfy a low-order polynomial recurrence.

The code in §8 performs both tests over exact rationals, with an independent holdout interval. A guessed relation should not be accepted unless it is then proved symbolically.

There is also an asymptotic warning. If both \(q_n\) and \(A_n/256^n\) have nonzero subdominant connection coefficients, then expanding their quotient produces successively

\[
\left(\frac{r_+}{r_0}\right)^n,
\quad
\left(\frac{r_-}{r_0}\right)^n,
\quad
\left(\frac{r_+}{r_0}\right)^{2n},
\quad
\left(\frac{r_+r_-}{r_0^2}\right)^n,
\quad\ldots
\]

in its transseries. Generically this gives infinitely many exponential scales, which is incompatible with a fixed finite-order P-recursive sequence. Turning this heuristic into a proof would require exact nonvanishing of the relevant connection coefficients.

So (A) is an exact *definition*, but not presently an exact constructive explanation.

# 4. Proposal (B): Euler derivatives or Euler integral transforms

Let

\[
F_*(z)=F_{209}(z/256)=\sum_{n\ge0}f_nz^n,
\qquad f_n=\frac{A_n}{256^n}.
\]

For integers \(a,b,c\),

\[
z^{-a}\frac{d^b}{dz^b}\left(z^cF_*(z)\right)
\]

has coefficients of the form

\[
(n+c)^{\underline b}\,f_n
\]

up to a fixed index shift. More generally, a finite rational differential operator

\[
T=\sum_{i=0}^r t_i(z)\theta^i,
\qquad \theta=z\frac d{dz},
\]

produces a finite-band polynomial/rational combination of shifted \(f_n\)'s.

This is exactly a local differential-module homomorphism. In Ore form, if \(T F_*=G\), then

\[
L_G T=U L_{209}^{(256)}
\tag{4.1}
\]

for some operator \(U\), after accounting for the polynomial boundary term at \(z=0\).

Thus proposal (B) is not a different mechanism from the Ore-intertwiner search; it is the generating-function version of the same finite local transformation.

## 4.1 The generating-function order correction

The P2.7 recurrence is

\[
A(n)q_{n+1}-B(n)q_n+C(n-1)q_{n-1}-D(n-2)q_{n-2}=0.
\]

Multiplying by \(z^{n+1}\) and summing gives, up to an explicit polynomial determined by \(q_0,q_1,q_2\),

\[
\boxed{
\left[
A(\theta-1)
-zB(\theta)
+z^2C(\theta)
-z^3D(\theta)
\right]G(z)
=R_{\mathrm{init}}(z).
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Since \(A,B,C,D\) have degree 12, the displayed differential operator has order 12. A shift-order-three recurrence has a three-dimensional **sequence** solution space, but this does not mean its OGF equation has differential order three.

Therefore the proposed “fourth-order AESZ equation reduces to a third-order target equation” is not presently a valid rank comparison. One must first compute and factor the actual homogeneous annihilator of \(G\).

If its minimal differential rank is greater than four, no finite differential operator applied to one AESZ period can produce it. If it has a rank-four factor, then (4.1) is the exact test.

## 4.2 Euler beta transforms

An Euler transform

\[
\mathcal E_{\alpha,\beta}F(z)
=
\frac1{B(\alpha,\beta-\alpha)}
\int_0^1t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-\alpha-1}F(zt)\,dt
\]

multiplies coefficients by

\[
\frac{(\alpha)_n}{(\beta)_n}.
\]

This is a scalar hypergeometric gauge. It changes every formal power by the same number \(\alpha-\beta\). It is testable by checking whether the coefficient ratio \(q_n/f_n\) has rational successive quotient. The numerical data do not suggest such a simple gauge.

No member of the elementary Euler family has been identified with P2.7.

# 5. Proposal (C): symmetric square

This proposal is ruled out by the characteristic roots alone.

If a third-order operator is a symmetric square of a second-order operator with roots \(u,v\), then its three characteristic roots are

\[
u^2,\quad uv,\quad v^2.
\]

Consequently one of the three roots, namely \(uv\), satisfies

\[
(uv)^3=(u^2)(uv)(v^2),
\]

so **one root cubed must equal the product of all three roots**. This property is invariant under a common scalar gauge.

For

\[
P_{27}(x)=4x^3-220x^2+8x-1,
\]

the product of the roots is \(1/4\). If a root \(x\) satisfied \(x^3=1/4\), then \(4x^3=1\), and substituting into \(P_{27}(x)=0\) would give

\[
-220x^2+8x=4x(2-55x)=0.
\]

Since \(x\ne0\), this forces \(x=2/55\), but

\[
4(2/55)^3\ne1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{P_{27}\text{ is not a symmetric-square spectrum, even after scalar gauge.}}
\tag{5.1}
\]

## 5.1 What the level-11 identity actually means

The identity

\[
16P_{27}\!\left(\frac{(t-2)^2}{4}\right)
=-C_{11}(t)C_{11}(4-t)
\]

is naturally explained by the centered even binomial transform

\[
\boxed{
(\mathcal KT)_n
=\frac1{4^n}\sum_{j=0}^{2n}
\binom{2n}{j}(-2)^{2n-j}T_j.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

For a pure exponential \(T_j=t^j\),

\[
(\mathcal KT)_n
=\frac{(t-2)^{2n}}{4^n}
=\left(\frac{(t-2)^2}{4}\right)^n.
\]

Thus (5.2) implements exactly the spectral map in the polynomial identity. It is a **quadratic trace/norm transform under \(t\leftrightarrow4-t\)**, not a symmetric square.

Applying (5.2) to Cooper's level-11 sequence is a legitimate nonlocal candidate, but it is not the P2.7 denominator as printed. With \(T_0=1,T_1=4,T_2=28\), one gets

\[
(\mathcal KT)_0=1,
\qquad
(\mathcal KT)_1=4,
\]

whereas

\[
\frac{q_1}{q_0}=0.859569944180336\ldots.
\]

So the spectral identity does not supply the missing connection normalization or accessory parameters.

For the level-11 four-term recurrence, see [Cooper, *Apéry-like sequences defined by four-term recurrence relations*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00757).

# 6. Proposal (D): fiber restriction or cohomological projection

A base pullback

\[
z\longmapsto z/256
\]

moves singularities but does not reduce the rank of a local system. A fourth-order Picard–Fuchs equation remains fourth order after an ordinary pullback.

A genuine rank-three quotient of a rank-four Calabi–Yau variation would require an invariant line or an invariant rank-three subspace, or an explicit algebraic correspondence inducing a quotient. Nothing of this kind has been exhibited for AESZ #209 and P2.7.

Moreover, the statement that the target generating function is rank three has not been established merely from the shift order of the recurrence; equation (4.2) shows why.

Restricting a Calabi–Yau threefold to a divisor changes the cohomological degree and generally produces a different variation. It does not canonically project \(H^3\) to a rank-three subsystem. To make proposal (D) mathematical one would need:

1. an explicit family \(Y_z\) realizing AESZ #209;
2. an explicit family of divisors or algebraic correspondences;
3. a map on Gauss–Manin systems;
4. an exact Picard–Fuchs computation showing that the image is the P2.7 operator;
5. matching of the initial period vector.

No such construction is presently known in the project or in the cited AESZ tables.

Yifan Yang's pullback is also not the required operation. It relates a special fifth-order exterior-square equation to a fourth-order Calabi–Yau equation; it is not an order-four to order-three reduction. Almkvist explicitly discusses this fifth-to-fourth-order construction in [arXiv:math/0612215](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0612215).

# 7. The most plausible corrected transform

With the currently verified formal powers \(\sigma_{27}=0\), the inner Zudilin sequence and the target can be brought into the same local asymptotic class by a simple **Cauchy product**, not a Hadamard product.

Let

\[
A_{64}(z)=A_{\mathrm{in}}(z/64).
\]

Its coefficient modes have roots \(r_j=\mu_j/64\) and power \(-3/2\). Define

\[
\chi(z)=\prod_{j=0,+,-}(1-r_jz)
=1-\frac{55}{64}z+\frac1{2048}z^2-\frac1{1048576}z^3.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\widetilde A(z)=\chi(z)^{-3/2}A_{64}(z)
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

has the same singular locations and shifts the local coefficient power from \(-3/2\) to \(0\) at each of the three cubic singularities.

In sequence form, (7.2) is the genuinely nonlocal Cauchy convolution

\[
\widetilde a_n
=\sum_{m=0}^n c_{n-m}\frac{a_m}{64^m},
\qquad
\chi(z)^{-3/2}=\sum_{j\ge0}c_jz^j.
\tag{7.3}
\]

This is the minimal asymptotically correct transform after de-Hadamarding the central binomial factor.

It is **not yet the target**. Indeed,

\[
A_{64}(z)=1+\frac7{64}z+\cdots,
\qquad
\chi(z)^{-3/2}=1+\frac{165}{128}z+\cdots,
\]

so

\[
\widetilde A(z)=1+\frac{179}{128}z+\cdots,
\]

whereas the normalized P2.7 denominator has

\[
\frac{G(z)}{q_0}=1+0.859569944180336\ldots z+\cdots.
\]

Thus even the uniquely natural exponent-correcting twist does not fix the global connection data.

The precise remaining finite question is whether there exists a rational differential post-operator

\[
T(z,\theta)=\sum_{i=0}^r t_i(z)\theta^i
\]

such that

\[
\boxed{G(z)=T(z,\theta)\widetilde A(z).}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Equation (7.4), if true, can be verified by exact Ore division. No such operator is presently known.

# 8. Exact Sage code for the Hadamard quotient and local-transform tests

The following script computes \(A_n,q_n\), forms both the incorrectly scaled and correctly scaled Hadamard quotients, tests for a rational step ratio, and searches for finite polynomial shift or differential post-operators. Every check is exact over \(\mathbb Q\), with holdout terms.

```python
from sage.all import *

QQn.<n> = PolynomialRing(QQ)


def aesz209(N):
    out = []
    for m in range(N):
        inner = sum(
            binomial(m, k)^2
            * binomial(m + k, m)
            * binomial(m + 2*k, m)
            for k in range(m + 1)
        )
        out.append(QQ(binomial(2*m, m) * inner))
    return out


def A(m):
    return 1024*(2*m+5)^4*(2*m+7)^3*(2*m+9)^3 \
        *(946*m^2+6407*m+10860)


def B(m):
    return 128*(2*m+7)^3*(2*m+9)^3 * (
        104060*m^6+1745370*m^5+12145238*m^4
        +44886481*m^3+92943995*m^2
        +102256019*m+46709052
    )


def C(m):
    return 16*(m+3)^4*(2*m+9)^3 * (
        3784*m^5+57792*m^4+351019*m^3
        +1059230*m^2+1587211*m+944620
    )


def D(m):
    return (m+3)^4*(m+4)^6*(946*m^2+4515*m+5399)


def p27_q(N):
    q = [
        QQ(-215040420000),
        QQ(-167282265043404)/905,
        QQ(-964185327658080)/6071,
    ]
    for m in range(2, N-1):
        q.append(
            QQ(B(m))/A(m)*q[m]
            - QQ(C(m-1))/A(m-1)*q[m-1]
            + QQ(D(m-2))/A(m-2)*q[m-2]
        )
    return q[:N]


N = 80
AA = aesz209(N)
q = p27_q(N)

# Quotient used in the question and root-balanced quotient.
h4 = [q[m]*4^m/AA[m] for m in range(N)]
h256 = [q[m]*256^m/AA[m] for m in range(N)]

print("n, q_n*4^n/A_n, successive ratio")
for m in range(16):
    rr = None if m == 0 else h4[m]/h4[m-1]
    print(m, h4[m], rr)

print("normalized root-balanced quotient")
for m in range(16):
    print(m, h256[m]/h256[0])


# Test h_{n+1}/h_n = P(n)/Q(n) for bounded degrees.
def rational_ratio_fit(seq, maxdeg=14, holdout=15):
    ratios = [seq[m+1]/seq[m] for m in range(len(seq)-1)]
    x = polygen(QQ, 'x')
    for dp in range(maxdeg+1):
        for dq in range(maxdeg+1):
            # Unknown coefficients of P and Q; homogeneous equations
            rows = []
            train = dp+dq+6
            if train + holdout >= len(ratios):
                continue
            for m in range(train):
                r = ratios[m]
                rows.append(
                    [QQ(m)^j for j in range(dp+1)]
                    + [-r*QQ(m)^j for j in range(dq+1)]
                )
            K = matrix(QQ, rows).right_kernel()
            for v in K.basis():
                P = sum(v[j]*x^j for j in range(dp+1))
                Q = sum(v[dp+1+j]*x^j for j in range(dq+1))
                if Q == 0:
                    continue
                if all(P(m) == ratios[m]*Q(m)
                       for m in range(len(ratios))):
                    return P.factor(), Q.factor()
    return None

print("hypergeometric ratio for h256:",
      rational_ratio_fit(h256))


# Search q_n = sum_{j=0}^r p_j(n) f_{n+j}, deg p_j <= d,
# where f_n=A_n/256^n.
f = [AA[m]/256^m for m in range(N)]

def search_shift_post(max_order=5, max_degree=18, holdout=15):
    for r in range(max_order+1):
        for d in range(max_degree+1):
            unknowns = (r+1)*(d+1)
            available = N-r
            train = unknowns+5
            if train+holdout > available:
                continue
            rows = []
            rhs = []
            for m in range(train):
                rows.append([
                    QQ(m)^k*f[m+j]
                    for j in range(r+1)
                    for k in range(d+1)
                ])
                rhs.append(q[m])
            M = matrix(QQ, rows)
            y = vector(QQ, rhs)
            if M.rank() != M.augment(y).rank():
                continue
            sol = M.solve_right(y)
            ok = True
            for m in range(available):
                val = sum(
                    sol[j*(d+1)+k]*QQ(m)^k*f[m+j]
                    for j in range(r+1)
                    for k in range(d+1)
                )
                if val != q[m]:
                    ok = False
                    break
            if ok:
                return r, d, sol
    return None

print("finite shift post-operator:", search_shift_post())
```

A positive guess from this code is only the start. It must be converted into an exact Ore identity. A negative answer within these bounds rules out only that finite ansatz, not every nonlocal transform.

# 9. Final answer

The current evidence does not support an exact identity of types (A)–(D).

What is exact is:

\[
F_{209}=A_{\mathrm{in}}\odot(1-4z)^{-1/2},
\]

\[
A_{\mathrm{in}}=F_{209}\odot
{}_2F_1\!\left(1,1;\tfrac12;\tfrac z4\right),
\]

and the correct singularity-aligning scale is

\[
z\mapsto z/256.
\]

The level-11 polynomial identity is explained exactly by the centered binomial transform (5.2), not by a symmetric square. The simplest exponent-correcting nonlocal operation is the Cauchy twist (7.2), but it fails the first target initial coefficient and therefore is not the complete transform.

Accordingly, the strongest honest statement is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{P2.7 shares AESZ/level-11 spectral data, but no exact AESZ-to-P2.7 period transform is currently known.}
}
\]

The next checkable target is (7.4): de-Hadamard the central binomial factor, scale by \(64\), apply \(\chi^{-3/2}\), and perform an exact rational differential-Hom search. If that Hom space is zero, the remaining connection must be genuinely integral/matrix-valued rather than a scalar pullback or Hadamard factor.
