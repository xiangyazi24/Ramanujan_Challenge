# P3.2 two-coordinate divisor geometry: exact optimization and no-go

## Executive verdict

The two coordinates

\[
q=2j+c,
\qquad j=\min(r,q-1-r),
\qquad c=q-2j\ \text{odd},
\]

produce two genuine global boundary carriers. They dispose of every target with small `j` or small `c` at logarithmic cost proportional to the number of coordinate values used. They do **not** iterate to an `o(n)` cover of the full horizontal target set.

The obstruction is already complete in the top-half block `q>n/2`, where the quotient `a=\lfloor n/q\rfloor` equals one. The two branches become the affine lines

\[
3j+c=n \qquad\text{(direct)},
\tag{E1}
\]

and

\[
3j+2c=n+1 \qquad\text{(reflected)}.
\tag{E2}
\]

If targets with `j\le J` and `c\le C` are removed, the remaining **ambient prime candidates** occupy the exact intervals

\[
\frac{2n+C}{3}<q<n-J
\tag{E3}
\]

on the direct branch and

\[
\frac{n+J+1}{2}<q<\frac{2n+2-C}{3}
\tag{E4}
\]

on the reflected branch. Consequently, for `J,C=o(n)`, their total Chebyshev weight is

\[
\boxed{
\frac n2-\frac{3J}{2}-\frac{2C}{3}+o(n).
}
\tag{E5}
\]

In particular, the intended choices `J,C=o(n/\log n)` leave candidate weight `n/2+o(n)` in the top half alone. To make both intervals empty one must have

\[
3J+C\ge n+O(1),
\tag{E6}
\]

so at least one boundary coordinate range is linear. The corresponding product carrier then has logarithmic height at least linear, and its elementary displayed bound is actually `\Omega(n\log n)`.

This yields the sharp deterministic optimization. From the stated linear carriers one obtains at best

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{\substack{n/2<q\le n\\q\text{ target}}}\log q
\le \frac n2+o(n),
}
\tag{E7}
\]

which is just the prime-number-theorem bound for the whole top-half prime interval. For all `q>\sqrt n`, the corresponding best geometry-only bound is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{\substack{\sqrt n<q\le n\\q\text{ target}}}\log q
\le n+o(n).
}
\tag{E8}
\]

No choice of thresholds, dyadic decomposition, affine reparameterization, divisor product, factorial ratio, cyclotomic factorization, or bounded-order resultant improves the order of magnitude. On a fixed central strip the target-prime valuation matrix of the coordinate atoms is diagonal: one coordinate atom carries one prime, and all cross-differences are units. Grouping atoms into a determinant or a cyclotomic product changes the presentation, not this local Smith ledger.

This is a no-go for the **linear-carrier geometry**, not a disproof of P3.2. The condition `q\mid A_j` is the only selective input. To obtain `o(n)`, one must prove a new Apéry-specific horizontal compression theorem. One precise sufficient input is given in Section 10: a uniform block carrier of logarithmic height `o(H)` for target primes in every branch-linear interval of length `H=n^{1/3}`. Multiplying those block carriers would cost `o(n)`; the quotient and branch boundaries cost only `O(n^{5/6})`. A bounded zero fiber `|Z_q|=O(1)` does not imply such a block theorem.

## 1. Exact global coordinate identities

Let `q` be an odd prime with `\sqrt n<q\le n`. Write

\[
n=aq+r,
\qquad 0\le r<q,
\qquad a=\left\lfloor\frac nq\right\rfloor<\sqrt n.
\tag{1.1}
\]

Put

\[
j=\min(r,q-1-r),
\qquad c=q-2j.
\tag{1.2}
\]

Then `c` is a positive odd integer and `q=2j+c`.

### Direct branch

If `r=j`, then

\[
n=aq+j.
\tag{1.3}
\]

Hence

\[
q\mid n-j
\tag{1.4}
\]

and

\[
2n+c=2aq+2j+c=(2a+1)q.
\tag{1.5}
\]

Thus

\[
q\mid 2n+c.
\tag{1.6}
\]

Eliminating `q=2j+c` gives the quotient-line equation

\[
\boxed{(2a+1)j+ac=n.}
\tag{1.7}
\]

### Reflected branch

If `r=q-1-j`, then

\[
n=aq+q-1-j=(a+1)q-j-1.
\tag{1.8}
\]

Hence

\[
q\mid n+j+1
\tag{1.9}
\]

and

\[
2n+2-c=2(a+1)q-2j-c=(2a+1)q.
\tag{1.10}
\]

Thus

\[
q\mid 2n+2-c.
\tag{1.11}
\]

Eliminating `q` gives

\[
\boxed{(2a+1)j+(a+1)c=n+1.}
\tag{1.12}
\]

Equations (1.7) and (1.12) are the complete two-coordinate divisor geometry. For every fixed quotient `a`, the candidates lie on two affine lines. The top-half block is `a=1`, so no descent in the quotient remains there.

## 2. The two exact boundary products

Define

\[
P_j(n)=(n-j)(n+j+1)
\tag{2.1}
\]

and

\[
Q_c(n)=(2n+c)(2n+2-c).
\tag{2.2}
\]

Every target prime satisfies

\[
q\mid P_j(n),
\qquad
q\mid Q_c(n).
\tag{2.3}
\]

Indeed, one factor in each product is selected by the branch identities above.

For thresholds `J,C\ge0`, put

\[
\mathcal B_{J,C}(n)=
\prod_{0\le j\le J}P_j(n)
\prod_{\substack{1\le c\le C\\c\text{ odd}}}Q_c(n).
\tag{2.4}
\]

Then

\[
\prod_{\substack{q\text{ target}\\j(q)\le J\ \text{or}\ c(q)\le C}}q
\mid \mathcal B_{J,C}(n).
\tag{2.5}
\]

Since `0\le j<n/2` and `1\le c\le n`, every factor in (2.4) is `O(n^2)`. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\log|\mathcal B_{J,C}(n)|
\ll (J+C+1)\log n.
}
\tag{2.6}
\]

This proves the two boundary claims globally, without quotient-shell summation.

There is also a bounded-multiplicity interpretation. A fixed `j` contributes factors of size at most `n` and `3n/2+1`; a fixed `c` contributes factors of size at most `3n` and `2n+1`. Each such factor has only `O(1)` prime divisors exceeding `\sqrt n`. Thus each individual coordinate value can cover only `O(1)` large-prime candidates. This remains true globally over all quotients.

## 3. Exact top-half geometry

Now restrict to

\[
\frac n2<q\le n.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Then `a=1`.

### Direct part

Here `r=n-q`. The direct inequality `r\le(q-1)/2` is equivalent, up to the single central integer boundary, to

\[
q\ge\frac{2n+1}{3}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The coordinates are

\[
j=n-q,
\qquad
c=q-2j=3q-2n,
\tag{3.3}
\]

and satisfy

\[
3j+c=n.
\tag{3.4}
\]

The conditions `j>J` and `c>C` become

\[
q<n-J
\tag{3.5}
\]

and

\[
q>\frac{2n+C}{3}.
\tag{3.6}
\]

Hence the direct interior is exactly (up to endpoints)

\[
I_D(J,C)=
\left(\frac{2n+C}{3},\ n-J\right).
\tag{3.7}
\]

### Reflected part

For `n/2<q<(2n+1)/3`, one has

\[
j=q-1-r=2q-n-1,
\tag{3.8}
\]

\[
c=q-2j=2n+2-3q,
\tag{3.9}
\]

and

\[
3j+2c=n+1.
\tag{3.10}
\]

The inequalities `j>J`, `c>C` become

\[
q>\frac{n+J+1}{2}
\tag{3.11}
\]

and

\[
q<\frac{2n+2-C}{3}.
\tag{3.12}
\]

Thus the reflected interior is

\[
I_R(J,C)=
\left(\frac{n+J+1}{2},\ \frac{2n+2-C}{3}\right).
\tag{3.13}
\]

The possible central prime `q=(2n+1)/3` has `c=1`; it belongs to the already harmless small-`c` boundary and costs `O(\log n)`.

## 4. Sharp candidate-weight calculation

Let

\[
\vartheta(x)=\sum_{q\le x\atop q\text{ prime}}\log q.
\]

The prime number theorem gives `\vartheta(x)=x+o(x)`. If `J,C=o(n)`, the ambient prime weight in the two interior intervals is

\[
\begin{aligned}
W_{\rm amb}(J,C;n)
={}&\vartheta(n-J)-\vartheta((2n+C)/3)\\
&+\vartheta((2n+2-C)/3)-\vartheta((n+J+1)/2)\\
={}&\frac n2-\frac{3J}{2}-\frac{2C}{3}+o(n).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
\]

This is an ambient statement: it does not assert that these primes are Apéry targets. Its role is to measure exactly how much the divisor geometry has failed to exclude.

For the intended thresholds

\[
J,C=o(n/\log n),
\tag{4.2}
\]

one obtains

\[
W_{\rm amb}(J,C;n)=\frac n2+o(n).
\tag{4.3}
\]

Thus both coordinates may be large on a positive-width set of top-half primes. There is no geometric contradiction.

### When do the two boundary regions cover the whole top half?

The direct interval is empty only if

\[
n-J\le\frac{2n+C}{3},
\]

that is,

\[
3J+C\ge n.
\tag{4.4}
\]

The reflected interval is empty only if

\[
\frac{n+J+1}{2}\ge\frac{2n+2-C}{3},
\]

that is,

\[
3J+2C\ge n+1.
\tag{4.5}
\]

For nonnegative `C`, (4.4) asymptotically dominates (4.5). Therefore any threshold cover of the whole top half satisfies

\[
\boxed{3J+C\ge n+O(1).}
\tag{4.6}
\]

Minimizing either `J+C` or the more accurate coordinate count `J+C/2` gives

\[
J=\frac n3+O(1),
\qquad C=O(1).
\tag{4.7}
\]

Thus a complete boundary cover necessarily uses `\Omega(n)` coordinate values.

## 5. The optimized deterministic bound

Split the actual top-half targets into the boundary part and the interior part. Equations (2.6), (3.7), and (3.13) give

\[
\begin{aligned}
W_{1}(n)
:={}&\sum_{n/2<q\le n\atop q\text{ target}}\log q\\
\le{}&K(J+C+1)\log n\\
&+\max\left(0,\frac n3-J-\frac C3\right)\\
&+\max\left(0,\frac n6-\frac J2-\frac C3\right)
+o(n)
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.1}
\]

for an absolute `K`.

For large `n`, increasing either threshold by one saves only `O(1)` ambient Chebyshev weight but costs `\asymp\log n` in the guaranteed product bound. Hence the right side is optimized at bounded `J,C`, and

\[
\boxed{W_1(n)\le n/2+o(n).}
\tag{5.2}
\]

This is exactly the trivial estimate

\[
W_1(n)\le\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(n/2)=n/2+o(n).
\tag{5.3}
\]

For the full large-prime range,

\[
W_{>\sqrt n}(n)
\le\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(\sqrt n)
=n+o(n).
\tag{5.4}
\]

The current smooth-radical reduction shows that replacing the moving target condition by the single integer `A_n` changes this weight by only `O(n^{2/3})`; it does not alter the linear barrier.

## 6. Why affine iteration and reparameterization stop

For every quotient `a`, equations (1.7) and (1.12) are two affine lines:

\[
(2a+1)j+ac=n,
\tag{6.1}
\]

\[
(2a+1)j+(a+1)c=n+1.
\tag{6.2}
\]

One can apply Euclidean changes of coordinates, solve for `j`, solve for `c`, or introduce the odd quotient `2a+1`. This does not reduce the `a=1` block. On that block the quotient is already constant, and the unresolved candidates occupy the two long line segments (E1)--(E2).

More generally, on a line segment of length `\asymp n`, every nonconstant affine integer coordinate has bounded-size fibers. Selecting `t` coordinate values therefore covers only `O(t)` candidates. If an affine coordinate is constant on the line, a carrier depending only on that constant would have to be divisible by every prime in a positive-width interval. Any such nonzero integer has logarithmic height `\Omega(n)`. Thus affine rotation merely exchanges:

```text
many coordinate values of logarithmic height O(log n) each
```

for

```text
one constant-coordinate carrier of logarithmic height Omega(n).
```

Neither yields `o(n)`.

The same statement holds for a fixed-degree polynomial reparameterization: a coordinate value has at most `O(d)` preimages unless the polynomial is constant on the branch. Fixed degree changes only constants. Allowing degree to grow introduces a determinant/resultant of proportional degree, and the target-prime product lower bound reappears in its height.

## 7. Exact diagonal saturation on a central strip

The multiscale obstruction can be made local and exact. Consider the direct central strip

\[
\mathcal Q_n=\left\{q\text{ prime}:\frac{3n}{4}<q\le\frac{4n}{5}\right\}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Every such prime is direct, with

\[
j(q)=n-q\in[n/5,n/4),
\tag{7.2}
\]

and

\[
c(q)=3q-2n\in(n/4,2n/5].
\tag{7.3}
\]

At the matching coordinate atoms,

\[
n-j(q)=q,
\tag{7.4}
\]

and

\[
2n+c(q)=3q.
\tag{7.5}
\]

For distinct `q,q'\in\mathcal Q_n`,

\[
n-j(q')=q',
\qquad
2n+c(q')=3q',
\tag{7.6}
\]

so `q` divides neither atom belonging to `q'`. Moreover

\[
|j(q)-j(q')|=|q-q'|<q,
\tag{7.7}
\]

and

\[
|c(q)-c(q')|=3|q-q'|<q
\tag{7.8}
\]

because the strip width is `n/20` and `q>3n/4`. Hence every nonzero pairwise difference and every Vandermonde factor is a `q`-adic unit.

This gives an exact diagonal valuation matrix:

\[
q\longleftrightarrow j(q)\longleftrightarrow c(q).
\tag{7.9}
\]

A product of coordinate atoms contains `q` if and only if it uses the atom matched to `q` (up to the harmless factor `3`). A signed product cancels `q` exactly when it cancels the exponent of that matched atom. A determinant or resultant built from the atoms and their cross-differences cannot create an additional `q`, because all cross-differences are units.

Therefore any nonzero integer produced solely from these linear atoms and forced to contain every prime in `\mathcal Q_n` satisfies

\[
\log|D_n|
\ge\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_n}\log q
=\frac n{20}+o(n).
\tag{7.10}
\]

This is a positive linear lower bound inside one fixed central strip. It is stronger than saying that a particular product was estimated badly: the target-prime local Smith coordinates themselves are independent.

## 8. Products, resultants, factorial ratios, and cyclotomic packaging

### 8.1 Divisor products

Products over intervals of `c` are rising/falling factorials such as

\[
\prod_{c\in I}(2n+c)
\tag{8.1}
\]

and

\[
\prod_{c\in I}(2n+2-c).
\tag{8.2}
\]

Writing them as factorial ratios shortens the notation but not their integer size or their target-prime valuations. In the central strip each target prime occurs in exactly one factor.

### 8.2 Cyclotomic factorization

Cyclotomic factorization reorganizes a factorial or a product of linear forms into irreducible multiplicative pieces. It does not alter `v_q`. If a signed cyclotomic ratio cancels the Archimedean bulk by giving exponent zero to the relevant linear factor, it also cancels the unique target prime carried by that factor. Thus the height-versus-selectivity ledger is exact:

```text
retain the matched atom  => retain q and pay log q;
cancel the matched atom  => lose q.
```

### 8.3 Resultants and Vandermonde determinants

A resultant among coordinate polynomials introduces leading coefficients and products of pairwise root differences. On the strip (7.1), all nonzero root differences are units modulo every target prime. The only source of target valuation is therefore the matched vanishing atom. The resultant cannot amplify or share that valuation across targets.

If the resultant degree grows with the number of coordinate values, its determinant height grows proportionally. Dividing by universal difference factors leaves the diagonal target product. This is the same primitive saturation found for the multi-target Racah cutoff module at commit `f3e242f`.

### 8.4 Multiscale covers

Partitioning the `j`- or `c`-axis into dyadic or adaptive blocks does not create overlap. On the central strip the incidence graph between candidate primes and either coordinate axis is a matching. A block containing `t` candidate primes has unavoidable large-prime radical of logarithmic height

\[
\sum_{q\text{ in block}}\log q.
\tag{8.3}
\]

Summing over blocks gives the same total. Multiscale decomposition is useful only after an Apéry-specific theorem removes an `o(1)` fraction of this ambient weight or compresses the actual target subset inside each block.

## 9. The role of `q\mid A_j`

The divisor geometry is nonselective: it applies to every prime candidate. The only selective statement is

\[
q\mid A_j.
\tag{9.1}
\]

A resultant or determinant that genuinely uses (9.1) is no longer a consequence of the two linear identities; it is precisely a new Apéry theorem.

The natural generic constructions do not provide the needed saving:

1. Multiplying the relevant `A_j` values is far too large. In the central strips, `j\asymp n`, and each `\log A_j\asymp n`.
2. Interpolating `A_j` by a polynomial and evaluating at the marked outer point reproduces the one-point Apéry carrier. The current repository's Newton/Fitting audits show that the transverse evaluation ideal is the unknown target radical itself.
3. Fixed-order recurrence Bezout identities detect a second zero in the **same characteristic**. An isolated horizontal target supplies only one marked zero; the saturated continuant is a unit on the pure-cross locus.
4. Multi-target finite-Racah cutoffs put the full target product in the index of the two long coordinates; the primitive maximal Fitting ideal is one.

There is also a sharp logical warning against using bounded zero fibers. Fix `n` and declare, for every prime `q` in a positive-width top-half interval,

\[
Z_q^*=\{j(q),q-1-j(q)\}.
\tag{9.2}
\]

Every row has exactly one reflection orbit, hence at most two zeros, but every selected prime hits the same horizontal column `n`. This abstract model satisfies the complete two-coordinate divisor geometry. It proves that

```text
reflection + |Z_q| <= 2 + the linear carriers
```

is compatible with linear horizontal target weight. It is not asserted to be the actual Apéry zero family; it identifies the exact missing quantifier.

The recurrence-order warning is equally sharp. Because transfer to an index `j<q` is invertible modulo `q`, one can prescribe one zero line independently in each residue characteristic and combine the local initial states by CRT. Thus the Apéry operator, with the initial direction allowed to vary, also permits arbitrary finite horizontal stars. A successful proof must use arithmetic of the distinguished Apéry initial state, not recurrence order alone.

## 10. One minimal extra Apéry-specific input that would close the geometry

Here is a localized carrier theorem strong enough to complete the argument and weak enough to expose exactly what is missing.

### Apéry block-compression hypothesis

Let

\[
H=n^{1/3}.
\tag{10.1}
\]

For every interval `I\subset(\sqrt n,n]` of length between `H` and `2H` which lies inside one fixed quotient branch (so `a=\lfloor n/q\rfloor` and the direct/reflected orientation are constant), suppose there is a nonzero integer `D_{n,I}` such that

\[
\prod_{\substack{q\in I\text{ prime}\\q\mid A_{j(q)}}}q
\mid D_{n,I}
\tag{10.2}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\log|D_{n,I}|\le\varepsilon_n H,
\qquad \varepsilon_n\to0,
}
\tag{10.3}
\]

uniformly in `I`.

This is a genuinely Apéry-specific block carrier: the linear coordinate product has logarithmic height `\asymp H`, not `o(H)`.

### Deduction of `o(n)`

There are `O(\sqrt n)` quotient and fold boundaries in `(\sqrt n,n]`. Remove an `O(H)` neighborhood of each. Their total length, hence total possible Chebyshev weight, is

\[
O(\sqrt n\,H)=O(n^{5/6})=o(n).
\tag{10.4}
\]

Partition the remaining set into `O(n/H)` intervals to which the hypothesis applies. Multiplying their carriers gives a nonzero integer divisible by every remaining target prime and of logarithmic height

\[
O(n/H)\cdot\varepsilon_n H
=O(\varepsilon_n n)
=o(n).
\tag{10.5}
\]

Adding (10.4) proves

\[
\sum_{\substack{\sqrt n<q\le n\\q\mid A_{j(q)}}}\log q=o(n).
\tag{10.6}
\]

Thus one uniform `o(1)` block saving over the ambient prime mass is sufficient. An equivalent counting form is

\[
\#\{q\in I:q\mid A_{j(q)}\}
\ll \frac{H}{\omega(n)\log n},
\qquad \omega(n)\to\infty,
\tag{10.7}
\]

uniformly on the same branch intervals.

Neither (10.3) nor (10.7) follows from a bound on `|Z_q|`: those are vertical row bounds, whereas (10.7) is a horizontal moving-residue estimate with one selected residue in each characteristic.

This is the minimal additional type of input the two-coordinate geometry can use. Once it is supplied, the geometry handles quotient boundaries and coordinate boundaries at sublinear cost. Without it, the central matching strip in Section 7 remains untouched.

## 11. Final conclusion

The identities

\[
q\mid(n-j)(n+j+1),
\qquad
q\mid(2n+c)(2n+2-c)
\]

are exact and useful. They prove global `O(J\log n)` and `O(C\log n)` boundary estimates without dyadic leakage. Their deterministic power ends there.

The top-half quotient-one block contains two long affine coordinate lines. Small-coordinate removal leaves ambient prime weight `n/2+o(n)`. Covering the lines completely requires `3J+C\ge n+O(1)`. On a central strip the coordinate valuation matrix is diagonal, pairwise differences are units, and every retained target costs its own factor `q`. Products, resultants, factorial ratios, cyclotomic decompositions, and multiscale groupings cannot reduce this cost.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The two-coordinate divisor geometry alone cannot prove P3.2.}
}
\]

Its sharp deterministic output is the trivial linear Chebyshev bound. The route can reopen only through a new Apéry-specific horizontal theorem, such as the block-compression hypothesis (10.2)--(10.3), that saves an `o(1)` factor over the ambient prime mass in each moving branch interval.
