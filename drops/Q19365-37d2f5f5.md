# R3b — horizontal phase audit and hardness barrier

## Executive verdict

There are two useful conclusions.

1. **I do not obtain H1 from any of the surveyed prime-exponential-sum technology.**  The obstruction can be located earlier and more sharply than “Apéry is not multiplicative.”  After Vaughan decomposition the relevant Type I/II object is literally
   \[
   e_{km}\!\left(b_{\,n-km}\right),
   \]
   with **both the modulus and the recurrence index depending on the product \(km\)**.  In every Fermat-quotient analogue that actually yields cancellation, the corresponding product dependence is linearized by the group-homomorphism law
   \[
   q_p(km)\equiv q_p(k)+q_p(m)\pmod p.
   \]
   Nothing analogous is available for the Apéry row.  The standard large sieve fails even earlier if applied to the phase points \(b_{n-p}/p\): the phase points with value \(0\) have multiplicity exactly \(N_1(n)\), so the required spacing input already contains the target.

2. There **is** a clean recognized-open-problem implication in the other direction:
   **Malik–Straub Conjecture 6.3 implies W.**
   Their conjecture says that a proportion
   \[
   \rho=e^{-1/2}
   \]
   of primes divide no Apéry number at all.  Such primes are automatically excluded from every q=1 bad window, and the density statement gives
   \[
   N_1(n)\le (1-\rho+o(1))L(n).
   \]
   Hence every fixed \(\delta<e^{-1/2}\) works in W for all sufficiently large \(n\).

I do **not** find a converse implication from H1, W, or even
\(N_1(n)=o(n/\log n)\) to Malik–Straub, to infinitely many ordinary primes of
the weight-4 level-8 newform, or to a positive-density non-Wieferich theorem.
The quantifiers point in different directions: our target is a worst-case
bound on one **diagonal** \(n=p+r\), whereas those problems ask about the
population of whole rows \(Z_p\) or about \(p^2\)-lifts.

A further useful correction to emphasis is that H1 is stronger than W needs.
With the normalization
\[
L(n)=\#\{p:n/2<p\le n,\ p\ {\rm prime}\},
\]
the one-phase identity gives
\[
N_1(n)\le \frac{L(n)}2+\frac{|S_1(n)|}{4}.
\]
Thus **any** fixed saving
\[
|S_1(n)|\le (2-\eta)L(n)
\]
from the trivial \(2L(n)\) bound already proves W, with
\(\delta=\eta/4\).  The proposed H1 bound
\(|S_1|\le(1-\eta)L\) would give the much stronger
\(\delta=(1+\eta)/4\).

# 1. Normalize S1 first: the two phases are the same phase

Let
\[
I_n=\{p\text{ prime}:n/2<p\le n\},\qquad L=L(n)=|I_n|.
\]
For \(p\in I_n\), put
\[
r=n-p,\qquad r^\ast=p-1-r=2p-n-1.
\]
Then \(0\le r,r^\ast<p\), and the proved Apéry reflection says
\[
b_{r^\ast}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
\]

The first sum in the question uses \(s=r\), because \(p=n-s\).  The second
uses \(s=r^\ast\), because
\[
p=\frac{n+r^\ast+1}{2}.
\]
Therefore the two summands attached to the same top-window prime are equal:
\[
e\!\left(\frac{b_r}{p}\right)
=
e\!\left(\frac{b_{r^\ast}}p\right).
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{
S_1(n)=2T(n),\qquad
T(n):=\sum_{p\in I_n}e_p(b_{n-p}),
}
\tag{1.1}
\]
where \(e_p(x)=e^{2\pi i x/p}\).

This is the cleanest form in which to audit analytic tools.  There is no
advantage left in treating the two affine rays separately.

Let
\[
B=N_1(n)=\#\{p\in I_n:p\mid b_{n-p}\}.
\]
For a bad prime the corresponding summand of \(T\) is \(1\); for every other
prime its real part is at least \(-1\).  Hence
\[
\Re T\ge B-(L-B)=2B-L,
\]
and so
\[
\boxed{\Re S_1\ge4B-2L.}
\tag{1.2}
\]
It follows that
\[
\boxed{
N_1(n)\le \frac L2+\frac{\Re S_1(n)}4
          \le \frac L2+\frac{|S_1(n)|}{4}.
}
\tag{1.3}
\]

Thus if
\[
|S_1(n)|\le cL,\qquad c<2,
\]
then
\[
N_1(n)\le\frac{2+c}{4}L
       =\left(1-\frac{2-c}{4}\right)L.
\tag{1.4}
\]
This is the verified one-phase lemma in its sharp constant form.

For H1 as stated, \(c=1-\eta\), giving
\[
N_1(n)\le \frac{3-\eta}{4}L.
\tag{1.5}
\]
For the weaker analytic target
\[
|S_1(n)|\le(2-\eta)L,
\tag{1.6}
\]
one still gets W with \(\delta=\eta/4\).

The coordinator's scan is therefore even more encouraging than H1 requires:
the observed maximum \(0.34021L\) is very far below the only load-bearing
barrier \(2L\).

# Part 1 — attack H1 honestly

## 2. Vaughan/Vinogradov Type I–II: the exact first failed step

For a prime-weighted version define
\[
F_n(m):=e_m(b_{n-m})
\]
for \(m\asymp n\), and consider
\[
\mathcal T(n;X,Y)
=
\sum_{X<m\le Y}\Lambda(m)F_n(m).
\tag{2.1}
\]
Partial summation would transfer a sufficiently uniform bound for such
von-Mangoldt sums to the prime sum \(T(n)\), so using Vaughan's identity is
legitimate at the formal decomposition stage.

Vaughan's identity applies to an **arbitrary** complex function \(F_n\).
The resulting pieces include Type-I sums of the shape
\[
\sum_{d\le U}a_d\sum_{m\le n/d}
 e_{dm}\!\left(b_{n-dm}\right)
\tag{2.2}
\]
and Type-II/bilinear sums of the shape
\[
\sum_{d\sim D}\sum_{m\sim M}
 \alpha_d\beta_m
 e_{dm}\!\left(b_{n-dm}\right).
\tag{2.3}
\]

This is exactly where the method stops.

### What happens in the Fermat-quotient model

For Fermat quotients
\[
q_p(u)=\frac{u^{p-1}-1}{p}\pmod p
\]
one has the fundamental law
\[
\boxed{
q_p(uv)\equiv q_p(u)+q_p(v)\pmod p.
}
\tag{2.4}
\]
Thus
\[
u\longmapsto e_p(aq_p(u))
\]
is a multiplicative character modulo \(p^2\).  This is the feature used by
Heath-Brown to turn the additive Fermat-quotient sum into an ordinary
character-sum problem, where Burgess-type machinery becomes available.

For prime arguments, Shparlinski's 2011 paper *Bounds of multiplicative
character sums with Fermat quotients of primes* explicitly applies Vaughan's
identity.  Its post-Vaughan input is not generic: it invokes one-dimensional
and bilinear bounds for
\[
\chi(q_p(km)).
\]
The paper's displayed Vaughan decomposition has inner sums \(f(km)\), and the
specialized lemmas bound exactly the \(q_p(km)\) expressions.  In other words,
the hard theorem has already used the algebra behind (2.4) before Vaughan can
produce a saving.

### Why this does not transfer

For Apéry there is no analogue
\[
b_{km}\stackrel{?}{=}\Phi(b_k,b_m),\qquad
b_{x+y}\stackrel{?}{=}\Psi(b_x,b_y)
\tag{2.5}
\]
in a fixed finite group, nor any fixed character \(\chi\) such that
\[
e_{km}(b_{n-km})
\]
factors into a function of \(k\) times a function of \(m\).

The three-term recurrence gives instead a **nonautonomous** transfer:
the step matrix from index \(s\) to \(s+1\) depends polynomially on \(s\).
After Vaughan, the index is \(s=n-km\), while the modulus is the composite
integer \(km\).  Even propagation of the divided recurrence modulo \(km\)
can encounter nonunits among the coefficients \((s+1)^3\).  Passing to the
division-free normalization avoids illegal division but leaves a product of
matrices depending on every intermediate index.  It does not create a
bilinear phase.

So the first failed implication is precise:

> **Vaughan decomposition succeeds; the first Type-I/II estimate has no
> available structural input.**  The known Fermat-quotient proof replaces
> \(q_p(km)\) by a group-law expression.  There is no corresponding reduction
> of \(b_{n-km}\).

I obtain no \((2-\eta)L\) bound from this route.

## 3. Heath-Brown's Heilbronn/Fermat-quotient mechanism

This analogy is worth separating from Vaughan because it identifies the
missing algebra even more cleanly.

For fixed \(p\), the map
\[
q_p:(\mathbb Z/p^2\mathbb Z)^\times\to\mathbb F_p
\]
is a group homomorphism from multiplication to addition.  Therefore its
zero fibre
\[
\{u:q_p(u)=0\}
\]
is a multiplicative subgroup: the subgroup of \(p\)-th-power residues modulo
\(p^2\).  Its other fibres are cosets.

Two classical consequences are exactly the two things one wants in an
exponential-sum proof:

* the additive phase \(e_p(aq_p(u))\) is a multiplicative character modulo
  \(p^2\);
* the zero event \(q_p(u)=0\) has multiplicative closure.

This is why the Heilbronn sum admits tools that do not apply to a generic
table of residues.

### Bourgain–Ford–Konyagin–Shparlinski

Bourgain–Ford–Konyagin–Shparlinski, *On the Divisibility of Fermat
Quotients*, Michigan Math. J. 59 (2010), 313–328, study the least \(a\)
with
\[
q_p(a)\not\equiv0\pmod p
\]
and obtain the polylogarithmic bound
\[
a\le(\log p)^{463/252+o(1)}.
\]
The proof exploits precisely the multiplicative structure of the kernel,
combined with smooth-number/subgroup and exponential-sum input.

To imitate this for Apéry zeros one would need a closure law such as
\[
r,s\in Z_p\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Phi(r,s)\in Z_p
\tag{3.1}
\]
for many \(\Phi(r,s)\), or a large group whose coset is \(Z_p\).
Nothing like this is known and the numerical zero sets do not look like
subgroups.

The Apéry–Lucas law
\[
b_{ap+r}\equiv b_a b_r\pmod p
\tag{3.2}
\]
does **not** repair the problem.  In q=1 we are precisely inside the seed row
\(0\le r<p\).  Formula (3.2) describes concatenating a *new base-\(p\)
digit above that row*; it supplies no binary operation closing the seed
zero set \(Z_p\) itself.

So the first failed step in the BFKSh template is the subgroup/kernel step,
before any analytic estimate is invoked.

### Heath-Brown bounds

Shparlinski's survey paper on Fermat quotients records Heath-Brown's
nontrivial exponential-sum bounds for consecutive Fermat quotients; with the
later/generalized parameter optimization, nontriviality reaches intervals
\(N\ge p^{1/2+\varepsilon}\).  The mechanism is still the same
multiplicative-character reinterpretation modulo \(p^2\).

There is no known character \(\chi_p\) on an algebraic group of bounded
complexity satisfying
\[
e_p(b_s)=\chi_p(g^s)
\quad\text{or}\quad
e_p(b_s)=\chi_p(A^s)
\tag{3.3}
\]
with \(g\) or \(A\) fixed.  The Apéry recurrence's step matrix changes with
\(s\).  This is the first failed step.

## 4. Large sieve over the phase points: it becomes circular

There are two natural ways to try a large sieve, and both fail for a very
specific reason.

### 4.1 Large sieve for the fractions \(b_{n-p}/p\)

Define
\[
\alpha_p=\frac{b_{n-p}\bmod p}{p}\in[0,1).
\tag{4.1}
\]
Then
\[
T(n)=\sum_{p\in I_n}e(\alpha_p).
\]

The dual large sieve can control an average over frequencies
\[
\sum_{h\le H}
\left|\sum_{p\in I_n}c_p e(h\alpha_p)\right|^2
\tag{4.2}
\]
provided the points \(\alpha_p\) are separated.

But the target bad primes satisfy
\[
p\mid b_{n-p}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\alpha_p=0.
\tag{4.3}
\]
Thus \(N_1(n)\) phase points are **exactly coincident** at zero.

Any large-sieve theorem stated with a minimum spacing \(\Delta\) is therefore
inapplicable unless \(N_1(n)\le1\).  A multiplicity-sensitive replacement
would introduce the maximum point multiplicity, which is at least
\(N_1(n)\).  That is already the quantity to be bounded.

Even if one discards the coincident point problem, distinct rational numbers
with denominators \(\asymp n\) have only the generic spacing
\[
\|\alpha_p-\alpha_q\|\gg n^{-2}.
\]
The dual large sieve then naturally asks for \(H\) on the \(n^2\) scale.
It gives an **average over \(h\)**; it does not single out the one frequency
\(h=1\).  Bounding the \(h=1\) term by the whole second moment is much larger
than \(L^2\) and gives no cancellation.

Hence the first failed step is:

> To use spacing, one must already control the multiplicity of
> \(\alpha_p=0\); to use frequency averaging, one still lacks a mechanism
> forcing the distinguished frequency \(h=1\) to be typical.

### 4.2 Square-modulus large sieve from Fermat quotients

Shparlinski's BLMS paper uses a Baier–Zhao large sieve with square moduli to
average Fermat-quotient exponential sums over \(p\).  That works because
\[
e_p(aq_p(u))
\]
is a primitive (or controlled) Dirichlet character modulo \(p^2\), so there
is a **common coefficient sequence** being tested against a family of
characters with square conductor.

There is no representation
\[
e_p(b_{n-p})=\chi_{p^2}(A_n)
\tag{4.4}
\]
for one \(p\)-independent integer \(A_n\) and a coherent family of
characters \(\chi_{p^2}\).  The numerator itself changes from
\(b_{n-p}\) to \(b_{n-q}\) as the modulus changes.

So the square-modulus large sieve does not even reach the same input class.

## 5. Constant-coefficient linear recurrences and Shparlinski-type bounds

There is a substantial literature on exponential sums involving linear
recurrence sequences modulo a **fixed** finite field or residue ring.
The basic structural form is constant coefficient:
\[
u_{m+r}=c_{r-1}u_{m+r-1}+\cdots+c_0u_m.
\tag{5.1}
\]
Modulo \(p\), the state evolves by a fixed companion matrix \(A\):
\[
v_m=A^m v_0.
\tag{5.2}
\]
After splitting the characteristic polynomial, the sequence is built from
fixed powers \(\lambda_i^m\).  Its period and the multiplicative orders of
the \(\lambda_i\)'s are therefore available as analytic parameters.

Recent formulations of Shparlinski's bounds make this dependence explicit:
for an irreducible characteristic polynomial, nontriviality is stated in
terms of the period \(\tau\) and gcd conditions involving
\(\tau\) and \(p^d-1\).  This is not cosmetic.  It is the source of the
finite-field orbit structure.

For Apéry,
\[
(s+1)^3 b_{s+1}
=
(34s^3+51s^2+27s+5)b_s-s^3b_{s-1}.
\tag{5.3}
\]
The transfer matrix is \(A_s\), not one fixed \(A\).  The cleared sequence
\((s!)^3b_s\) still satisfies a recurrence with coefficients depending on
\(s\).  There are therefore no fixed characteristic roots
\(\lambda_i\), no period coming from an order in
\(\mathbb F_{p^r}^\times\), and no identity \(v_s=A^sv_0\).

There is an additional mismatch in H1: the characteristic also moves,
\(p=n-s\).  A theorem uniform for one fixed recurrence modulo one fixed
prime does not automatically control the diagonal
\[
(s,p)=(n-p,p).
\]

So the first failed step is exactly (5.2): **the Apéry orbit is
nonautonomous**.

The same diagnosis applies to results on \(a^m\bmod q\), \(a^m\bmod m\),
and their nonlinear-index variants.  Their analytic leverage comes from a
fixed-base multiplicative orbit and its order.  Replacing \(a^m\) by a
P-recursive nonautonomous orbit removes that organizing group.

## 6. Fixed-modulus reciprocal/Kloosterman technology also misses the target

A different family of estimates treats phases such as
\[
e_p\!\left(\frac{a}{f(x)}\right)
\]
or bilinear variants with a **fixed modulus \(p\)** and bounded-degree
rational function \(f\).

Our phase is
\[
e_{n-s}(b_s),
\tag{6.1}
\]
or, after Vaughan,
\[
e_{km}(b_{n-km}).
\tag{6.2}
\]
There is neither a fixed modulus nor a bounded-degree rational phase in the
summation variable.  The integer \(b_s\) has exponential Archimedean growth
and is generated by a nonautonomous recurrence.

One can Taylor-expand \(1/(n-s)\) only for a genuinely short range of \(s\).
Because \(b_s\) grows like \(\exp(3.526s)\), reducing the numerator before
taking the phase is indispensable; a real-analytic expansion of
\(b_s/(n-s)\) does not respect that reduction.  Restricting to
\(s=O(\log n)\) gives only \(O(\log n)\) terms, negligible compared with
\(L\asymp n/\log n\), and says nothing about the long bulk.

## 7. What Part 1 actually yields

I find **no existing theorem** among the surveyed tools that proves even
\[
|S_1(n)|\le(2-\eta)L(n)
\tag{7.1}
\]
for one fixed \(\eta>0\) and every sufficiently large \(n\).

This is the weakest constant-saving statement that the one-phase lemma needs.

The unconditional analytic bound remains
\[
|S_1(n)|\le2L(n).
\tag{7.2}
\]

There is no elementary uniform improvement from the fact that a good residue
is nonzero.  A nonzero residue can be \(1\), giving
\[
\Re e_p(1)=1-O(p^{-2}),
\]
so excluding exact zero alone gives only \(O(L/n^2)\)-scale angular loss in
the worst case.

The verified numerical behavior
\[
|S_1|/L\asymp L^{-1/2}
\]
in the median range is therefore qualitatively much stronger than anything
the above black-box technology forces.

My recommendation is to replace “prove H1” by the formally weaker target

\[
\boxed{
\sup_{n\ge n_0}\frac{|S_1(n)|}{L(n)}\le2-\eta
}
\tag{H1'}
\]
for any fixed \(\eta>0\).
It is enough for W, and it cleanly exposes the exact analytic barrier.

# Part 2 — recognized hardness / conjectural implications

## 8. Malik–Straub Conjecture 6.3 implies W

This is the cleanest rigorous implication I found.

Let
\[
\mathcal P_0
=
\{p:\ p\nmid b_m\text{ for every }m\ge0\}
\]
be the set of primes dividing no Apéry number.

Malik and Straub, *Divisibility properties of sporadic Apéry-like numbers*,
Research in Number Theory 2 (2016), Article 5, Conjecture 6.3, state:
\[
\boxed{
\frac{\#\{p\le x:p\in\mathcal P_0\}}{\pi(x)}
\longrightarrow
\rho:=e^{-1/2}.
}
\tag{8.1}
\]
They also explicitly note that the weaker question whether there are
infinitely many such primes was posed by Rowland–Yassawi and is open.

Assume (8.1).  Write
\[
A(x)=\#\{p\le x:p\in\mathcal P_0\}.
\]
Then
\[
A(x)=\rho\pi(x)+o(\pi(x)).
\tag{8.2}
\]
By the prime number theorem,
\[
L(n)=\pi(n)-\pi(n/2)\sim\frac{n}{2\log n}.
\tag{8.3}
\]
Subtract (8.2) at \(n\) and \(n/2\):
\[
A(n)-A(n/2)
=
\rho(\pi(n)-\pi(n/2))+o(n/\log n)
=
(\rho+o(1))L(n).
\tag{8.4}
\]

Every prime counted on the left side is in the q=1 window and divides
**no** Apéry number, hence certainly does not divide \(b_{n-p}\).
Therefore
\[
N_1(n)
\le
L(n)-\bigl(A(n)-A(n/2)\bigr)
=
(1-\rho+o(1))L(n).
\tag{8.5}
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Malik--Straub Conjecture 6.3}
\Longrightarrow
\text{W for every }\delta<e^{-1/2}.
}
\tag{8.6}
\]

This implication is pointwise in \(n\) once the natural-density asymptotic is
assumed; no random model is used in the proof.

### What it does not imply

Conjecture 6.3 gives only a constant excluded proportion.  It does **not**
give
\[
N_1(n)=o(L(n)).
\]
The remaining proportion \(1-e^{-1/2}\) of primes could, as far as the
conjecture itself says, be arranged very unfavorably on a single diagonal.

It also does not imply H1: knowing merely that a prime has no zero says that
\(b_{n-p}\not\equiv0\pmod p\), but the phase may still be
\(e_p(1)\), arbitrarily close to \(1\).

## 9. Does our target imply infinitely many primes dividing no Apéry number?

I do not see such an implication, and there is a simple quantifier barrier.

The full q=1 target says that for every \(n\), the diagonal
\[
\{(p,r):p+r=n,\ p\in(n/2,n],\ r\in Z_p\}
\tag{9.1}
\]
contains \(o(n/\log n)\) points.

“Prime \(p\) divides some Apéry number” asks only whether the entire row
\(Z_p\) is nonempty.

A family can have every row nonempty while every diagonal is tiny.  The
abstract reflection-compatible example
\[
Z_p=\{1,p-2\}
\tag{9.2}
\]
has no consecutive zeros and is closed under \(r\mapsto p-1-r\).
Every row is nonempty.  Yet on a q=1 diagonal one needs either
\[
n=p+1
\quad\text{or}\quad
n=2p-2,
\]
so for a fixed \(n\) there are at most two candidate primes.

This is **not** claimed to be realizable by the Apéry recurrence; it is an
information-theoretic countermodel showing that the statement
\(N_1(n)=o(n/\log n)\), by itself, does not encode row-emptiness.

Therefore I cannot turn a proof of our target into a proof of the
Rowland–Yassawi/Malik–Straub infinitude problem without an additional
Apéry-specific theorem coupling diagonal sparsity to whole-row emptiness.

The same observation applies a fortiori to W and H1.

## 10. Ordinary primes for the weight-4 level-8 newform

The repository has the exact banked relation
\[
b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p(f)\pmod p,
\qquad
f=\eta(2z)^4\eta(4z)^4,
\tag{10.1}
\]
and identifies odd \(Z_p\) exactly with non-ordinary primes
\(p\mid a_p(f)\).

The general problem is recognized as difficult: current literature still
states that infinitely many ordinary primes are not known for \(\Delta\), or
more generally for arbitrary non-CM modular forms of weight \(k\ge4\).

Does our q=1 target imply progress on that problem?  Not directly.

If \(p\) is non-ordinary, the central zero creates the q=1 incidence at
\[
n=p+\frac{p-1}{2}=\frac{3p-1}{2}.
\tag{10.2}
\]
The map
\[
p\longmapsto \frac{3p-1}{2}
\tag{10.3}
\]
is injective.  Thus even if there were infinitely many—or hypothetically
extremely many—non-ordinary primes, their **forced central incidences occur
at different \(n\)**, one per diagonal.

At \(n=(3p-1)/2\), the target allows
\[
N_1(n)=1
\]
with enormous room to spare, since \(n/\log n\to\infty\).

So neither W nor \(N_1=o(n/\log n)\) gives a counting contradiction to an
abundance of non-ordinary primes.

This does not prove logical independence inside the actual Apéry system:
conceivably some unknown theorem could show that many non-ordinary rows force
additional off-center zeros that align.  But no such theorem is presently
known, and the central modular-form correspondence alone gives no implication.

The same argument shows H1 does not force ordinary primes: a single phase
equal to \(1\) among \(L\asymp p/\log p\) phases does not threaten a
constant-saving exponential-sum bound.

## 11. Non-Wieferich-type statements: wrong p-adic level

Any usual “Wieferich-type” refinement asks about a lift modulo \(p^2\), for
example whether a mod-\(p\) relation acquires an extra factor \(p\).

Our target sees only
\[
b_{n-p}\pmod p.
\tag{11.1}
\]
It is unchanged if one alters every hypothetical \(p^2\)-lift while keeping
the same mod-\(p\) row.

Consequently, no implication to a positive density of non-Wieferich-type
primes follows from the q=1 statement alone.

Conversely, a theorem saying that some distinguished Apéry or modular-form
quantity is usually not divisible by \(p^2\) does not control how often
\(b_{n-p}\) is divisible by \(p\) across the top window.  It is a valuation
statement conditional on—or at a special instance of—a mod-\(p\) event, not
a cross-characteristic count of those events.

This matches the repository's first-jet audit: adding a \(p^2\) condition did
not automatically create a second independent zero equation.

## 12. Does abc imply W?

I find no derivation, and the usual abc mechanism points in the wrong
direction.

For a fixed bad prime
\[
p\mid b_s,\qquad s=n-p<n/2,
\]
abc can only become relevant after one embeds \(b_s\) into a suitable
integer equation.  Standard abc consequences tend to force **large radicals**
or large prime factors of an integer.  W needs an upper bound on the number
of prescribed primes \(p\asymp n\) dividing many *different* integers
\(b_s\).

Suppose a bad set \(P_n\) has \(M\) primes.  Since the indices
\[
s=n-p
\]
are distinct,
\[
\prod_{p\in P_n}p
\quad\text{divides}\quad
\prod_{p\in P_n} b_{n-p}.
\tag{12.1}
\]
The left side has logarithm
\[
M\log n+O(M),
\tag{12.2}
\]
while
\[
\log b_s\sim\lambda s,\qquad \lambda\approx3.526.
\]
The right side has available logarithmic height
\[
\sum_{p\in P_n}\log b_{n-p}
\asymp
\lambda\sum_{p\in P_n}(n-p),
\tag{12.3}
\]
which can be of order \(Mn\), not \(M\log n\).

So even the crude integer capacity exceeds what is needed by a factor of
order \(n/\log n\).  An abc lower bound on radicals does not reverse this
inequality.  One would need a new **cross-index identity** forcing all the
bad primes into one small-height integer.  Fable's closure of the natural
minor/resultant carriers says the obvious way to manufacture such an integer
does not have the required height.

Therefore:
\[
\boxed{\text{No known }abc\Rightarrow W\text{ implication.}}
\tag{12.4}
\]
I do not claim a theorem that abc cannot imply W through some unknown
Apéry-specific identity.

## 13. Does GRH imply W?

Not in any standard formulation.

GRH is powerful when the prime condition is encoded as Frobenius lying in a
specified subset of a **fixed** Galois extension or compatible system.
Here the condition is
\[
p\mid b_{n-p},
\tag{13.1}
\]
where

* the residual characteristic is the same \(p\) being counted;
* the coefficient index \(n-p\) moves with \(p\);
* the relevant mod-\(p\) Apéry row is in defining characteristic.

The repository's compatible-system audit already isolated precisely this
problem: auxiliary-\(\ell\) large sieves require \(\ell\ne p\), whereas the
target is the diagonal \(\ell=p\).

There is no fixed finite extension \(K_n/\mathbb Q\) of controlled
discriminant known for which (13.1) is a fixed Chebotarev condition with
enough uniformity in \(n\).  Without such an encoding, GRH has no object on
which to act.

Thus ordinary Dirichlet GRH, Artin GRH, or GRH for the fixed modular form do
not presently imply W.

## 14. Does Hardy–Littlewood k-tuples imply W?

No: it controls the wrong Boolean variable.

In S1 the prime indicators are
\[
[n-s\text{ prime}]
\quad\text{and}\quad
[(n+s+1)/2\text{ prime}].
\]
A sufficiently uniform Hardy–Littlewood conjecture can predict the population
and correlations of those prime flags.

But after conditioning on which values are prime, the remaining sum is
\[
\sum_{p\in I_n}e_p(b_{n-p}),
\tag{14.1}
\]
and Hardy–Littlewood says nothing about these residue phases.

Indeed the coordinator's experiment already shows that the hard phenomenon
is angular cancellation **among an essentially deterministic set of about
\(L\) primes**, not uncertainty in the number of primes.

So the first failed step is immediate: replacing the prime indicator by its
Hardy–Littlewood main term leaves exactly the horizontal Apéry phase problem.

## 15. What kind of “Chowla conjecture” would imply the target?

A genuinely uniform horizontal Chowla statement would, but it would be a new
conjecture rather than a recognized existing theorem/conjecture.

For example, either of the following would suffice:

### Phase form

For every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), uniformly in all large \(n\),
\[
\left|
\sum_{p\in I_n} e_p(b_{n-p})
\right|
\le (1-\varepsilon)L(n).
\tag{15.1}
\]
By (1.1), this is a form of H1 and hence gives W.

To obtain the full
\[
N_1(n)=o(L(n)),
\]
one would want the stronger square-root/random-walk prediction
\[
\sum_{p\in I_n} e_p(b_{n-p})
=O_\varepsilon(L^{1/2+\varepsilon})
\tag{15.2}
\]
uniformly in \(n\).

### Zero-event form

A uniform large-deviation conjecture saying that the events
\[
X_{n,p}=1_{\{p\mid b_{n-p}\}}
\tag{15.3}
\]
have enough high-order cross-prime decorrelation to exclude
\(\sum X_{n,p}\ge\varepsilon L\) for **every** \(n\) would also suffice.

But fixed-order moments are not enough for an all-\(n\) conclusion.  The
repository has already seen this boundary: low factorial moments and
average-in-\(n\) dispersion can prove density-one statements while still
leaving isolated exceptional \(n\)'s untouched.

Thus a “Chowla-type” assumption capable of proving the all-index target must
have growing-order or genuinely uniform tail content.  I do not know a
recognized standard conjecture in the literature stated at that strength for
Apéry residues.

Calling (15.1) or (15.2) “Chowla” is therefore descriptive, not an appeal to
an existing named conjecture.

# 17. Why W need not imply H1: another useful separation

At the level of information contained in the statements, W only bounds the
number of phases that are **exactly** \(1\).

H1 controls the vector sum of **all** phases.

An abstract residue model can have
\[
b_{n-p}\equiv1\pmod p
\]
for every top-window prime.  Then
\[
N_1(n)=0
\]
but
\[
S_1(n)=2\sum_{p\in I_n}e_p(1)
      =2L(n)+o(L(n)).
\tag{17.1}
\]
So zero sparsity alone cannot force H1.  Again this is not asserted to be an
Apéry-realizable model; it shows why proving W by a zero-set route and proving
H1 by an angular-cancellation route are logically different projects.

This is also why Malik–Straub Conjecture 6.3 does not imply H1.

# 18. Self-attack / limitations

1. **Literature completeness.**  I searched the specific families named in the
   question, plus the current linear-recurrence and ordinary-prime literature.
   I cannot prove that no obscure theorem exists.  My negative claims are
   “the standard theorem does not apply, for the stated first failed step,”
   not bibliographic nonexistence theorems.

2. **Vaughan failure is not an impossibility theorem.**  The fact that known
   Type-I/II estimates need group structure does not prove that a new
   bilinear estimate for
   \[
   e_{km}(b_{n-km})
   \]
   is impossible.  Such an estimate would in fact be a very plausible
   statement of the missing new ingredient.

3. **Large-sieve circularity.**  I used the literal phase points
   \(\alpha_p=(b_{n-p}\bmod p)/p\).  A more sophisticated large sieve could
   use a lifted vector of several Apéry coordinates rather than these scalar
   points.  The existing carrier/minor closure strongly discourages the
   obvious versions, but does not rule out an unknown representation.

4. **Abstract countermodels.**  The examples \(Z_p=\{1,p-2\}\) and “all
   phases \(=e_p(1)\)” are only information-theoretic separations.  They do
   not satisfy the Apéry recurrence and do not prove formal independence of
   the corresponding arithmetic assertions.

5. **Ordinary-prime relation.**  I used the repository's already checked
   equivalence between the central Apéry zero and non-ordinarity of the
   weight-4 level-8 newform.  I did not reprove Ahlgren–Ono/Stienstra–Beukers
   inside this audit.

6. **No new numerical computation.**  The prompt forbids Python/sandbox work,
   and the coordinator supplied an independent reproduction of the R3 scan.
   I therefore used those verified measurements and did not silently run a
   second computation.

7. **Normalization of L.**  Throughout this response
   \[
   L(n)=\pi(n)-\pi(n/2)
   \]
   is the number of q=1 primes.  Since S1 contains the two reflected lifts,
   its trivial bound is \(2L\).  If Q19361 used a convention in which
   \(L\) denotes the *two-lift term count*, divide the displayed S1/L
   constants by the corresponding factor of two.  The invariant statement
   is (1.3).

# 19. What I would do next

The result of this audit is that “horizontal exponential sum” should not be
treated as a generic analytic-number-theory problem.  The generic prime
machinery decomposes the prime indicator successfully and then immediately
asks for a new Apéry-specific bilinear theorem.

The sharp next lemma is therefore:

> **Apéry Type-II constant-saving lemma.**  
> There are fixed \(\eta>0\) and admissible dyadic Type-II ranges
> \(D,M\) with \(DM\asymp n\) such that the Vaughan pieces associated to
> \[
> (d,m)\longmapsto e_{dm}(b_{n-dm})
> \]
> have a power or at least fixed-proportion saving over the trivial bilinear
> bound, uniformly in \(n\).

One should try to falsify this **directly** before building more carriers.
If it is true, Vaughan can convert it to (H1').  If it is false, the observed
square-root behavior of the prime sum must be coming from structure that is
destroyed by the composite-modulus Type-II decomposition, and a different
identity is needed.

The hardness-barrier result in Part 2 says there is already a respectable
conditional stopping point: **Malik–Straub 6.3 proves W outright.**  So W is
not merely an arbitrary intermediate conjecture; it sits strictly below a
recognized open conjecture about zero-free Apéry primes, while the full
\(N_1=o(L)\) target demands much more cross-characteristic decorrelation than
that conjecture supplies.
