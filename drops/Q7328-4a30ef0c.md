## 2. Exact identity for top primes

Let

\[
R_n=[x^n]B(x)(-\log(1-x))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{b_{n-k}}k.
\]

Write

\[
U_n=L_nR_n,\qquad L_n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n).
\]

Fix a prime

\[
p\in(n/2,n].
\]

Because `p` occurs in `L_n` exactly once, modulo `p` we have

\[
L_n/p\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Now split the sum defining `R_n`. The only term whose denominator contains the prime `p` is the term with

\[
k=p,
\]

because every other `k` satisfies `1\le k<n` and `p\nmid k`.

Hence

\[
U_n=L_n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{b_{n-k}}k
\]

has modulo-`p` contribution

\[
(L_n/p)b_{n-p}
\]

from the `k=p` term, while every other summand contains a factor `L_n` divisible by `p` and therefore vanishes modulo `p`.

Therefore

\[
U_n\equiv (L_n/p)b_{n-p}\pmod p.
\]

Since `L_n/p` is invertible modulo `p`,

\[
p\mid U_n\iff p\mid b_{n-p}.
\]

This proves the stated equivalence.

## 3. Radical reformulation

Define the top radical over primes `(n/2,n]` by

\[
\operatorname{rad}_{top}(m)=\prod_{n/2<p\le n,\ p\mid m}p.
\]

The previous equivalence gives

\[
\operatorname{rad}_{top}(\gcd(L_n,U_n))
=\prod_{n/2<p\le n,\ p\mid b_{n-p}}p.
\]

Equivalently, because every top prime divides `L_n`, failure of a top prime to divide `U_n` is exactly survival of that prime in the reduced denominator of `R_n`.

If

\[
R_n=A_n/D_n,\qquad \gcd(A_n,D_n)=1,
\]

then

\[
p\mid D_n
\iff
p\nmid U_n
\]

for primes in the top interval. Hence

\[
\operatorname{rad}_{top}(\gcd(L_n,U_n))
=rac{\prod_{n/2<p\le n}p}{\operatorname{rad}_{top}(D_n)}.
\]

This identity is exact.

# 4. The required denominator lower bound

The desired statement is essentially

\[
\log D_n\ge \log L_n-o(n).
\]

Equivalently, almost all prime mass occurring in `L_n` must remain in the reduced denominator of `R_n`.

This is much stronger than the usual denominator statements for G-functions.

## What standard theory gives

For a G-function satisfying a differential equation with arithmetic coefficients, one obtains results of the form:

- coefficients have controlled denominator growth;
- there exists an integer sequence multiplying coefficients to make them integral;
- local denominators are bounded using p-adic radii and nilpotence.

These are **upper bounds**:

\[
D_n\mid M_n
\]

for some controlled `M_n`, or estimates such as

\[
\log D_n=O(n).
\]

They do not imply that the denominator is large.

An upper bound permits complete cancellation. It cannot rule out

\[
D_n=1
\]

or small denominator subsequences.

Therefore global nilpotence alone cannot prove the required lower bound.

---

# 5. Why local monodromy is insufficient

The singularity structure of the Apéry differential equation controls:

- possible p-adic slopes;
- local exponent data;
- Frobenius compatibility.

However, the denominator of the specific coefficient convolution

\[
B(x)(-\log(1-x))
\]

is a finer arithmetic invariant.

The question is not whether the differential equation allows denominators, but whether reduction modulo each large prime produces a nonzero obstruction.

The missing statement is effectively:

For almost every prime `p` in `(n/2,n]`,

\[
b_{n-p}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

That is a distribution/nonvanishing statement about Apéry coefficients modulo varying primes.

Neither global nilpotence nor regular-singular monodromy provides this automatically.

---

# 6. p-adic normal-function viewpoint

The convolution with `-log(1-x)` is naturally interpreted as adjoining a logarithmic extension.

The logarithm corresponds to a mixed extension, so `R_n` should be viewed as the period sequence of an extension object rather than just a coefficient sequence.

A successful proof would likely need a statement of the form:

- the mixed extension is nontrivial modulo almost every large prime;
- its Frobenius invariant does not vanish;
- the resulting denominator obstruction survives in the coefficient expansion.

This is a plausible motivic route, but no general theorem currently turns nontriviality of a normal function into the required uniform prime-by-prime denominator lower bound.

---

# 7. Apéry interpolation route

Interpolation identities for Apéry numbers can prove congruence relations such as:

\[
b_n\pmod p
\]

being related to lower indices or Frobenius transforms.

Such results are useful for identifying exceptional primes. But the needed theorem requires a uniform bound on the number of exceptional top primes:

\[
\#\{p\in(n/2,n]:p\mid b_{n-p}\}=o(n/\log n).
\]

Current interpolation/congruence technology does not establish this.

A finite computation cannot substitute for this missing uniform estimate.

---

# 8. Li_3 convolution and mixed extension

The `Li_3` convolution is closely related in structure:

- polylogarithms arise from iterated logarithmic extensions;
- Apéry-type differential equations arise from variations of mixed Tate/K3-type objects;
- adjoining logarithms corresponds to moving from pure periods to extensions.

Thus the convolution

\[
B(x)(-\log(1-x))
\]

is not accidental: it is a concrete coefficient realization of a mixed normal-function extension.

However, recognizing this structure does not prove denominator growth. One still needs an arithmetic nonvanishing theorem for the extension modulo primes.

---

# 9. Final conclusion

## Proven

The exact identity

\[
p\mid U_n\iff p\mid b_{n-p}\qquad(n/2<p\le n)
\]

and the radical identity involving `gcd(L_n,U_n)` and `D_n` follow by a one-line top-prime isolation argument.

## Not proved

The required estimate

\[
\log D_n\ge \log L_n-o(n)
\]

is substantially stronger than all standard G-function denominator estimates.

## Precise obstruction

Existing theory controls the **maximum possible denominator growth**. The missing ingredient is a **uniform lower bound preventing p-adic cancellation in a mixed Apéry logarithmic extension**.

A future proof would require a theorem of one of the following forms:

1. a Frobenius/nonvanishing theorem for the Apéry mixed normal function;
2. a large-prime nonvanishing theorem for `b_{n-p} mod p`;
3. a p-adic interpolation theorem with an exceptional-prime density estimate.

No such theorem follows from global nilpotence, local monodromy, or known G-function denominator theory alone.
