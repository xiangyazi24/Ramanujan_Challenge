# Q915 audit: no new closure from the currently proved inputs

## Conclusion

The available Apéry-specific inputs do not currently yield a new propagation/repulsion lemma. The correct scoped conclusion is option (3): the weakest additional arithmetic statement needed for closure can be isolated, but it is not proved by the existing recurrence, companion denominator factorization, or polylogarithmic exceptional-set theorem.

The failed step is not in the exceptional theorem. The gap is that a bad index supplies many large prime divisors of a single row value, but no proved mechanism forces those primes to create enough weighted bad indices elsewhere.

## What is known from the bad-index hypothesis

For a bad index n, the hypotheses give

\[
\sum_{p\in S(n)}\log p \gg_ε n,
\]

where

\[
|S(n)|=\Omega_ε(n/\log n),\qquad p>\sqrt n,
\]

and each p satisfies the folded-index condition

\[
p\mid b_{n\bmod p}.
\]

The congruence descent also gives

\[
p\mid b_n,
\]

and via the companion relation the corresponding denominator divisibility information for \(L_n^3a_n\).

However, these are local statements at the same n. They do not imply that any of the indices

\[
r_p=n\bmod p
\]

or nearby transformed indices are themselves exceptional.

## Attempted propagation through the recurrence

The recurrence relates neighboring values:

\[
(n+1)^3u_{n+1}=P(n)u_n-n^3u_{n-1}.
\]

A prime dividing b_n does not force a prime dividing b_{n+1} or b_{n-1}; the coefficients are not invertible constraints in the required direction. The same issue remains after using the companion sequence and Casoratian: the identity controls a linear combination of a and b, but does not turn one large-prime divisor set into another large-prime divisor set.

Thus recurrence alone does not provide the missing repulsion weight.

## Attempted use of companion denominators

The factorization input gives restrictions on primes dividing the reduced companion denominator. It can prune possible companion blocks, but it does not prove that a large set

\[
\{p>\sqrt n:p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\}
\]

creates a second bad index.

The missing implication would need a statement of the form:

For every bad n and every sufficiently large subset of its forced primes, there exists a collection T(n) of new indices with

\[
\sum_{m\in T(n)}\frac{1}{m}\quad\text{or}\quad
\sum_{m\in T(n)}\log G_m
\]

large enough to contradict the finite harmonic weight or the \(O((\log N)^2)\) exceptional count.

No such statement follows from the current companion factorization.

## Countermodel status

The old abstract prefix-lcm/antiadjacency countermodel is insufficient here because it does not preserve Apéry recurrence. But no stronger Apéry-preserving countermodel is needed for the present conclusion: the missing implication has simply not been established.

The correct statement is therefore not that an Apéry counterexample exists, but that the currently proved lemmas leave the propagation implication unproved.

## Minimal extra arithmetic statement that would close the theorem

A sufficient additional theorem is:

**Large-radical repulsion lemma.** There exists c>0 such that every sufficiently large n with

\[
\log G_n>\epsilon n
\]

produces a set of distinct indices M(n), disjoint after bounded overlap, satisfying

\[
\sum_{m\in M(n)}\log G_m\ge c n.
\]

Together with

\[
\sum_{m\le N}\log G_m=O((\log N)^2),
\]

this immediately contradicts infinitely many bad indices.

A weaker but still sufficient version is a guaranteed new-index harmonic weight lower bound exceeding the finite harmonic budget.
