# Q7162 (rc4): Apéry four-zero window attack

## Conclusion

The proposed implication

> every consecutive actual four-zero window of span \(s\le \sqrt p\) has an adjacent pair of zeros summing to \(p-1\)

does **not** follow from the currently available continuant/reflection identities. The missing step is a genuine arithmetic lemma about the location of zeros, not a formal consequence of the transfer algebra. The narrow correct statement is a projective-orbit statement: a short chain forces a relation between projective states under the recurrence, but it does not force one zero to be the reflection partner of another.

The obstruction is that the reflection involution acts on the full period, while a short chain only gives local annihilation of continuants. A separated off-center chain can satisfy all local continuant equations without any reflected endpoint.

## 1. What the identities actually give

Let

\[
\pi_n=[b_n:c_n],
\]

and let \(N_h(x)\) denote the continuant controlling a return after a gap of length \(h\). The anchor identity is

\[
N_{r+1}(-1)=(r!)^3 b_r.
\]

Hence an actual zero \(b_r=0\) gives

\[
N_{r+1}(-1)=0.
\]

Reflection gives

\[
b_{p-1-r}=b_r.
\]

Therefore every actual zero has a reflected zero. This is a global symmetry statement:

\[
r\mapsto p-1-r.
\]

It does not say that a second nearby zero is the reflection of the first.

For consecutive zeros

\[
r, r+a, r+a+b, r+a+b+c,
\]

we only know

\[
N_{r+1}(-1)=N_{r+a+1}(-1)=N_{r+a+b+1}(-1)=N_{r+s+1}(-1)=0.
\]

These are four separate continuant vanishings.

## 2. The tempting sign/order argument fails

A possible argument would be:

1. reflection places zeros symmetrically;
2. a short interval cannot contain two unrelated reflected copies;
3. therefore one gap must cross the reflection center.

Step 2 is false.

The reflection partner of a zero at \(r\) is at

\[
r'=p-1-r,
\]

with separation

\[
|r'-r|=|p-1-2r|.
\]

For an off-center chain this separation is typically \(\Theta(p)\), much larger than \(\sqrt p\). The existence of a reflected zero outside the short interval imposes no ordering constraint on the local four-zero pattern.

The computed phantom projective fibers already exhibit the exact failure mode: the transfer equations admit short chains whose local projective state agrees but whose zeros have no reflection adjacency.

The p=3727 off-center long-window examples are consistent with this: the issue is not lack of reflection, but that reflection occurs far away.

## 3. Addition law audit

The exact addition identity

\[
N_{x+1+h}(-1)=N_{x+2}(-1)N_h(x)
 -(x+1)^6N_{x+1}(-1)N_{h-1}(x+1)
\]

looks promising because it decomposes a long return.

However, applying it to a separated pair requires the unknown intermediate factors.

Suppose two chains start at \(r\) and \(r+L\), with

\[
L\asymp p.
\]

The identity expresses the long return through terms such as

\[
N_L(r),\quad N_{L-1}(r+1).
\]

The two short chains only provide vanishing of continuants with lengths at most \(H=\sqrt p\). There is no algebraic elimination of the long continuants unless one proves an additional low-degree resultant/content theorem. No such theorem follows from the existing recurrence identities.

Equivalently, the transfer matrix factorization is

\[
T(r+L,r)=T(r+L,r+H)T(r+H,r),
\]

and the short chain controls only the last factor. The middle product retains \(\Theta(p)\) degrees of freedom.

## 4. Exact obstruction

The missing implication would require a statement of the form:

\[
N_{a}(r)=N_b(r+a)=N_c(r+a+b)=0
\implies
r+a+b+c=p-1-r'\quad\text{for some nearby zero }r'.
\]

But the recurrence has order two, and a projective state determines future evolution only after fixing the state, not the location of the previous zero. Two different locations can share the same projective state.

Thus the actual invariant is

\[
\pi_r=\pi_{r'}
\]

(or a scalar multiple relation), not

\[
r+r'=p-1.
\]

The latter is an extra arithmetic statement.

## 5. What can be proved next

The strongest currently justified lemma is:

**Local projective return lemma.** If

\[
b_r=b_{r+a}=b_{r+a+b}=b_{r+s}=0,
\]

then the transfer matrices of the three gaps annihilate the same projective coordinate:

\[
T(r+s,r)([0:1])=[0:1],
\]

and each gap corresponds to a vanishing continuant.

To obtain the desired adjacent-reflection conclusion, one needs one additional ingredient:

**Missing lemma (sufficient form).** For primitive short returns with \(s\le\sqrt p\), any second return to the same projective zero state must occur either within the same short interval or at the reflection location \(p-1-r\).

This is a rank-one/projective recurrence rigidity theorem. It is not contained in reflection or the addition law.

## 6. Consequence for P32

The main theorem route cannot currently replace the variance estimate by a deterministic reflection pairing argument. The weak-DPLS proof still needs control of separated pairs.

The residual term is therefore exactly:

\[
\sum_{X<p\le2X}\sum_q
\sum_{\substack{\text{two primitive chains}\
\text{same }q\\
\text{distance }L>2H}}
1,
\]

with \(H=\sqrt p\).

Overlap pairs may be attacked by bounded-span algebra; separated pairs require a different averaging mechanism (large sieve, energy estimate, or a new rigidity theorem).

No proof of the adjacent-pair reflection claim exists from the current continuant toolkit alone.
