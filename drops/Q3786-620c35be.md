## 2. Averaging over `h`: the exact endpoint is zero-pair energy

Write

\[
Z_p:=\{0\le j<p:p\mid b_j\},
\qquad z_p:=|Z_p|,
\]

and define the positive-gap correlation

\[
R_p(h)
:=\#\{x:0\le x<x+h<p,\ x,x+h\in Z_p\}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

There is an exact identity

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{h=1}^{p-1}R_p(h)=\binom{z_p}{2}.}
\tag{2.2}
\]

The two orientations merely decide which endpoint of the pair is the primary digit. Therefore, after forgetting the `q`-zero condition, every far-minus edge is injected into an oriented pair of distinct `p`-zeros, with at most two orientations. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{q}|X^-_{p,q}|
\le 2\binom{z_p}{2}.}
\tag{2.3}
\]

More precisely, if `z_p^{row}` counts only the lower primary residues admitted by the shell, then

\[
\sum_q|X^-_{p,q}|
\le 2z_p^{row}z_p.
\tag{2.4}
\]

This is the correct form for combining with the quotient-six first moment.

The far cutoff gives no deterministic factor. For an arbitrary set `A` in an interval, all pairs may have distance greater than `H_0`: take two sparse blocks separated by more than `H_0`, or a single sparse set with mutual spacings greater than `H_0`. Consequently there is no inequality of the form

\[
\sum_{h>H_0}R_p(h)
\le o(1)\,z_p^2
\]

from spacing, reflection, or the `2/3` window law alone.

Using the banked estimates

\[
\sum_{p\asymp T}z_p^{row}=H_6(T)
\ll T^{8/5}(\log T)^{-4/5},
\]

\[
\max_{p\asymp T}z_p\ll T^{2/3},
\]

(2.4) gives

\[
\mathcal F^-_{\rm far}(T)
\ll T^{2/3}H_6(T)
\ll T^{34/15}(\log T)^{-4/5}.
\tag{2.5}
\]

The logarithmically weighted version is

\[
\sum_{p,q}|X^-_{p,q}|\log p
\ll T^{34/15}(\log T)^{1/5}.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Neither exponent is useful for the desired `T^2`-scale deletion. The point is not a poor application of Cauchy--Schwarz: identity (2.2) shows that `h`-averaging has reached the exact support-level endpoint.

## 3. The bilinear and large-sieve routes do not gain

For the branch `7d>p`, for example,

\[

\qquad h=7q-8p.
\tag{3.1}

The other orientation has the same shape with the affine reflection endpoint changed.

### 3.1 Fourier expansion gives only Parseval

Put

\[
c_{p,h}(x):=1_{Z_p}(x)1_{Z_p}(x+h).
\]

Expanding the `q`-indicator gives

\[
|X^-_{p,q}|
=
\frac1q\sum_{a\bmod q}
\widehat{1_{Z_q}}(a)e_q(a(w-1))
\sum_xc_{p,h}(x)e_q(-ax).
\tag{3.2}
\]

Cauchy and Parseval yield exactly

\[
|X^-_{p,q}|
\le \sqrt{z_qR_p(h)}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

This is weaker than the elementary bound

\[
|X^-_{p,q}|\le\min\{z_q,R_p(h)\}.
\]

There is no hidden large-sieve average in (3.2), because as `q` varies the gap

\[
h=\pm(7q-8p)
\]

varies, so the coefficient sequence `c_{p,h}` varies with the modulus. The standard additive large sieve applies to one fixed coefficient sequence; applying it separately for each `q` is merely Parseval again.

One may instead regard `(x,x+h)` as a two-dimensional coefficient array. Its natural length is `\asymp T^2`, while the moduli are `\asymp T`; the large-sieve constant is then already `\asymp T^2`. This again gives no power saving.

### 3.2 Sieve on the two affine prime forms stops at the ambient scale

For fixed `h`, (1.10) or (1.12) asks that two affine forms in `p` be prime. An upper-bound Selberg sieve gives, after the congruence restrictions are imposed,

\[
\#\{p\asymp T:p\text{ and }(8p\pm h)/7\text{ prime}\}
\ll \mathfrak S(h)\frac{T}{(\log T)^2}.
\tag{3.4}
\]

Averaging the ordinary singular series over `h\ll T` gives only

\[
\ll \frac{T^2}{(\log T)^2}
\tag{3.5}
\]

prime-form pairs. This is exactly the scale one is trying to beat, even before the `x`-multiplicity is inserted. Removing `h\le H_0=o(T)` changes no exponent.

The saturation construction in Section 5 realizes `\gg T^2/(log T)^2` nonempty far-minus sets on actual prime pairs, so the ambient sieve estimate (3.5) is sharp for this mechanism.

## 4. The resultant routes fail for three separate exact reasons

### 4.1 `b_x` is not a bounded-degree polynomial in `x`

For a fixed prime `p`, one can interpolate the digit function by the unique polynomial

\[
B_p(X)\in\mathbb F_p[X],
\qquad \deg B_p<p,
\qquad B_p(x)=b_x\quad(0\le x<p).
\]

Then

\[
p\mid b_x,N_h(x)
\]

is a common-root condition for `B_p` and `N_h` modulo `p`. But `B_p` changes with `p` and has degree `p-1`. Thus

\[
\operatorname{Res}_X(B_p,N_h)
\]

is a `p`-specific object of degree product `\asymp ph`; it is not one prescribed characteristic-zero integer over which `p` can be averaged. Any naive integer lift has resultant-height budget of order at least the generic `ph` coefficient scale, i.e. `\Theta(T^2)` when `p,h\asymp T`, before summing over `h`.

This is not a technical choice of interpolation basis. A bounded-degree polynomial representing all `b_x` on a full `p`-digit interval would itself be a new theorem contradicting the current recurrence complexity.

### 4.2 Pairwise continuant resultants see only repeated `h` at one `x`

If the same `x` is a root of both `N_h` and `N_k` modulo `p`, then `p` divides

\[
\operatorname{Res}_X(N_h,N_k).
\]

But a single far-minus edge supplies only one gap `h`. Singleton gaps, which are enough to saturate the aggregate, never enter such a resultant.

Even on the repeated-gap stratum, the banked height is

\[
\log|\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_k)|
=O\bigl(hk\log(h+k)\bigr).
\tag{4.1}
\]

For `h,k\asymp T`, division by `\log p\asymp\log T` permits `O(T^2)` large-prime occurrences for one pair `(h,k)`, whereas there are only `O(T)` possible roots to begin with. The resultant bound is therefore vacuous in precisely the far range. The cutoff makes the resultant larger, not smaller.

### 4.3 A one-characteristic resultant cannot encode the `q`-zero

The assertion

\[
q\mid b_{w-1-x},
\qquad q\ne p,
\]

lives in a different localization. Over `\mathbb Z_{(p)}`, the integer `q` is a unit, so introducing a witness for the quotient `b_{w-1-x}/q` can be eliminated by a unit pivot. Consequently the bare existence of the `q`-quotient contributes no `p`-primary resultant or Fitting factor.

To couple the two conditions, one would need a new coefficient identity whose surviving coefficients acquire actual `p`-divisibility from the `q`-selectedness. None of the ordinary continuant, Dodgson, adjacent-resultant, or separated-resultant identities does this; they are same-characteristic identities.

A crude scalar alternative also fails. For fixed `(x,h)`, the product of lower primes satisfying

\[
p\mid\gcd(b_x,N_h(x))
\]

has logarithm at most

\[
\log b_x=O(x).
\]

Thus the number of such primes `p\asymp T` is only bounded by `O(x/log T)`. Summing over `x,h\ll T` gives `O(T^3/log T)`, far above the target. Adding the primality of the affine `q`-form does not improve this deterministically.

## 6. Exact scope of the no-go

Theorem 5.1 deliberately preserves the inputs consumed by the proposed mechanisms:

- actual primes in the two affine forms;
- exact legal q6 far-minus coordinates;
- reflection and no adjacency;
- a `2/3` window bound;
- pointwise and aggregate zero-count bounds stronger than the banked ones;
- Gessel-Lucas compatibility on the finite range;
- gap-polynomial degree `3h-3`, reflection parity, squarefreeness, and nonzero resultants.

It does **not** preserve the full Apéry recurrence or its exact pole-value and adjacent-resultant formulas. Therefore it does not disprove an Apéry-specific theorem. It proves the narrower statement requested here:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the far cutoff plus the two linear prime forms do not create
bilinear, large-sieve, or generic-resultant saving.}
}
\]

The ordinary large sieve cannot distinguish the countermodel from the actual support data, and generic resultant arguments see polynomials with all the same degree/reflection/nonvanishing properties. The exact Apéry recurrence is the only remaining place where a successful argument could obtain information not present in this countermodel.

## Conclusion

No new useful aggregate exponent is proved for the actual far-minus sets. The exact support-level envelope is (V1), and the shell-scale countermodel (5.7) shows that the three proposed mechanisms cannot improve it to the required little-`o` statement merely by exploiting `h>T^{1/2}/\omega` and the two affine prime forms.

Accordingly the result of this attack is a **precise mechanism no-go**, not a reformulation of the far-minus problem.
