# P3.2 — strategy for proving the cross-slope determinant is a p-unit

## Bottom line

The most promising route is **not** an equidistribution theorem and not a theorem saying that three Dwork coordinates are generically independent. For an all-primes statement, the right target is an **exact determinant-line / Casoratian identity**.

The cross-slope quantity is a determinant built from two linear charge operators evaluated on a fundamental pair of solutions. Therefore the first thing to try is to forget the special basis `(b,c)` entirely and compute the **wedge of the two charge functionals**. If the normalized slope-7 and slope-4 charges are honest linear functionals on the same two-dimensional recurrence module, then

\[
\Delta_{7,4}(b,c)
=
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
 C_7(b)&C_7(c)\\
 p^3 C_4(b)&p^3 C_4(c)
\end{pmatrix}
\]

must equal

\[
U_{p,j}\,W_j(b,c),
\]

where `U_{p,j}` depends only on the two charge rows and on the recurrence transfer matrices, while `W_j(b,c)` is the Casoratian. Since `j<p`, the Casoratian is automatically a p-unit. Thus the entire question becomes:

> **Can one prove that the normalized row-wedge `U_{p,j}` is a p-unit?**

This is a much better target than proving directly that

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j\ne0\pmod p.
\]

Indeed, if Q3522 is correct, then the latter expression is just one coordinate expression for the same basis-invariant wedge. An exact wedge calculation would explain *why* the apparently unrelated invariants `eta`, `g_j`, and `c_j` cannot cancel.

My ranking of the proposed approaches is therefore:

1. **Adjoint transfer / determinant-line / lifted Casoratian:** strongest and genuinely capable of proving an all-p theorem.
2. **Dwork-Katz crystal:** useful only after identifying the determinant with a canonical Frobenius determinant/minor or pairing; by itself it does not give nonvanishing of an arbitrary affine combination of first-jet coordinates.
3. **Modular form at `(p-1)/2`:** valuable as a special-fiber check or to eliminate a residual exceptional factor, but it does not propagate to general `j` in any known direct way.
4. **Equidistribution:** cannot prove the desired all-p statement, and the heuristic in the question has a basic issue: `sum_p 1/p` diverges.

The rest of this note makes the determinant-line route precise.

## 1. Put the recurrence in a two-dimensional transfer module

Write

\[
(n+1)^3 y_{n+1}=P(n)y_n-n^3y_{n-1},
\qquad
P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

For `n>=1`, use the state

\[
Y_n=\binom{y_n}{y_{n-1}},
\]

so that

\[
Y_{n+1}=T_nY_n,
\qquad
T_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{P(n)}{(n+1)^3}&-\dfrac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Hence

\[
\det T_n=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}.
\]

For two solutions `u,v`, the Casoratian therefore satisfies

\[
W_n(u,v):=u_nv_{n+1}-u_{n+1}v_n,
\qquad
W_n=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}W_{n-1}.
\]

For the Apéry/companion basis `(b,c)`, this gives an explicit rational Casoratian. With the usual initial data `b_0=1,b_1=5,c_0=0,c_1=1`, one obtains

\[
b_jc_{j+1}-b_{j+1}c_j=\frac1{(j+1)^3}.
\]

Your notes use the shifted convention `1/j^3`; nothing below depends on this indexing choice. The only fact needed is:

\[
0<j<p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad W_j(b,c)\in\mathbf Z_p^\times.
\]

At a target zero `p|b_j`, reduction of the Casoratian immediately gives

\[
b_{j+1}c_j\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

So both `b_{j+1}` and `c_j` are p-units. This is all the *ordinary* Casoratian gives for free.

## 2. The key structural observation: charge determinants are wedges

Let `S` be the two-dimensional solution space of the recurrence over `Q_p` on the regular interval relevant to the target. Suppose the two normalized charges are linear maps

\[
\Lambda_7:S\to\mathbf Q_p,
\qquad
\widetilde\Lambda_4:=p^3\Lambda_4:S\to\mathbf Q_p.
\]

After transporting both to the same state index `r`, write

\[
\Lambda_7(y)=\lambda_7Y_r,
\qquad
\widetilde\Lambda_4(y)=\lambda_4Y_r
\]

for two row vectors `lambda_7,lambda_4`.

Let

\[
\Phi_r=
\begin{pmatrix}
 b_r&c_r\\
 b_{r-1}&c_{r-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then exactly

\[
\begin{pmatrix}
\Lambda_7(b)&\Lambda_7(c)\\
\widetilde\Lambda_4(b)&\widetilde\Lambda_4(c)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_7\\
\lambda_4
\end{pmatrix}
\Phi_r.
\]

Taking determinants gives

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_{7,4}(b,c)
=
\det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_7\\\lambda_4\end{pmatrix}
\det\Phi_r.
}
\]

This is the central identity.

The second factor is the Casoratian and is a p-unit for `r<p`. Therefore

\[
 v_p(\Delta_{7,4})
 =v_p\!\left(\det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_7\\\lambda_4\end{pmatrix}\right).
\]

Most importantly, the right side is **independent of the choice of fundamental basis**. It cannot intrinsically depend on `eta`, `g_j`, or `c_j` separately.

Thus if Q3522 gives, on the valuation-one zero locus,

\[
\Delta_{7,4}
\equiv
\eta-2g_j-5c_j\pmod p,
\]

then there must be a hidden exact identity of the form

\[
\boxed{
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
\equiv U_{p,j}\,W_j(b,c)\pmod p,
}
\]

where `U_{p,j}` is obtained from the row wedge alone.

That is the identity I would try to prove next.

## 3. The sharp theorem to aim for

A theorem strong enough to close the question would be:

> **Normalized cross-slope wedge theorem.**  For every core prime `p>5` and `0<j<p`, let `Lambda_7^{p,j}` and `Lambda_4^{p,j}` be the exact slope-7 and slope-4 first-nonlegal mass-two charge functionals, with the slope-4 functional normalized by the exact singular factor `p^3`. Then there exists an explicit `U(p,j) in Z_(p)` such that for every fundamental pair of recurrence solutions `(u,v)`,
> \[
> \det
> \begin{pmatrix}
> \Lambda_7(u)&\Lambda_7(v)\\
> p^3\Lambda_4(u)&p^3\Lambda_4(v)
> \end{pmatrix}
> =U(p,j)\,W_j(u,v),
> \]
> and `U(p,j)` is a p-unit.

Once this is proved, Q3522 immediately yields

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j\ne0\pmod p
\]

for every valuation-one target.

This theorem is also the right conceptual statement: it says that the two charge rows form a p-adic dual basis up to a unit.

## 4. How to compute `U(p,j)` without computing `b` or `c`

Use **adjoint row transport**.

If a terminal charge is represented by a row `lambda_{n+1}` at state `Y_{n+1}`, then at state `Y_n` it is represented by

\[
\lambda_n=\lambda_{n+1}T_n.
\]

Therefore both charge rows can be transported to a common index by multiplying only explicit `2x2` rational matrices. Their wedge transforms by determinants:

\[
(\lambda T_n)\wedge(\mu T_n)
=(\det T_n)(\lambda\wedge\mu).
\]

So along any regular segment the wedge propagation is completely explicit:

\[
\lambda_a\wedge\mu_a
=
\left(\prod_{n=a}^{b}\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\right)
(\lambda_{b+1}\wedge\mu_{b+1}).
\]

The product telescopes.

The only delicate locations are the singular crossings where `(n+1)^3` contributes a power of `p`. But this is exactly why the charge was normalized by `p^3`. Rather than reducing modulo `p` prematurely, isolate the singular transfer matrix, clear its exact denominator, and compute the wedge over `Z_p`.

So the concrete calculation should be:

1. Write the *pre-division* slope-7 and slope-4 charge numerators as linear rows on their native endpoint states.
2. Multiply the slope-4 row by the exact `p^3` normalization before any reduction.
3. Adjoint-transport both rows to the same regular state, preferably `(y_j,y_{j+1})` or `(y_j,y_{j-1})`.
4. Take their `2x2` determinant symbolically.
5. Factor the result in `Z[p,j]` or `Z_(p)[j]`.
6. Reduce only the final factorization modulo `p`.

If the result is, for example,

\[
U(p,j)=\pm\frac{A(p,j)}{B(p,j)}
\]

with `B(p,j)` visibly a p-unit for `0<j<p`, then the problem is reduced to proving `A(p,j) not congruent 0 mod p`.

I would expect one of two outcomes:

- **Best outcome:** `A(p,j)` is itself a product of obvious unit factors such as powers of `j`, `j+1`, or fixed small constants. Then the theorem is finished.
- **Second-best outcome:** `A(0,j)` has a small fixed-degree exceptional factor. Then classify its roots and show that none can simultaneously satisfy the Apéry-zero condition. That is still vastly easier than controlling `eta,g_j,c_j` statistically.

This calculation should be done on the **full determinant**, before using the target condition `b_j=0`. The factorization

\[
\Delta_{7,4}\equiv C_7(b)\,p^3C_4(c)\pmod p
\]

is useful after the determinant-line identity is known, but expanding in the special basis too early hides the wedge structure.

## 5. Important caveat: quotient-defined charges

There is one possible obstruction to the naïve wedge argument.

If `C_7` or `p^3 C_4` is defined only **after dividing by a quantity known to vanish on the target locus**, then it may not literally be a globally defined linear functional on the whole solution module.

If that happens, do not abandon the determinant-line method. Instead:

- return to the integral numerator charges before division;
- form their exterior determinant there;
- factor out the common forced powers of `p` at the determinant level;
- only then specialize to `b_j congruent 0 mod p` and valuation one.

The invariant object should be the saturated exterior product of the two numerator rows. In a DVR this is exactly the right way to avoid basis-dependent artifacts from taking quotients one coordinate at a time.

So the first technical audit for Q3537 should be:

> Are `C_7` and `p^3 C_4` honest linear rows on the same rank-two lattice, or only target-locus quotients?

Either answer still leaves a determinant-line route, but the second requires saturation before reduction.

## 6. Why the ordinary companion Casoratian does not by itself prove the target

At `p|b_j`, the exact Casoratian gives

\[
p\eta\,c_{j+1}-b_{j+1}c_j=W_j.
\]

Modulo `p`, this proves `c_j` is a unit. It does **not** constrain the first-order Frobenius/index jet `g_j`.

This is not just a technical gap. The current repository already records a closely related no-go in `problems/3.2/AVENUES.md`: the companion marking is basis-covariant, and at a distinguished return the marked row is merely a scalar multiple of the fixed functional `L(x,y)=y-5x`; differentiation in the mark adds the simple-root statement but no new independent return equation. In other words, zeroth-order companion data alone do not manufacture a new arithmetic constraint.

For Q3537 the useful object is therefore not the single Casoratian, but a **shifted/lifted Casoratian** or a discrete Green identity that sees the first jet.

## 7. A concrete first-jet equation for `g_r`

There is an elementary route to the needed lifted identity.

Define, for `0<=r<p` in the range where the Dwork first-jet expansion holds,

\[
b_{p+r}=5b_r+p g_r\pmod{p^2}.
\]

Insert this into the Apéry recurrence at index `p+r` and expand the polynomial coefficients to first order in `p`:

\[
P(p+r)=P(r)+pP'(r)\pmod{p^2},
\]

\[
(p+r)^3=r^3+3pr^2\pmod{p^2},
\]

\[
(p+r+1)^3=(r+1)^3+3p(r+1)^2\pmod{p^2}.
\]

After cancelling `5` times the original recurrence for `b_r`, one gets the exact mod-p inhomogeneous equation

\[
\boxed{
(r+1)^3g_{r+1}-P(r)g_r+r^3g_{r-1}
=
5P'(r)b_r
-15r^2b_{r-1}
-15(r+1)^2b_{r+1}.
}
\]

Let

\[
(Ly)_r=(r+1)^3y_{r+1}-P(r)y_r+r^3y_{r-1}.
\]

Then `Lb=0`, `Lc=0`, and

\[
Lg=F,
\]

where

\[
F_r=5P'(r)b_r-15r^2b_{r-1}-15(r+1)^2b_{r+1}.
\]

The recurrence operator is a self-adjoint Jacobi-type difference operator because the backward coefficient `r^3` is exactly the previous forward coefficient. Consequently one has the discrete Lagrange identity

\[
 u_r(Lv)_r-v_r(Lu)_r
=(r+1)^3W_r(u,v)-r^3W_{r-1}(u,v).
\]

Taking `u=c`, `v=g` gives

\[
 c_rF_r
=(r+1)^3W_r(c,g)-r^3W_{r-1}(c,g).
\]

Summing from `a` to `b` telescopes:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{r=a}^{b}c_rF_r
=(b+1)^3W_b(c,g)-a^3W_{a-1}(c,g).
}
\]

This is exactly the sort of identity that can turn `g_j` into a boundary term plus an explicit Green sum.

The next move is to combine this with the **p-adic lift of the ordinary Casoratian at the zero**

\[
p\eta\,c_{j+1}-b_{j+1}c_j=W_j,
\]

and with the endpoint identities defining the slope-7 and slope-4 first nonlegal charges. The coefficients `-2` and `-5` in

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
\]

are sufficiently structured that I would actively look for them as the residual boundary coefficient after this telescoping.

This gives a very concrete fallback if the direct charge-row wedge is awkward to extract from Q3522.

### What a successful Green computation should look like

The ideal endpoint is a formula of the shape

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
=R(j)c_j
\quad\text{or}\quad
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
=\frac{R(j)}{b_{j+1}}
\pmod p,
\]

with `R(j)` an explicit rational function whose denominator is a p-unit and whose numerator never vanishes for an Apéry zero.

Because `c_j` and `b_{j+1}` are already units by the ordinary Casoratian, either formula would finish the proof.

Conceptually, this is the same determinant-line mechanism as the row-wedge argument: the discrete Green identity is the coordinate version of adjoint exterior transport.

## 8. What Dwork-Katz does and does not give

There is genuine relevant theory here, but it must be used at the correct level.

Stienstra-Beukers connect the Apéry Picard-Fuchs equation with the formal Brauer group of an elliptic K3 family, and Katz's 1985 theorem reconstructs unit-root `F`-crystals from expansion coefficients under suitable Hasse-Witt hypotheses. Modern Beukers-Vlasenko Dwork-crystal theory similarly gives explicit Frobenius and connection matrices from coefficient data and proves matrix congruences.

What these results naturally control are objects such as:

- invertibility of the Hasse-Witt matrix;
- existence and integrality of the unit-root subcrystal;
- Frobenius matrices modulo powers of `p`;
- determinants/eigenvalues of Frobenius on canonical subquotients;
- compatibility of Frobenius with the connection.

They do **not** imply that an arbitrary linear form in three coordinates of a chosen trivialization is nonzero.

In rank one, the Frobenius eigenvalue on the unit-root line is a unit by definition, but an off-diagonal extension coordinate may perfectly well vanish. In higher rank, a matrix can be invertible while a specified entry, or a specified affine combination of entries, is zero.

Therefore there is no off-the-shelf “Dwork-Katz theorem” of the form

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j\ne0.
\]

### How Dwork-Katz *could* prove it

The theory becomes decisive if one can identify the target with a **canonical determinant or pairing**. For example, if one proves

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
=
\langle F(e_1),e_2\rangle
\]

where the right side is a polarization pairing known to be a unit, or

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
=
\det(F|M_{\mathrm{unit}})
\]

after an exact normalization, then the crystal structure gives the unit theorem.

So the correct Dwork question is not “are `eta` and `g_j` constrained?” but rather:

> **Is the cross-slope wedge the reduction of the determinant line of a canonical Frobenius/connection subquotient?**

That is a plausible geometric reinterpretation of the elementary row-wedge theorem above.

If the elementary transfer computation already proves the wedge is a unit, invoking the full K3 crystal is unnecessary. If the transfer computation leaves an unexplained unit factor, the crystal may explain it conceptually.

References most relevant to this formulation:

- J. Stienstra and F. Beukers, *On the Picard-Fuchs Equation and the Formal Brauer Group of Certain Elliptic K3-Surfaces*, Math. Ann. 271 (1985), 269-304.
- N. Katz, *Internal reconstruction of unit-root F-crystals via expansion-coefficients*, Ann. Sci. ENS 18 (1985), 245-285, DOI `10.24033/asens.1490`.
- F. Beukers and M. Vlasenko, *Dwork Crystals I*, IMRN 2021, 8807-8844, DOI `10.1093/imrn/rnaa119`.

## 9. The modular-form route is special to the half-index

For the zeta(3) Apéry numbers `A(n)`, Ahlgren-Ono proved the Beukers supercongruence

\[
A\!\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)
\equiv a(p)\pmod{p^2},
\]

where

\[
\eta(2\tau)^4\eta(4\tau)^4
=\sum_{n\ge1}a(n)q^n
\]

is the normalized weight-4 newform of level 8.

Thus at the half-index, if `p|A((p-1)/2)`, one indeed gets a modular interpretation of the Bockstein

\[
\eta
=\frac{A((p-1)/2)}p
\equiv\frac{a(p)}p\pmod p.
\]

But this does not propagate to general `j`.

The half-index is special because `(p-1)/2` converts the truncated hypergeometric expression into one with quadratic-character parameters over `F_p`; this is what allows the Gaussian hypergeometric/Frobenius-trace interpretation. A general `j` is not the `p`th Fourier coefficient of a fixed modular form, and there is no Hecke relation moving from the half-index to arbitrary `j`.

To use modularity globally one would need a much stronger construction: a family of motives or finite-field hypergeometric sheaves parameterized by `j` for which `eta`, `g_j`, and the companion coordinate appear as canonical Frobenius data. That is possible in principle, but it is much more elaborate than the transfer-wedge calculation and still would not automatically give nonvanishing of a specific matrix entry.

So I would use the modular form only in two ways:

1. as a stringent check on any proposed exact formula for `U(p,j)` at `j=(p-1)/2`;
2. possibly to eliminate a residual exceptional factor if the row-wedge computation leaves exactly the half-index as a special case.

Relevant reference:

- S. Ahlgren and K. Ono, *A Gaussian hypergeometric series evaluation and Apéry number congruences*, J. Reine Angew. Math. 518 (2000), 187-212, DOI `10.1515/crll.2000.004`.

## 10. Equidistribution cannot prove the desired statement

There are two separate problems.

### 10.1 The heuristic sum does not converge

If, conditional on being a valuation-one Apéry zero, the three residues `eta,g_j,c_j` behaved like independent uniform elements of `F_p`, then

\[
\Pr(\eta-2g_j-5c_j=0)\approx\frac1p.
\]

But

\[
\sum_{p}\frac1p=\infty.
\]

So even the naïve independent model does **not** predict only finitely many failures. If there are `O(1)` relevant target zeros for a positive proportion of primes, the model suggests a very slowly divergent expected number of exceptions.

To obtain a convergent Borel-Cantelli heuristic one would need failure probability roughly `O(1/p^{1+epsilon})`, for example `1/p^2`, not `1/p`.

This makes the current computational fact `0/46` perfectly compatible with both possibilities: a structural no-failure theorem, or extremely sparse eventual failures.

### 10.2 Rigorous equidistribution is the wrong strength anyway

Even a strong Sato-Tate/Chebotarev-type theorem would normally prove a density statement. The modulus here varies with the residue characteristic and the event is an exact mod-p cancellation involving a first Bockstein and an index jet. Proving an all-primes exclusion would require much more than ordinary equidistribution.

In fact, if one truly believed the three coordinates were generic independent uniforms, that heuristic should make one *less* confident in an all-p theorem. The experimental absence of failures is evidence for a hidden algebraic identity, not for generic randomness.

## 11. Why the computation 46/46 is theoretically informative

The observed unit determinant for every tested valuation-one zero is much more suggestive when viewed through the wedge lens.

A random affine linear combination would have occasional zeros. A determinant of two canonically transported rows, on the other hand, is exactly the sort of quantity that can be protected by:

- a fixed Casoratian;
- an adjoint conservation law;
- a determinant line;
- a polarization;
- a saturated lattice index.

So the empirical pattern points toward **structural transversality**, not probabilistic genericity.

I would therefore stop increasing the prime bound once the symbolic row-wedge computation is available. The next useful computation is not `p<50000`; it is an exact symbolic determinant of the two normalized charge rows.

## 12. Recommended proof program

### Phase A — basis-free exact algebra

1. Recover from Q3522 the exact, pre-reduction definitions of the first nonlegal mass-two charges on slopes 7 and 4.
2. Verify linearity in an arbitrary recurrence solution `y`.
3. Introduce the normalized slope-4 row `p^3 C_4` before specialization.
4. Transport both charge rows to the same regular state using the explicit adjoint transfer matrices.
5. Compute their exterior determinant `U(p,j)` exactly.
6. Factor `U(p,j)` and prove it is a p-unit for `0<j<p`, or classify its mod-p exceptional locus.

This phase is the highest-priority attack.

### Phase B — if the charge quotient is singular

1. Replace each quotient charge by its integral numerator row.
2. Compute the saturated exterior product in the rank-two dual lattice.
3. Remove the exact forced `p`-power from the *determinant*, not separately from individual coordinates.
4. Re-run Phase A on the saturated determinant.

### Phase C — discrete Green identity

If direct row extraction is messy, use the first-jet equation

\[
Lg=F
\]

and the telescoping Lagrange identity with the companion `c` to derive a boundary formula. Combine it with the lifted Casoratian at `j`. The target is an explicit formula for

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j
\]

as a unit multiple of `c_j`, `1/b_{j+1}`, or the ordinary Casoratian.

### Phase D — crystal interpretation only if needed

If Phase A produces a p-adic unit whose nonvanishing is conceptually opaque, try to identify the row wedge with the determinant of a canonical unit-root/Hodge piece. Then Katz/Stienstra-Beukers/Beukers-Vlasenko become the correct tools.

### Phase E — modular form for exceptional residues only

If the exact algebra leaves a small exceptional `j` locus, use the half-index modular supercongruence where applicable. Do not try to propagate the level-8 newform identity to arbitrary `j` without a new motivic family.

## 13. A falsifiable diagnostic before investing in Dwork geometry

There is a very quick test of the whole strategy.

Take the exact definitions of the two charge operators and evaluate them not only on `(b,c)` but on a symbolic initial state

\[
y_j=X,\qquad y_{j+1}=Y.
\]

Propagate symbolically and write

\[
C_7(y)=A_7(p,j)X+B_7(p,j)Y,
\]

\[
p^3C_4(y)=A_4(p,j)X+B_4(p,j)Y.
\]

Then compute

\[
U(p,j)=A_7B_4-B_7A_4.
\]

If `U(p,j)` reduces to a unit, Q3537 is essentially solved and the Dwork reduction becomes a corollary identity among `eta,g_j,c_j`.

If `U(p,j) identically 0`, then the two charges are intrinsically dependent and the observed determinant must be coming from quotient/saturation subtleties; that tells you immediately to use Phase B.

If `U(p,j)` has a nontrivial mod-p zero locus, then an all-p theorem cannot come from determinant-line formalism alone; one must show Apéry zeros avoid that locus.

This test is therefore much more informative than another large numerical prime sweep.

## 14. Assessment of the four ideas in the question

### (1) Dwork operator

**Useful, but only after a canonical-minor identification.** There is no general Dwork-Katz theorem forbidding

\[
\eta-2g_j-5c_j=0.
\]

The right crystal statement to seek is that the cross-slope determinant is a normalized Frobenius determinant/pairing on a canonical subquotient. If that identification exists, Dwork-Katz can prove the relevant determinant is a unit.

### (2) Companion identity

**The ordinary Casoratian alone is insufficient.** It proves `c_j` is a p-unit and fixes its product with `b_{j+1}` modulo `p`, but does not control the first index jet `g_j`. The promising upgrade is the *lifted/shifted Casoratian plus discrete Green identity*, equivalently the exterior determinant of the two charge rows.

### (3) Modular form

**Strong at the half-index, not a propagation mechanism.** The Ahlgren-Ono congruence is special to `(p-1)/2` and quadratic-character hypergeometric geometry. Use it as a check or exceptional-case tool, not as the primary general-`j` proof.

### (4) Equidistribution

**Not suitable for all-p nonvanishing.** The claimed convergence is false because `sum_p 1/p` diverges. Even a rigorous independence theorem would at best give statistics, and the independent-uniform heuristic actually permits/encourages rare failures.

---

## Final recommendation

The best next theorem is **not** a theorem about the three invariants separately. It is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the saturated exterior product of the slope-7 and normalized slope-4 charge rows is a p-unit.}
}
\]

Prove that directly from the transfer matrices. Then use the exact Casoratian of `(b,c)` to conclude that the cross-slope determinant is a p-unit. The Q3522 identity

\[
\det_{bc}\equiv\eta-2g_j-5c_j\pmod p
\]

should be viewed as a **coordinate shadow** of this basis-free determinant identity, not as three random p-adic quantities whose cancellation has to be excluded probabilistically.

If the direct row-wedge is obstructed by the singular quotient defining `C_4`, compute the saturated numerator wedge and/or derive the same statement through the first-jet Green identity. Only after that should one invoke Dwork-Katz, and then only to identify the surviving wedge with a canonical determinant line.

That is the route with a realistic chance of proving `det_bc != 0 mod p` for **every** core valuation-one target, rather than merely for density one or for all tested primes.
