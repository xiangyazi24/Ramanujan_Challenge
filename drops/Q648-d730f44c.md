# Direct divisor geometry and AFP: where the counting argument fails

## Summary

The divisor-surface viewpoint is useful, but the naive summation over prime divisors of $b_r$ counts the wrong object. The error is exactly that $ω(b_r)$ counts all prime divisors of the integer $b_r$, while AFP counts only prime divisors $p$ satisfying the diagonal constraint

\[
 r<p \quad\text{and}\quad r\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}.
\]

Equivalently, after swapping sums, AFP counts incidences on the region

\[
\{(r,p): p\mid b_r,\ p>r\},
\]
not the full divisor graph of the sequence $(b_r)$.

The missing saving is therefore a **large-prime divisor estimate** for the Apéry sequence, not a bound on the total number of prime factors.

---

# 1. Where the $X^2/\log X$ estimate goes wrong

The identity

\[
\sum_{p\le X} Z_p
=\sum_{r<X}\#\{p\le X:p\mid b_r,\ p>r\}
\]

is correct.

The mistake occurs in replacing the inner term by $\omega(b_r)$.

Indeed,

\[
\#\{p\le X:p\mid b_r,p>r\}
\leq \omega(b_r),
\]

but the upper bound is usually extremely wasteful.

A typical prime divisor counted by $\omega(b_r)$ has no reason to satisfy

\[
 p>r.
\]

The small divisors are exactly the divisors controlled by Lucas/Dwork periodicity. They appear repeatedly as $r$ varies.

For example, for a fixed small prime $p$,

\[
\#\{r<X:p\mid b_r\}
\approx \frac{X}{p}Z_p,
\]

because $b_{qp+s}\equiv b_qb_s\pmod p$.

Thus a small prime can divide many $b_r$, and its contribution must be counted by the vertical fiber $Z_p$, not by charging it separately to every $b_r$.

The correct decomposition is:

\[
\sum_{p\le X}Z_p
=
\sum_{r<X}\omega(b_r,(r,X]).
\]

The interval restriction $(r,X]$ is the essential part.

---

# 2. Can one prove $\omega(b_r,(r,X])=O(1)$?

Not currently. This statement is essentially a large-prime factor theorem for Apéry numbers.

A weaker statement follows from size:

\[
\log b_r\sim r\log(17+12\sqrt2),
\]

so

\[
\omega(b_r,(r,X])\leq \frac{\log b_r}{\log r}
\sim C\frac r{\log r}.
\]

This is far too weak.

The desired AFP-compatible statement would be closer to

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{r<X}\omega(b_r,(r,X])\ll \pi(X)
}
\]

or a dyadic version

\[
\sum_{r<X}\#\{p\in(Y,2Y]:p\mid b_r\}\ll \pi(Y).
\]

This is exactly a cross-modulus anti-concentration statement.

The existing gap-polynomial machinery controls

\[
p\mid b_r,\qquad p\mid b_{r+h}
\]

for the same prime $p$.

It does not control a large prime dividing one isolated $b_r$.

---

# 3. Why ABC/Szpiro does not directly solve it

ABC gives bounds on radical size:

\[
\operatorname{rad}(abc)\gg H^{1-\epsilon}
\]

for coprime triples $a+b=c$.

But the statement

\[
p\mid b_r
\]

contains no visible additive relation involving $b_r$ and its radical.

One could apply ABC only after embedding $b_r$ into a suitable Diophantine identity. The known Apéry recurrence does not produce such an identity with enough coprimality.

Similarly Szpiro-type conjectures control discriminants of elliptic curves. The Apéry numbers arise from periods of K3 surfaces, but the divisor of the integer coefficient $b_r$ is not a discriminant divisor.

Therefore:

- ABC: no direct implication.
- Szpiro: no direct implication.
- A future $S$-unit theorem for Apéry recurrence values might be relevant, but the current sequence is not an $S$-unit recurrence.

---

# 4. Arithmetic geometry of the divisor surface $V$

The set

\[
V=\{(r,p):p\mid b_r\}
\]

is not a divisor on a fixed algebraic surface in the usual geometric sense.

The reason is that $r$ is a discrete index of a recurrence, not an algebraic parameter on which $b_r$ is a polynomial of bounded degree.

The generating function is algebraic/holonomic, but the map

\[
r\mapsto b_r
\]

has degree growing with $r$ modulo $p$.

So the naive Bezout picture fails: there is no fixed curve

\[
F(r,p)=0
\]

whose degree controls intersections.

The correct geometric object is closer to a family of Frobenius dynamical systems:

\[
\Pi_r=M(r-1)\cdots M(0),
\]

and

\[
p\mid b_r
\iff
\Pi_r e_1\in\{x=0\}\subset \mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p).
\]

Thus $V$ is a collection of Frobenius hitting loci, not a classical divisor.

---

# 5. What geometry would be sufficient?

A successful geometric theorem would need one of the following.

## (i) Uniform p-adic analytic interpolation

If the Apéry unit-root crystal produced a uniformly overconvergent function

\[
F_p(s)
\]

on $\mathbf Z_p$, then Strassmann would give bounded zeros in each residue disk.

This is the strongest route to

\[
Z_p=O(1).
\]

## (ii) Large-prime divisor equidistribution

A theorem of the form

\[
\#\{r<X:p\mid b_r\}
\approx X/p
\]

uniformly for large primes would imply AFP by summing over $p$.

This is a Bombieri--Vinogradov style statement for Apéry Frobenius data.

## (iii) Horizontal monodromy

The existing K3/elliptic sheaf controls variation in the fiber parameter. AFP requires controlling the horizontal variation

\[
(r,p)\mapsto b_r\bmod p.
\]

This is a different direction.

---

# Final answers

## 1. Direct proof of

\[
\omega(b_r,(r,X])=O(1)
\]

No known proof exists. It is essentially equivalent to the missing large-prime anti-concentration statement behind AFP.

## 2. ABC/Szpiro

No direct application is known. They control radicals/discriminants of algebraic relations, while the problem is distribution of prime divisors of a recurrence sequence.

## 3. Geometry of $V$

The divisor interpretation is heuristic only. The true object is a Frobenius dynamical incidence variety. The bounded-degree curve intuition fails because the recurrence degree grows with the index.

The right replacement for the divisor-degree idea is:

\[
\textbf{bounded conductor + horizontal Frobenius anti-concentration.}
\]

That is exactly the missing theorem needed to turn the empirical AFP phenomenon into a proof.
