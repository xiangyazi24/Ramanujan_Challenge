# Q493: Additive energy approach to (HM)_3

## Executive summary

The additive-energy route is interesting, but the proposed implication is not quite the right one. The obstruction is that the CRT product
\[
S=Z_{p_1}\times Z_{p_2}\times Z_{p_3}\subset \mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z
\]
is an **extremely sparse set**: typically \(|S|=O(1)\), while \(M\asymp X^3\). Standard additive-combinatorics machinery (BSG, Freiman theory) is designed for dense sets with large additive energy. Here the desired estimate is essentially a distribution statement for a handful of CRT points.

The most promising route is not ordinary additive energy but a hybrid:

1. use the algebraic structure of \(Z_p\) to control exceptional CRT coincidences;
2. use trace-function/Frobenius independence for the remaining equidistribution;
3. interpret the interval condition as a one-dimensional projection problem rather than a sumset problem.

---

## (A) Additive energy and the BSG chain

Let
\[
E(S)=\#\{a+b=c+d:a,b,c,d\in S\}.
\]
For a random subset of density \(\delta=|S|/M\), one expects
\[
E(S)\approx \frac{|S|^4}{M}.
\]

The standard Balog--Szemerédi--Gowers chain is:

\[
E(S)\gg \frac{|S|^3}{K}
\]

implies that there exists \(A\subset S\) with
\[
|A|\gg_K |S|
\]
and
\[
|A+A|\ll_K |A|.
\]

Then Freiman theory gives approximate containment of \(A\) in a low-dimensional generalized arithmetic progression.

However, this does **not** imply
\[
|S\cap[0,X^2]|\ll |S|X^{-1}.
\]

The reason is that the interval condition is not an additive relation. A set can have perfectly random additive energy but still have an unusually large intersection with one interval. Energy controls additive concentration, not location bias.

The implication that would be needed is closer to:

```
small additive energy
        |
        v
Fourier decay of 1_S
        |
        v
small correlation with interval indicator
        |
        v
anti-clustering
```

The missing step is Fourier decay. BSG alone does not provide it.

For the CRT product, the relevant Fourier transform is
\[
\widehat{1_S}(t)=\prod_i\widehat{1_{Z_{p_i}}}(tM/p_i).
\]
Thus the real question is bounds on the individual short exponential sums
\[
\sum_{r\in Z_p}e_p(tr).
\]
This is exactly where Frobenius/trace information enters.

## (B) Geometry of numbers

The condition
\[
R(r_1,r_2,r_3)<X^2
\]
means that a CRT representative lies in a short interval inside \([0,M)\), with
\[
\frac{X^2}{M}\asymp X^{-1}.
\]

One can write this as a lattice problem. The lattice is generated by the congruence constraints
\[
x\equiv r_i\pmod {p_i}.
\]
Its determinant is approximately
\[
M=p_1p_2p_3\asymp X^3.
\]

Minkowski gives information about the shortest nonzero lattice vector. It does not directly give the number of lattice points in a fixed interval of length \(X^2\).

The fundamental issue is dimension. The lattice lives in a three-dimensional CRT space, but the observable is the one-dimensional residue map
\[
(r_1,r_2,r_3)\mapsto R(r_1,r_2,r_3).
\]
Minkowski controls geometry of the lattice, not the arithmetic placement of the \(O(1)^3\) chosen points.

A successful geometry-of-numbers proof would need an additional input such as:

\[
\lambda_1(\Lambda)\gg X^{1+\epsilon}
\]

for the relevant difference lattice. That would rule out many close CRT collisions. But proving this lower bound is essentially the original anti-clustering problem.

Therefore geometry of numbers reformulates the problem but does not solve it automatically.

## (C) Best approach in the bounded-|Z_p| regime

The regime is actually too sparse for classical additive combinatorics.

Typical sizes:

\[
|Z_p|=0,1,2,3,
\]
so
\[
|S|=|Z_{p_1}||Z_{p_2}||Z_{p_3}|=O(1).
\]

Then
\[
E(S)=O(1),
\]
while the random benchmark
\[
\frac{|S|^4}{M}
\]
is much smaller than 1. The usual asymptotic meaning of energy estimates disappears.

The right object is the distribution of the individual CRT points.

For a fixed triple of primes, write
\[
S=\{s_1,\dots,s_N\},\qquad N=O(1).
\]
The desired statement is simply:

\[
\#\{i:s_i<X^2\}\ll N/X.
\]

This is a discrepancy problem.

The natural Fourier criterion is Erdős--Turán:

\[
\left|\#(S\cap[0,X^2])-N\frac{X^2}{M}\right|
\]

is controlled by exponential sums
\[
\sum_{s\in S}e_M(hs).
\]

CRT factorization gives
\[
\sum_{s\in S}e_M(hs)
=
\prod_i\sum_{r\in Z_{p_i}}e_{p_i}(h_i r).
\]

Hence the decisive input is not additive energy but cancellation in
\[
F_p(h)=\sum_{r\in Z_p}e_p(hr).
\]

This connects directly to the Apéry trace-function interpretation.

---

## Role of palindromicity

The identity
\[
Z_p=p-1-Z_p
\]
helps because it gives
\[
F_p(h)=e_p(-h)F_p(-h),
\]
so the Fourier coefficients have a constrained phase.

But palindromicity alone does not imply cancellation. A set such as
\[
\{0,p-1\}
\]
is perfectly palindromic but has maximal Fourier bias.

The missing ingredient is quantitative equidistribution of the Frobenius zeros.

---

## Final ranking of approaches

### 1. Trace-function cancellation: most promising

Need bounds like
\[
|F_p(h)|\ll p^{-\delta}
\]
or averaged variants. Then CRT factorization gives the required saving.

This uses the Sp_4 monodromy input in the correct place.

### 2. Geometry of numbers: useful secondary tool

Good for ruling out structured collisions, but needs arithmetic input.

### 3. Additive energy / BSG: least suitable

The set is too sparse. Energy theory detects additive structure, while the problem is about location/discrepancy of finitely many CRT points.

---

## Conclusion

The anti-clustering condition (HM)_3 is not naturally an additive-energy theorem. In the bounded-zero regime, \(S\) is closer to a random point cloud than an additive set.

The correct chain is:

\[
\text{Sp}_4\text{ trace function}
\rightarrow
\text{Fourier cancellation of }Z_p
\rightarrow
\text{CRT discrepancy}
\rightarrow
(HM)_3.
\]

Additive energy can serve as a diagnostic: unexpectedly large energy would reveal hidden structure. But proving the required \(X^{-1}\) anti-clustering requires controlling Fourier coefficients of the Apéry zero sets, not BSG/Freiman theory.
