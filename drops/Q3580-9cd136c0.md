# P3.2 — composed exponential sum from the Apéry/K3 Mellin structure

## Verdict

Let

\[
S_p(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1} e_p(t b_r),\qquad
b_r=\sum_{k=0}^r\binom rk^2\binom{r+k}{k}^2,
\]

with \(t\in\mathbf F_p^\times\). The Mellin/K3 origin does **not**, by itself, give a Deligne square-root bound for this sum. The obstruction is exactly the nonlinear composition

\[
\text{(Mellin coefficient)}\quad b_r\qquad\longmapsto\qquad e_p(t b_r).
\]

The finite-field Mellin transform is linear; the outer Artin--Schreier character is not. After making the Mellin transform completely explicit, one gets either

1. an additive character sum of a polynomial of degree \(\asymp p\), whose natural Artin--Schreier conductor is \(\asymp p\), or
2. a length-\(p\) multiplicative-character expansion whose Cauchy/Parseval bound is still only \(O(p)\).

So, with the currently justified geometric input, the sharp uniform pointwise estimate remains

\[
\boxed{|S_p(t)|\le p.}
\]

I do **not** see a valid route from the K3 monodromy alone to any uniform \(p^{1-\delta}\) bound, let alone \(p^{1/2+\varepsilon}\). A square-root bound would require a genuinely new statement: that the *composed* function \(r\mapsto e_p(t b_r)\), after an algebraic reparameterization, is a bounded-conductor trace function. The obvious reparameterization has conductor of order \(p\), so this is precisely what is missing.

There is, however, a much more promising consequence for the actual zero-set problem: one does **not** need a pointwise square-root bound for every \(t\). A near-linear **collision-energy bound**

\[
E_p:=\#\{(r,s)\in[0,p-1]^2:b_r=b_s\}\ll p^{1+\varepsilon}
\]

would already imply

\[
|Z_p|\ll p^{1/2+\varepsilon/2}.
\]

That is the cleanest replacement target I see.

## 1. First correction: the individual \(v\)-sum is not a geometric progression

Suppose one has the finite-field moment/Mellin representation

\[
b_r=\sum_{v\in\mathbf F_p^\times} f_p(v)v^r\qquad (\bmod p).
\]

Then

\[
e_p(tb_r)=\prod_v e_p\!\bigl(t f_p(v)v^r\bigr),
\]

but an individual factor summed over \(r\) is

\[
\sum_r e_p(c v^r),
\]

not \(\sum_r (e_p(c)v)^r\). Thus it is **not** an ordinary geometric series.

If \(d=\operatorname{ord}(v)\) and \(N=p-1\), then over one full period

\[
\sum_{r=0}^{N-1}e_p(c v^r)
=\frac{N}{d}\sum_{x\in\langle v\rangle}e_p(cx).
\]

For primitive \(v\), this is exactly \(-1\) when \(c\ne0\). For general \(v\), the subgroup sum is a Gauss period and is bounded by \(O(\sqrt p)\). So each single factor is very well understood.

But this does **not** help with

\[
\sum_r\prod_v e_p\!\bigl(t f_p(v)v^r\bigr).
\]

The Fourier transform of a product is a **convolution**, not a product. With roughly \(p\) factors, the zero-frequency coefficient becomes a huge constrained convolution over all tuples of frequencies whose sum is zero. There is no decoupling unless one proves additional independence/correlation estimates between all of those factors.

This is not merely a technical inconvenience: the Mellin matrix \((v^r)_{r,v}\) on \(\mathbf F_p^\times\) is invertible. Thus an arbitrary vector \((b_r)_{r\bmod p-1}\) has some inverse Mellin vector \(f_p\). The existence of a Mellin representation alone imposes no cancellation whatsoever on \(e_p(tb_r)\). The special geometry of the actual \(f_p\) would have to enter in a much stronger way.

## 2. The decisive reparameterization: Mellin becomes a degree-\(p\) polynomial phase

Let \(g\) be a primitive root modulo \(p\), write

\[
v=g^a,\qquad x=g^r,
\]

and put

\[
c_a=f_p(g^a).
\]

Then

\[
b_r=\sum_{a=0}^{p-2}c_a g^{ar}
=\sum_{a=0}^{p-2}c_a x^a
=:P_p(x).
\]

Since \(r\mapsto x=g^r\) is a bijection from \(\mathbf Z/(p-1)\) to \(\mathbf F_p^\times\), the Mellin part of the exponential sum is exactly

\[
\sum_{r=0}^{p-2}e_p(tb_r)
=\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p^\times}e_p\bigl(tP_p(x)\bigr).
\]

Also \(b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\), so the original sum differs by the harmless endpoint \(e_p(t)\):

\[
S_p(t)=e_p(t)+\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p^\times}e_p\bigl(tP_p(x)\bigr).
\]

This formula gives the cleanest explanation of why ordinary Weil/Deligne does not solve the problem.

The polynomial \(P_p\) has degree at most \(p-2\), and for a dense inverse Mellin transform it generically has degree comparable with \(p\). The Artin--Schreier sheaf

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi(tP_p(x))}
\]

therefore has conductor comparable with \(\deg P_p\), hence comparable with \(p\). The usual one-variable Weil bound gives only

\[
\left|\sum_x e_p(tP_p(x))\right|
\ll (\deg P_p)\sqrt p,
\]

which is worse than the trivial bound when \(\deg P_p\asymp p\). After taking the minimum with \(p\), one is back at

\[
|S_p(t)|\le p.
\]

The point is subtle but fundamental: **low geometric complexity of the function \(v\mapsto f_p(v)\) does not imply low algebraic complexity of \(x\mapsto P_p(x)\)**. Passing from \(v\) to the exponent \(a=\log_g v\) uses the discrete logarithm, which is not an algebraic map of bounded degree over \(\mathbf F_p\).

Thus the multiplicative Fourier transform does not expose a bounded-degree phase; it exposes a phase of degree essentially \(p\).

## 3. Standard multiplicative Fourier expansion of the *outer* additive character also does not help

There is another exact transform one can try. For \(y\ne0\), multiplicative Fourier inversion gives

\[
e_p(ty)
=\frac1{p-1}\sum_{\chi}G(\bar\chi)\,\chi(ty),
\]

where \(\chi\) runs over multiplicative characters of \(\mathbf F_p^\times\) and

\[
G(\bar\chi)=\sum_{x\ne0}\bar\chi(x)e_p(x).
\]

Let

\[
Z_p=\#\{r:b_r=0\},
\qquad
M_p(\chi)=\sum_{r:b_r\ne0}\chi(b_r).
\]

Then

\[
S_p(t)
=Z_p+\frac1{p-1}\sum_\chi G(\bar\chi)\chi(t)M_p(\chi).
\]

So the additive exponential sum is converted into a weighted sum of the multiplicative character sums \(M_p(\chi)\).

But this does not reduce the difficulty. Let

\[
E_p^\times
=\#\{(r,s):b_r=b_s\ne0\}.
\]

Multiplicative Parseval gives

\[
\sum_\chi |M_p(\chi)|^2=(p-1)E_p^\times,
\]

while the Gauss sums satisfy the exact identity

\[
\sum_\chi |G(\bar\chi)|^2=(p-1)^2.
\]

Therefore Cauchy gives only

\[
\left|\frac1{p-1}\sum_\chi G(\bar\chi)\chi(t)M_p(\chi)\right|
\le \sqrt{(p-1)E_p^\times}.
\]

Even in the essentially optimal collision regime \(E_p^\times\asymp p\), this is \(O(p)\), not \(O(\sqrt p)\).

Even a hypothetical individual bound

\[
M_p(\chi)\ll\sqrt p
\]

for every nontrivial \(\chi\) would still give only the trivial scale after summing over \(p-1\) characters. One would additionally need square-root cancellation **across the \(\chi\)-sum itself**, which is basically the original problem in dual form.

So multiplicative Fourier inversion moves the nonlinearity; it does not remove it.

## 4. What Katz-style Mellin monodromy does and does not control

Katz's finite-field Mellin-transform theory is absolutely relevant conceptually, but it controls a different operation.

For a geometrically defined trace function \(K(v)\), the standard complex/\(\ell\)-adic Mellin transform is

\[
\mathcal M_K(\chi)=\sum_{v\in\mathbf F_p^\times}K(v)\chi(v).
\]

Under suitable hypotheses, monodromy controls the distribution and moments of the normalized values \(\mathcal M_K(\chi)\) as \(\chi\) varies. This is the framework of Katz's *Convolution and Equidistribution: Sato--Tate Theorems for Finite-Field Mellin Transforms*.

Our quantity is instead

\[
\sum_r e_p\bigl(t\,b_r\bigr),
\]

where \(b_r\in\mathbf F_p\) is a coefficient/moment value and then one applies an additive character to its **reduction modulo \(p\)**.

There is no general sheaf operation saying

\[
\text{trace function }T(r)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r\mapsto e_p(T(r))
\]

with bounded rank and bounded conductor. Artin--Schreier pullback works when \(T(r)\) is an algebraic function \(F(r)\in\mathbf F_p(r)\) of bounded complexity. It does not work merely because \(T(r)\) is itself a Frobenius trace of some unrelated sheaf.

One can formally expand \(e_p(tT)\) in powers of \(T\), and powers of a trace can be represented by tensor powers of the underlying sheaf, but the required degree runs up to order \(p\); the ranks/conductors then explode and Deligne becomes useless. That is another formulation of the same obstruction.

Hence high monodromy of the K3 trace family gives excellent control of **linear trace-function sums and fixed moments**, but not of this nonlinear additive character of the coefficient values.

## 5. Important monodromy correction for the classical Apéry sequence

For the classical \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry numbers used here, the minimal Picard--Fuchs operator is

\[
\theta^3
-z(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+z^2(\theta+1)^3,
\]

and this third-order operator is a symmetric square of a second-order Picard--Fuchs equation. Thus the canonical transcendental local system of the Apéry K3 pencil is rank \(3\), of symmetric-square/orthogonal type (the image of an \(SL_2\)-type rank-2 system, hence \(SO_3/PGL_2\)-type after projectivization).

So I would **not** use "Sp4 monodromy" as the decisive input for this exact Apéry sequence unless one has separately constructed a rank-4 realization and proved that its relevant trace is exactly the function entering this exponential sum. The fact that Apéry numbers are the diagonal of a rational function in four variables does **not** imply that the minimal geometric monodromy is \(Sp_4\). Number of diagonal variables and rank of the minimal Picard--Fuchs local system are different notions.

This does not weaken the no-go above: even replacing \(SO_3\) by a genuine large \(Sp_4\) monodromy group would still not justify applying Deligne after the nonlinear map \(x\mapsto e_p(tx)\).

## 6. A genuinely strong 2026 structural theorem: the Apéry truncation is a square

There is a very relevant recent theorem of Caruso--Fürnsinn--Vargas-Montoya--Zudilin, *Galois Groups of Apéry-like Series Modulo Primes* (arXiv:2510.23298, 2026 version).

Let

\[
A_p(X)=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rX^r\in\mathbf F_p[X].
\]

They prove that there is \(B_p\in\mathbf F_p[X]\) such that

\[
A_p(X)=B_p(X)^2,
\qquad p\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{24},
\]

and

\[
A_p(X)=(X^2-34X+1)B_p(X)^2,
\qquad p\equiv13,17,19,23\pmod{24}.
\]

This is stronger and more concrete than a vague "K3 convolution" slogan. It says that the coefficient vector \((b_r)\) is literally a self-convolution of a vector of length about \(p/2\), up to the fixed quadratic factor in half of the residue classes.

However, it still does **not** yield a pointwise bound for \(S_p(t)\). If

\[
B_p(X)=\sum_j c_jX^j,
\]

then in the square case

\[
b_r=\sum_{i+j=r}c_ic_j.
\]

Ordinary Fourier transform in the index \(r\) linearizes this convolution:

\[
\sum_r b_r\omega^r=B_p(\omega)^2.
\]

But our sum is

\[
\sum_r e_p\!\left(t\sum_{i+j=r}c_ic_j\right),
\]

which applies the nonlinear additive character **before** any Fourier transform in \(r\). There is no identity turning this into a product of one-dimensional sums in the \(c_j\).

Also, the convolution is not sparse: \(\deg B_p\) is roughly \((p-1)/2\) or \((p-3)/2\). So generic bilinear-decoupling bounds do not automatically give a power saving.

Still, this factorization is probably the best concrete algebraic structure to exploit if one wants a new attack. It suggests studying additive-energy/correlation statistics of coefficients of \(B_p^2\), rather than trying to factor \(S_p\) itself.

## 7. The exact geometric criterion that *would* give square root

The desired conclusion would follow immediately from the following kind of statement.

Choose a primitive root \(g\), and define the interpolation function

\[
F_p(x)=b_{\log_g x},\qquad x\in\mathbf F_p^\times.
\]

If for every \(p\) one could realize

\[
x\longmapsto e_p(tF_p(x))
\]

as the trace function of a geometrically nontrivial middle-extension sheaf \(\mathcal G_{p,t}\) on \(\mathbf G_m\) with

\[
\operatorname{cond}(\mathcal G_{p,t})\ll p^\varepsilon
\]

uniformly in \(t\ne0\), then Deligne would give

\[
|S_p(t)|\ll p^{1/2+\varepsilon}.
\]

For an \(O(1)\)-conductor family one gets \(O(\sqrt p)\).

But the explicit interpolation above gives

\[
F_p(x)=P_p(x),\qquad \deg P_p\asymp p,
\]

so the natural Artin--Schreier sheaf has conductor \(\asymp p\), not \(O(1)\). Thus the exact missing theorem is a **conductor collapse**: prove that \(P_p\), after allowed Artin--Schreier equivalences or some non-obvious geometric transform, has an equivalent representation of conductor \(p^{o(1)}\).

I currently see no evidence for such a collapse. The square factorization of \(A_p(X)\) is not one, because it concerns the coefficient-generating polynomial, not the phase polynomial \(P_p(x)\) obtained after discrete-log reindexing.

## 8. For \(Z_p\), pointwise control of \(S_p(t)\) is stronger than necessary

This is the most useful consequence for P3.2.

Let

\[
\nu_p(a)=\#\{0\le r<p:b_r=a\},
\qquad
E_p=\sum_{a\in\mathbf F_p}\nu_p(a)^2
=\#\{(r,s):b_r=b_s\}.
\]

Additive orthogonality gives the exact second-moment identity

\[
\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p}|S_p(t)|^2=pE_p.
\]

Since \(S_p(0)=p\),

\[
\sum_{t\ne0}|S_p(t)|^2=p(E_p-p).
\]

On the other hand,

\[
|Z_p|
=\frac1p\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p}S_p(t)
=1+\frac1p\sum_{t\ne0}S_p(t).
\]

Therefore Cauchy gives

\[
\boxed{
|Z_p-1|
\le
\sqrt{\frac{p-1}{p}\,(E_p-p)}.
}
\]

Consequently,

\[
E_p\ll p^{1+\varepsilon}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|Z_p|\ll p^{1/2+\varepsilon/2}.
\]

This avoids the much harder task of proving a uniform \(O(\sqrt p)\) bound for every nonzero \(t\).

It also gives the exact RMS scale

\[
\frac1{p-1}\sum_{t\ne0}|S_p(t)|^2
=\frac{p(E_p-p)}{p-1}.
\]

So a near-minimal collision energy automatically gives square-root cancellation **on average over \(t\)**, which is all one needs for the zero count after one final Cauchy step.

## 9. Reflection shows \(\sqrt p\) is the unavoidable natural scale

The Apéry numbers satisfy the exact reflection congruence

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
\]

Indeed, for the terms which survive modulo \(p\),

\[
\binom{p-1-r}{k}
\equiv(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k},
\qquad
\binom{p-1-r+k}{k}
\equiv(-1)^k\binom rk,
\]

and squaring gives the same summand as in \(b_r\).

Thus the \(p\) coefficient indices consist of \((p-1)/2\) forced equal pairs plus the fixed midpoint \(r=(p-1)/2\). Even if there are **no other collisions at all**,

\[
E_p\ge 4\cdot\frac{p-1}{2}+1=2p-1.
\]

Hence

\[
\frac1{p-1}\sum_{t\ne0}|S_p(t)|^2\ge p.
\]

So \(\sqrt p\) is not just the hoped-for scale; it is the forced RMS scale. A true pointwise \(O(\sqrt p)\) theorem would therefore be essentially optimal.

This reflection also shows that the obvious symmetry itself does not create cancellation in \(S_p\): it doubles equal phases.

## 10. Recommended next target

For P3.2, I would **not** spend the next attack trying to prove

\[
S_p(t)\ll p^{1/2+\varepsilon}\quad\text{uniformly in }t.
\]

That requires a new bounded-conductor realization of a nonlinear coefficient-phase function, and neither the K3 trace interpretation, the Mellin transform, nor the 2026 square-factor theorem currently supplies it.

I would instead attack

\[
\boxed{
E_p
=\#\{(r,s):b_r=b_s\}
\ll p^{1+\varepsilon}.
}
\]

Equivalently, writing the coefficient function periodically on \(\mathbf F_p\), define

\[
C_p(h)=\#\{r\in\mathbf F_p:b_{r+h}=b_r\}.
\]

Then

\[
E_p=\sum_{h\in\mathbf F_p}C_p(h).
\]

A proof that the nonzero-gap correlations have total size \(p^{1+o(1)}\) would immediately give the desired \(p^{1/2+o(1)}\) zero-set bound. This is much closer in spirit to the gap-polynomial/recurrence machinery already active in P3.2 than the composed exponential sum is.

The recent factorization

\[
A_p=B_p^2\quad\text{or}\quad (X^2-34X+1)B_p^2
\]

may be useful here because it turns \(b_r=b_s\) into equality between two self-convolution coefficients. That is a concrete additive-combinatorial object on which one can try energy methods, recurrence identities, or algebraic elimination. It still looks difficult, but it targets exactly the statistic needed by the zero count.

---

## Bottom line

The convolution/Mellin idea does expose important structure, but it does **not** decouple the composed exponential sum.

The sharp conclusion I can justify is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{K3/Mellin geometry alone currently gives no uniform power saving for }S_p(t);
\quad |S_p(t)|\le p\text{ remains the rigorous pointwise bound.}
}
\]

The precise reason is that after discrete-log reparameterization the phase is an Artin--Schreier polynomial of degree/conductor \(\asymp p\), while standard multiplicative Fourier inversion merely transfers the problem to a length-\(p\) family of character sums.

For the actual P3.2 zero-set objective, the sharper strategic replacement is

\[
\boxed{E_p\ll p^{1+o(1)}},
\]

because that alone yields

\[
\boxed{|Z_p|\ll p^{1/2+o(1)}}.
\]
