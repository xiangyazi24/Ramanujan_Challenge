# Adjacent-value law, Riccati returns, and sparsity of Apéry zeros

## Executive conclusion

The adjacent-value law is a strong local constraint, but by itself it does not force a large gap between zeros. It proves that zeros are simple and fixes the local projective jump across a zero. The global question is a return problem for a non-autonomous \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\)-cocycle. A genuine bound such as \(Z_p=O(1)\) needs additional monodromy/expander input.

The useful conclusions are:

1. If \(b_r=0\), then \(b_{r-1},b_{r+1}\neq0\), and
\[
 b_{r+1}=-\frac{r^3}{(r+1)^3}b_{r-1}.
\]
This is a local reflection of the Wronskian, not a spacing theorem.

2. The Riccati orbit formulation is correct. Consecutive zeros are return times of a non-autonomous Möbius cocycle. A random model predicts return time \(\asymp p\), but this is not a theorem for the Apéry cocycle.

3. The \((-2/p)\) degree drop of the symmetric polynomial \(Q_p\) improves the algebraic root budget only if one proves that most roots do not lie in the image set \(S_p\). Degree alone does not imply few roots on \(S_p\).

4. There is a monodromy interpretation, but finite-field monodromy does not directly give a winding-number bound.

## (A) What does the adjacent-value law imply?

From the Wronskian
\[
 c_r b_{r-1}-c_{r-1}b_r=\frac1{r^3}
\]
we get, at a zero \(b_r=0\),
\[
 c_r b_{r-1}=\frac1{r^3}.
\]
Hence \(b_{r-1}\neq0\). Applying the recurrence at the zero gives
\[
(r+1)^3b_{r+1}= -r^3b_{r-1},
\]
so
\[
\boxed{b_{r+1}/b_{r-1}=-(r/(r+1))^3.}
\]

Therefore:

- zeros cannot be adjacent;
- the orbit through a zero has a uniquely prescribed two-step continuation;
- the projective state after crossing the zero is fixed.

However, suppose \(r_1,r_2\) are two zeros. The condition at \(r_1\) determines the ratio
\(b_{r_1+1}/b_{r_1-1}\), but the value of the ratio at \(r_2\) depends on the entire transfer product
\[
M(r_2-1)\cdots M(r_1+1).
\]
No contradiction arises from a short return. Thus the law gives
\[
r_2-r_1\ge2,
\]
but not \(\gg\sqrt p\) or \(\gg p^\epsilon\) by itself.

The missing ingredient would be a statement that the intermediate cocycle has sufficiently large image and no exceptional short relations.

## (B) Riccati orbit interpretation

Define
\[
x_r=\frac{b_r}{b_{r-1}}.
\]
Then
\[
x_{r+1}=\frac{P(r)}{(r+1)^3}-\frac{r^3}{(r+1)^3x_r}.
\]
This is a sequence of Möbius transformations
\[
x_{r+1}=T_r(x_r).
\]
A zero means \(x_r=0\). A second zero means
\[
T_{r_2-1}\circ\cdots\circ T_r(0)=0.
\]

This is exactly a cocycle return problem in \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\).

A random Möbius model would have return probability about \(1/p\), giving expected return time \(p\). This matches the observed equidistribution heuristics.

But making this rigorous requires proving that the Apéry cocycle behaves like a random walk. Standard finite-field orbit results do not apply because:

- the matrices vary with \(r\);
- the maps are deterministic, not sampled independently;
- the cocycle is constrained by the Picard-Fuchs equation.

A possible route would be:

\[
\text{large monodromy} + \text{mixing of the cocycle}
\Longrightarrow
\#\{r:x_r=0\}=O(1).
\]

Such a theorem is currently missing.

## (C) Effect of the \((-2/p)\) degree drop

Reflection gives a polynomial representation
\[
b_r=Q_p(r(p-1-r)).
\]
The map
\[
r\mapsto r(p-1-r)
\]
has image \(S_p\subset\mathbf F_p\). Except for the central point, every value in \(S_p\) corresponds to two zeros.

If
\[
\deg Q_p=d,
\]
then the total number of roots of \(Q_p\) in \(\mathbf F_p\) is at most \(d\). Hence
\[
|Z_p|\le 2d+1.
\]

When \((-2/p)=-1\), the factorization causes a degree drop:
\[
d<(p-1)/2.
\]
This gives a genuine improvement over the trivial reflection bound.

However, it does not imply
\[
|Z_p|=O(1).
\]
The reason is that a degree \(p/3\) polynomial can still have \(p/3\) roots. One needs information about the restriction
\[
Q_p|_{S_p}.
\]

The desired theorem would be something like:

\[
\#\{u\in S_p:Q_p(u)=0\}=O(1).
\]

This is a value-distribution problem, not a degree problem.

## (D) Monodromy of the discrete Riccati equation

The transfer matrix gives
\[
\binom{b_{r+1}}{b_r}=M(r)\binom{b_r}{b_{r-1}}.
\]
Projectivizing gives the Riccati cocycle.

For a fixed product
\[
\Phi(r,s)=M(s-1)\cdots M(r),
\]
the zero condition is
\[
\Phi(r,s)[b_r:b_{r-1}]=[0:1].
\]

Thus zeros are intersections of the cocycle orbit with a fixed divisor in \(\mathbf P^1\).

There is a monodromy viewpoint:

- the Apéry differential equation has a rank-two local system after reduction;
- the transfer matrices are discrete Frobenius transports;
- zeros are visits to a distinguished line.

But finite monodromy only describes the generated group. It does not control the number of visits of one deterministic orbit to one point.

A bounded-zero theorem would need an additional statement such as:

\[
\#\{r<p:\Phi(r,0)v\in L\}
\ll 1,
\]

coming from either:

- Deligne/Katz bounds for the associated trace function;
- algebraic independence of gap polynomials;
- expansion of the generated subgroup acting on \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\).

---

## Final assessment

The adjacent-value law is an important rigidity identity. It proves that a zero is a simple transverse crossing of the projective orbit and determines the local jump. It does not by itself imply sparse zeros.

The correct hierarchy is:

\[
\text{Wronskian}
\Rightarrow
\text{simple zeros}
\Rightarrow
\text{Riccati return problem}
\Rightarrow
\text{need cocycle mixing/monodromy}
\Rightarrow
\text{zero-count bounds}.
\]

The missing theorem for P3.2 is therefore not another local recurrence identity; it is a global anti-concentration theorem for the Apéry projective cocycle.
