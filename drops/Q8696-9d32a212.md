## 1. Exact endpoints and the target radical inside \(R_n\)

The integer range is
\[
2\le u\le H,
\qquad
p=n-u,
\]
so
\[
\rho=n-H\le p\le n-2.
\tag{1.1}
\]
Since \(H\le n/4\),
\[
p\ge\frac{3n}{4}>\frac n2,
\qquad
u:=u<p.
\tag{1.2}
\]
For large even \(n\), every candidate prime is odd, so every candidate
\(u=n-p\) is odd.  In particular, only even translation gaps can occur
between two candidate indices.

The lower endpoint needs no asymptotic smoothing.  If \(4\mid n\), then
\(ho=3n/4\); for \(n>4\), this endpoint can be prime only in exceptional
small cases when its factor \(3\) is harmlessly checked directly.  If
\(n\equiv2\pmod4\), then
\[
\rho=\frac{3n+2}{4},
\]
and \(p=\rho\) is a genuine possible boundary prime.  The carrier \(G_n\)
was deliberately stopped at \(C_{\rho-1}\), so it includes this boundary.

For every \(u\),
\[
\gcd(n-u,B_u)\mid n-u.
\]
Hence
\[
R_n\mid
\prod_{u=2}^{H}(n-u)
=
\prod_{j=\rho}^{n-2}j
=
\frac{(n-2)!}{(\rho-1)!}.
\tag{1.3}
\]
This full product has logarithmic height
\[
\log\frac{(n-2)!}{(\rho-1)!}
=\frac n4\log n+O(n),
\tag{1.4}
\]
so a bound on its ordinary height is much too large.

The target large-prime part, however, is exact.  Let \(\ell>n/2\) be a
prime dividing one factor \(n-u<n\).  Then
\[
n-u=\ell,
\]
because \(2\ell>n>n-u\).  This \(u\) is unique, and
\[
v_\ell(\gcd(n-u,B_u))
=\begin{cases}
1,&\ell\mid B_u,\\
0,&\ell\nmid B_u.
\end{cases}
\]
Therefore
\[
(R_n)_{>n/2}
=\prod_{u\in\mathcal A_n}(n-u)
=e^{Q_n},
\]
which proves (V5).  Thus the whole-product question contains the target
exactly, but its small-prime part is an additional and stronger problem.

## 2. Direct quotient-one Lucas calculation

Although the project contains the general Apéry--Lucas theorem, the present
sector admits a short direct proof that keeps the dependence \(p=n-u\)
visible.

Let \(p=n-u\) be a candidate prime.  Since \(u\le n/4\),
\[
2u<p.
\tag{2.1}
\]
Every summation index in \(B_{p+u}\) has a unique form
\[
k=ap+b,
\qquad a\in\{0,1\},\quad 0\le b<p.
\]
Lucas's binomial congruence gives
\[
\binom{p+u}{ap+b}
\equiv
\binom1a\binom ub
\pmod p.
\]
Thus only \(0\le b\le u\) survives.  For those \(b\), equation (2.1)
ensures \(u+b<p\), so there is no carry in the second binomial.  Hence
\[
\binom{p+u+ap+b}{ap+b}
\equiv
\binom{1+a}{a}\binom{u+b}{b}
\pmod p.
\]
Consequently
\[
\begin{aligned}
B_{p+u}
&\equiv
\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{b=0}^{u}
 \binom1a^2\binom{1+a}{a}^2
 \binom ub^2\binom{u+b}{b}^2\\
&=(1+4)B_u
=5B_u
\pmod p.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.2}
\]
Since \(p>5\),
\[
\boxed{p\mid B_u\iff p\mid B_n.}
\tag{2.3}
\]
Thus \(e^{Q_n}\) is also the squarefree \([\rho,n-2]\)-part of the one
fixed integer \(B_n\).  This observation alone is height-saturated, as
assumed in the question; the next sections show that the exact recurrence
carrier is saturated for a different, structural reason.

## 3. Cleared continuants and the endpoint identity

For \(r\ge1\), \(N_r(x)\) can be viewed as the determinant of the
\((r-1)\times(r-1)\) symmetric tridiagonal matrix with diagonal
\[
P(x+1),P(x+2),\ldots,P(x+r-1)
\]
and off-diagonal entries
\[
(x+2)^3,(x+3)^3,\ldots,(x+r-1)^3.
\]
Expansion at the right edge gives (V0).  Expansion at the left edge gives
\[
\boxed{
N_r(x)
=P(x+1)N_{r-1}(x+1)
-(x+2)^6N_{r-2}(x+2)
}
\tag{3.1}
\]
for \(r\ge2\).

The endpoint evaluation is
\[
\boxed{
N_{r+1}(-1)=(r!)^3B_r
}
\tag{3.2}
\]
for every \(r\ge0\).

For \(r=0\), this is \(N_1(-1)=1=B_0\).  For \(r=1\), it is
\(N_2(-1)=P(0)=5=B_1\).  If it holds at \(r-1\) and \(r-2\), then
\[
\begin{aligned}
N_{r+1}(-1)
&=P(r-1)N_r(-1)-(r-1)^6N_{r-1}(-1)\\
&=((r-1)!)^3
 \left(P(r-1)B_{r-1}-(r-1)^3B_{r-2}\right)\\
&=(r!)^3B_r
\end{aligned}
\]
by the Apéry recurrence.  This proves (3.2), including both initial
boundaries.

## 4. The exact moving-modulus telescope

Set
\[
C_s=N_{n-s}(s).
\]
Equation (3.1) becomes
\[
\boxed{
C_s=P(s+1)C_{s+1}-(s+2)^6C_{s+2}
}
\tag{4.1}
\]
for \(0\le s\le n-2\), with
\[
C_n=0,
\qquad C_{n-1}=1.
\]

Fix a target candidate \(p=n-u\).  For \(0\le s\le p-3\), write
\[
\binom{C_s}{C_{s+1}}
=
M_s\binom{C_{s+1}}{C_{s+2}},
\qquad
M_s=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(s+1)&-(s+2)^6\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{4.2}
\]
Its determinant is
\[
\det M_s=(s+2)^6,
\]
a unit modulo \(p\).  Therefore
\[
(C_0,C_1)\equiv(0,0)\pmod p
\iff
(C_{p-2},C_{p-1})\equiv(0,0)\pmod p.
\tag{4.3}
\]

At the unique singular edge \(s=p-2\), equation (4.1) is
\[
C_{p-2}=P(p-1)C_{p-1}-p^6C_p.
\tag{4.4}
\]
Now
\[
P(p-1)\equiv P(-1)=-5\not\equiv0\pmod p,
\]
so
\[
(C_{p-2},C_{p-1})\equiv(0,0)\pmod p
\iff
C_{p-1}\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{4.5}
\]
Finally,
\[
\begin{aligned}
C_{p-1}
&=N_{n-p+1}(p-1)\\
&=N_{u+1}(p-1)\\
&\equiv N_{u+1}(-1)\\
&=(u!)^3B_u
\pmod p.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.6}
\]
Because \(u<p\), the factorial is a \(p\)-unit.  Combining
(4.3)--(4.6) gives the exact equivalence
\[
\boxed{
 p\mid B_u
 \iff
 p\mid C_0\text{ and }p\mid C_1.
}
\tag{4.7}
\]

If these conditions hold, (4.5) gives
\(C_{p-2}\equiv C_{p-1}\equiv0\), and the invertible transfers propagate
backward.  Thus
\[
\boxed{
 p\mid C_s\qquad(0\le s\le p-1).
}
\tag{4.8}
\]
Every candidate satisfies \(p\ge\rho\), so \(p-1\ge\rho-1\).  Hence
\(p\mid G_n\).  Conversely, for a prime \(p\in[\rho,n-2]\), divisibility
\(p\mid G_n\) includes \(p\mid C_0,C_1\), and (4.7) gives
\(p\mid B_{n-p}\).  This proves the exact carrier identity (V1).

### Interior endpoint sharpening

If
\[
G_n^\circ=\gcd(C_0,C_1,\ldots,C_\rho),
\]
then \(G_n^\circ\) captures exactly the primes \(p>\rho\) in the target
interval.  The sole possible boundary \(p=\rho\), corresponding to
\(u=H\), is handled by \(G_n\) and contributes at most one logarithm.
This distinction is needed because a boundary prime has \(p-1=\rho-1\),
not \(p-1\ge\rho\).

## 5. Dodgson condensation gives the exact factorial tail

The continuants satisfy
\[
\boxed{
N_{r+2}(x)N_r(x+1)
-N_{r+1}(x)N_{r+1}(x+1)
=-\prod_{j=2}^{r+1}(x+j)^6
}
\tag{5.1}
\]
for every \(r\ge0\).  The empty product at \(r=0\) is one, and the
identity reads \(0-1=-1\).

For a direct proof, let the left side be \(\Delta_r(x)\).  Substitution of
(V0) into \(N_{r+2}(x)\) and into \(N_{r+1}(x+1)\) gives
\[
\Delta_r(x)=(x+r+1)^6\Delta_{r-1}(x).
\]
Since \(\Delta_0(x)=-1\), iteration proves (5.1) with the displayed
sign and every endpoint factor.

Take
\[
x=\rho-2,
\qquad r=H.
\]
Since \(ho+H=n\), equation (5.1) becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
&C_{\rho-2}\,N_H(\rho-1)
-N_{H+1}(\rho-2)\,C_{\rho-1}\\
&\hspace{25mm}
=-\prod_{j=\rho}^{n-1}j^6.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.2}
\]
The integer \(G_n\) divides both \(C_{\rho-2}\) and \(C_{\rho-1}\), so
it divides both terms on the left.  This proves (V2).

For the interior carrier, take \(x=\rho-1\) and \(r=H-1\).  One obtains
\[
\boxed{
 G_n^\circ\mid
 \left(\prod_{j=\rho+1}^{n-1}j\right)^6.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]
Thus the boundary prime \(p=\rho\) is the only reason for retaining the
factor \(ho\) in (V2).

For a candidate \(p\in[\rho,n-2]\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 v_p\left(\frac{(n-1)!}{(\rho-1)!}\right)
 &=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{p}\right\rfloor
   -\left\lfloor\frac{\rho-1}{p}\right\rfloor\\
 &=1-0=1,
\end{aligned}
\]
because \(p>n/2\).  This verifies (V3) exactly.  The extra endpoint
\(n-1\) in the determinant corresponds to \(u=1\); if \(n-1\) is prime,
(4.7) reduces its possible divisibility to \(B_1=5\), so it is not a
target prime for large \(n\).

Equation (5.2) is the canonical order-two subresultant/Casoratian of the
fixed-row transfer system.  After division by the cut-edge product on the
right, its primitive value is exactly \(-1\).  Thus the natural
subresultant route is not merely too large: its complete primitive residual
is a unit.

## 6. Exact local Smith form

The preceding saturation can be read locally.  Over \(\mathbb Z_p\), all
matrices \(M_s\) with \(s\le p-3\) lie in
\(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_p)\).  Hence the ideals
\[
(C_s,C_{s+1})_{\mathbb Z_p}
\]
are unchanged as \(s\) moves from zero to \(p-2\).

At the singular edge,
\[
M_{p-2}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(p-1)&-p^6\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Because one entry is \(1\) and the determinant is \(p^6\), its Smith
normal form is exactly
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{SNF}_{\mathbb Z_p}(M_{p-2})
=\operatorname{diag}(1,p^6).
}
\tag{6.1}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
(C_{p-2},C_{p-1})
=(C_{p-1},p^6C_p)
\quad\text{as ideals of }\mathbb Z_p.
\tag{6.2}
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
 v_p(\gcd(C_0,C_1))
 =
 \min\left\{
 v_p(N_{u+1}(p-1)),
 6+v_p(N_u(p))
 \right\}.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]
The recurrence forces one \(p\)-divisibility when \(p\mid B_u\), but it
does not force a second independent invariant.  Valuation one is fully
compatible with the exact Smith form; the exponent six is determinant
loss, not extra target selectivity.  This is why valuation refinements of
the same two-state transfer cannot reduce the prime support.

## 7. Reflection and the raw Wronskian are tautological here

Let \(p=n-u\) be a target prime.  Strong reflection gives
\[
B_{p-1-u}\equiv B_u\pmod p.
\]
Since \(p\ge3u\), the reflected index
\[
v=p-1-u
\]
is greater than \(u\).  Put
\[
h=v-u=p-1-2u=n-3u-1.
\]
Because \(p\) is odd, \(h\) is even, and
\[
\boxed{2u+h+1=p.}
\tag{7.1}
\]

The continuant reflection identity
\[
N_h(-x-h-1)=(-1)^{h-1}N_h(x)
\tag{7.2}
\]
makes \(N_h\) odd around the midpoint when \(h\) is even.  Hence the
primitive linear polynomial \(2x+h+1\) divides \(N_h(x)\) in
\(\mathbb Z[x]\):
\[
N_h(x)=(2x+h+1)N_h^\circ(x),
\qquad N_h^\circ(x)\in\mathbb Z[x].
\tag{7.3}
\]
Evaluating at the actual lower digit gives
\[
\boxed{N_h(u)=pN_h^\circ(u).}
\tag{7.4}
\]
This divisibility holds for every integer pair satisfying (7.1), before
one asks whether \(p\mid B_u\).  Therefore the raw gap-Wronskian or
resultant detects only reflection geometry.  After removing the forced
linear factor, the actual zero condition does not imply
\[
p\mid N_h^\circ(u).
\]
A theorem proving that extra divisibility would be new; it is not a
consequence of the recurrence, reflection, or the ordinary gap criterion.

## 8. Why the full composite product does not satisfy the prime telescope

The proof of (4.7) uses two prime-specific facts:

1. every factor \(1,2,\ldots,u\) is a unit modulo \(p\);
2. before the unique edge \(s=p-2\), every transfer determinant
   \((s+2)^6\) is a unit modulo \(p\).

For a general composite modulus
\[
d=n-u,
\]
the endpoint congruence is only
\[
C_{d-1}=N_{u+1}(d-1)
\equiv (u!)^3B_u\pmod d.
\tag{8.1}
\]
Although \(d>u\), a composite \(d\) can have prime factors at most \(u\),
so \((u!)^3\) need not be invertible modulo \(d\).  Moreover the transfer
chain can meet several nonunit edges, one for each small prime factor.
Consequently
\[
d\mid C_0,C_1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d\mid B_u
\]
is false as a uniform composite-modulus statement; factorial pollution is
unavoidable.

This is the exact obstruction to turning the whole product \(R_n\) into a
single clean continuant gcd.  The large-prime radical avoids it because
\(p=n-u>u\) is prime, and that radical is already exactly (V5).

## 9. The standard diagonal does not alter the carrier

For completeness, the Apéry diagonal identity is
\[
B_u=[x^uy^uz^uw^u]\,
\frac{1}{(1-x-y)(1-z-w)-xyzw}.
\tag{9.1}
\]
Indeed,
\[
\frac1{(1-x-y)(1-z-w)-xyzw}
=
\sum_{j\ge0}
\frac{(xyzw)^j}
     {(1-x-y)^{j+1}(1-z-w)^{j+1}},
\]
and the diagonal coefficient is
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{u}
\left(
\frac{(2u-j)!}{j!(u-j)!^2}
\right)^2
=
\sum_{k=0}^{u}
\binom uk^2\binom{u+k}{k}^2.
\]

This representation is exact, but it is coefficientwise in \(u\).  Taking
the product over \(2\le u\le H\) introduces independent diagonal degrees
and reproduces the same product \(R_n\) with the same moving moduli
\(n-u\).  It does not change the exact large-prime identity (V5), nor does
it remove the factorial support in (V2).  Any successful diagonal or
cohomological argument must therefore add a genuinely new relation between
different diagonal degrees and different residue characteristics; the
standard diagonal formula alone supplies none.

## 10. Quantitative ceiling

The prime number theorem in the fixed-ratio interval
\([\rho,n]\) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
L_n
&\le\pi(n-2)-\pi(\rho-1)\\
&=\left(1+o(1)\right)
  \frac{n-\rho}{\log n}\\
&=\left(\frac14+o(1)\right)\frac n{\log n},
\end{aligned}
\]
which is (V6).  Likewise
\[
\begin{aligned}
Q_n
&\le\vartheta(n-2)-\vartheta(\rho-1)\\
&=n-\rho+o(n)\\
&=\left(\frac14+o(1)\right)n,
\end{aligned}
\]
which is (V7).

Since every target prime lies in \([3n/4,n]\),
\[
L_n\log(3n/4)\le Q_n\le L_n\log n.
\tag{10.1}
\]
Thus
\[
Q_n=o(n)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
L_n=o(n/\log n).
\tag{10.2}
\]
The exact recurrence telescope reaches the linear ceiling (V7) and stops.
It does not prove either side of (10.2).

## 11. The narrowest actual-Apéry residual

Define the target indicator
\[
a_n(u)
=
\mathbf1_{\mathbb P}(n-u)
\mathbf1_{\{n-u\mid B_u\}}
\qquad(2\le u\le H).
\]
For \(1\le h\le H-2\), put
\[
\mathcal C_n(h)
=\sum_{u=2}^{H-h}a_n(u)a_n(u+h).
\]
Expanding the definition gives exactly (V8).  Every unordered pair of
distinct target indices has one and only one positive gap, so
\[
\sum_{h=1}^{H-2}\mathcal C_n(h)
=\sum_{2\le u<v\le H}a_n(u)a_n(v)
=\binom{L_n}{2}.
\]
This proves (V9).  Since target indices are odd, only even \(h\) can
contribute.

It follows that (FRQ1) is equivalent to the required fixed-row theorem.
More quantitatively, if for some fixed \(\delta>0\)
\[
2\sum_{h=1}^{H-2}\mathcal C_n(h)
\le C\frac{n^{2-\delta}}{\log^2 n},
\tag{11.1}
\]
then
\[
L_n(L_n-1)
\le C\frac{n^{2-\delta}}{\log^2 n},
\]
and hence
\[
\boxed{
L_n\le
1+\sqrt C\,\frac{n^{1-\delta/2}}{\log n}.
}
\tag{11.2}
\]
Using \(Q_n\le L_n\log n\),
\[
\boxed{
Q_n\ll_C n^{1-\delta/2}+\log n.
}
\tag{11.3}
\]

The pair statistic is genuinely mixed-characteristic:
\[
p=n-u,
\qquad
q=n-u-h=p-h,
\]
\[
p\mid B_u,
\qquad
q\mid B_{u+h}.
\]
The one-prime recurrence telescope at \(p\) and the one at \(q\) live in
different residue fields.  Their factorial determinants are independent
structural carriers.  Proving (FRQ1), or any power saving such as (11.1),
requires a new actual-Apéry relation coupling these two characteristics.
That is the residual left after the fixed-row product and subresultant have
been completely saturated.

## Terminal conclusion

The whole fixed-row attack has an exact theorem-level outcome:

\[
\boxed{
 e^{Q_n}
 = (R_n)_{>n/2}
 =\operatorname{rad}_{[\rho,n-2]}(G_n),
}
\]
\[
\boxed{
 G_n\mid
 \left(\frac{(n-1)!}{(\rho-1)!}\right)^6,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{sat}_{(n-1)!/(\rho-1)!}(G_n)=1.
}
\]

Thus the actual Apéry recurrence does telescope modulo every moving prime
\(p=n-u\), but the telescope lands exactly on the sixth power of the
moving factorial tail.  The local transfer has Smith form
\(\operatorname{diag}(1,p^6)\), and the reflected gap has the forced factor
\(2u+h+1=p\).  These are exact algebraic obstructions, not heuristic
failures.

No recurrence-free countermodel is used, and no assertion is made that the
actual Apéry sequence attains the ceiling
\((1/4+o(1))n/\log n\).  What remains is precisely the actual two-prime
fixed-row correlation (FRQ1).
