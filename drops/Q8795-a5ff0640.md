# Q8795: Can Cartier, automata, or K3 Hasse-Witt methods improve the Apéry zero-set bound?

## Executive conclusion

Among the proposed approaches, the most promising is the Cartier/K3 route, but in its current form none of the five approaches gives a proven improvement over

\[
|Z_p|=O(p^{2/3}).
\]

The obstruction is conceptual: all available structures describe the **global geometry** of the Apéry family, whereas \(Z_p\) asks for a very fine **coefficient-level vanishing problem**. The square factorization and Lucas automaton structure are real constraints, but they do not currently force enough cancellation to beat the gap-polynomial bound.

The best assessment is:

- Cartier/Hasse-Witt: potentially the deepest route, but requires a new theorem connecting Frobenius slopes to individual coefficient zeros.
- Automata: explains digit structure but is essentially circular for the first digit block.
- Square factorization: gives extra algebraic structure, but no known degree reduction for the gap method.
- Diagonal/Frobenius methods: control periods and slopes, not coefficient support.

Therefore the answer is: **no unconditional improvement below \(p^{2/3}\) is currently obtained, but the Cartier/Hasse-Witt route is the one plausible path to an O(1)-type theorem.**

---

# 1. Cartier operator and the square root \(B_p\)

We have

\[
A_p(t)=\sum_{d=0}^{p-1}b_dt^d=B_p(t)^2
\]

(up to the discriminant twist in the non-ordinary congruence classes).

The Cartier operator detects the Frobenius-stable part of the cohomology. It explains why the square root exists: the Apéry K3 has extra Frobenius structure.

However, the implication

\[
A_p=B_p^2 \Rightarrow \text{small coefficient zero set}
\]

is false in general.

Writing

\[
B_p(t)=\sum c_it^i,
\]

gives

\[
b_d=\sum_{i+j=d}c_ic_j.
\]

Thus \(b_d=0\) is a quadratic convolution relation. But a quadratic convolution sequence can have many zeros even when the original support is large.

The square root gives hidden equations, but not a zero-density theorem.

The missing statement would be something like:

> A Frobenius square root polynomial of the Apéry K3 cannot have many vanishing convolution coefficients.

No theorem of this type is currently available.

---

# 2. Automaton approach

Lucas gives

\[
b_d\equiv \prod_i b_{d_i}\pmod p
\]

for

\[
d=\sum d_ip^i.
\]

Hence on \([0,p^k)\), zeros factor completely:

\[
\#\{d<p^k:b_d=0\}=p^k-(p-|Z_p|)^k.
\]

Equivalently the zero density is

\[
1-(1-|Z_p|/p)^k.
\]

This is strong information, but it does not improve the one-digit problem.

For the first digit block \([0,p)\), the automaton states are exactly the possible residues. The automaton is therefore encoding the unknown object rather than simplifying it.

Cobham theory is powerful for fixed finite alphabets, but here the alphabet size grows with p. The family of automata is not uniform.

The crucial missing ingredient would be a uniform bound on the complexity of the automaton as \(p\to\infty\). Nothing currently gives this.

---

# 3. Diagonal/Frobenius approach

The diagonal representation implies that \(A_p\) satisfies Frobenius equations and that the associated K3 family has controlled Newton polygons.

For ordinary primes the slopes are the expected 0 and 1 slopes.

However slopes measure valuations of Frobenius eigenvalues:

\[
\alpha_i\sim p^{\lambda_i}.
\]

They do not directly control whether

\[
b_d=0.
\]

Coefficient vanishing is a much finer question than the Newton polygon.

Analogy: a polynomial may have perfectly controlled roots and slopes but still have many zero coefficients.

Thus the diagonal approach alone does not improve \(|Z_p|\).

---

# 4. Combining \(B_p^2\) with gap polynomials

This is the most concrete possible combination.

The gap method studies polynomial relations forced by many zeros:

\[
b_r=b_{r+h}=0.
\]

A square factorization gives additional identities:

\[
\sum_{i=0}^d c_ic_{d-i}=0.
\]

The hope would be that several zero coefficients force too many quadratic relations among the \(c_i\), reducing the dimension.

The problem is that the coefficients \(c_i\) are not independent variables. They arise from a degree roughly \(p/2\) polynomial. The convolution equations are compatible with many-dimensional families.

A successful argument would need a theorem such as:

\[
\dim\{B:B^2\text{ has }m\text{ prescribed zero coefficients}\}<\dim B-m.
\]

No such estimate is known over finite fields in this setting.

Therefore the square factorization has not yet improved the \(p^{2/3}\) gap bound.

---

# 5. Hasse-Witt matrix

The Hasse-Witt matrix is the most geometrically meaningful object.

Its entries are explicit polynomial functions of the family parameter, and its rank distinguishes ordinary and supersingular fibers.

The possible route is:

\[
\text{coefficient zero}
\Rightarrow
\text{Cartier degeneracy}
\Rightarrow
\text{Hasse-Witt condition}.
\]

If this implication were strong enough, algebraic geometry could bound \(|Z_p|\).

At present the implication is too weak.

The Hasse-Witt matrix controls cohomology classes. The condition

\[
b_d=0
\]

is a statement about one particular coefficient of a period expansion.

There is no known theorem converting many coefficient zeros into a large Hasse-Witt degeneracy locus.

---

# Final ranking of approaches

## Most promising: Hasse-Witt + Cartier

A future theorem of the form

\[
\#\{d:b_d=0\}\leq C
\]

because the Cartier orbit intersects a divisor only finitely often would immediately solve the problem.

This would be an arithmetic-dynamics theorem on Frobenius orbits.

## Second: square factorization + gap method

Could work if the convolution constraints are shown to reduce dimension. Currently missing.

## Third: automata

Excellent structural description, but the growing alphabet prevents a direct Christol/Cobham argument.

## Fourth: slopes/diagonals

Too coarse; they see Frobenius eigenvalues, not coefficient support.

---

# Definitive answer

No currently established method among the five proves

\[
|Z_p|=O(1)
\]

or even improves

\[
O(p^{2/3}).
\]

The existing gap-polynomial estimate remains the best unconditional bound.

The main new insight is that the Cartier/Hasse-Witt formulation identifies the right missing theorem:

> Prove that the Frobenius orbit of the Apéry K3 avoids the coefficient-zero divisor except for O(1) times.

That is a genuine arithmetic monodromy problem. If solved, it would likely explain the observed Poisson(1/2) distribution and collapse the current \(p^{2/3}\) barrier.
