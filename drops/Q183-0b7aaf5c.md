# The convolution approach to \(S_c=O(\sqrt p)\)

## Short answer

The proposed argument identifies the correct obstruction, but the key step is false in general:

\[
\psi(c\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|M))
\]

is **not** obtained by applying an Artin--Schreier sheaf to the trace and then tensoring rank-one eigenvalue sheaves. The eigenvalues of a rank-two lisse sheaf are not themselves Frobenius traces of rank-one lisse sheaves on the parameter variety.

However, there is a genuine sheaf-theoretic construction: the pullback of an Artin--Schreier sheaf along the trace function of a lisse sheaf. Its conductor is often controllable, but it is a different object from the proposed eigenvalue factorization. The remaining problem is bounding its conductor uniformly.

So the statement

\[
S_c=\sum_j\psi(c b_j)=O(\sqrt p)
\]

is plausible by Deligne once the correct trace-pullback sheaf is constructed, but it does **not** follow from the rank-one convolution argument.

---

# 1. Does the rank-two Mellin sheaf split?

Let \(M\) be the Mellin sheaf. At a geometric point \(\chi_j\), Frobenius has eigenvalues

\[
\alpha_j,\beta_j,
\]

and

\[
b_j=\alpha_j+\beta_j.
\]

This does not mean that globally

\[
M=L_1\oplus L_2.
\]

A geometrically irreducible rank-two sheaf with monodromy group such as \(SL_2\), \(Sp_2\), or a nontrivial subgroup of \(GL_2\), does not split into rank-one summands.

The eigenvalues exist only after passing to the splitting field of the Frobenius polynomial at each point.

There is no global lisse sheaf whose Frobenius trace is \(\alpha_j\).

Therefore the identity

\[
\psi(c\alpha_j)=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_j|L_1)
\]

has no reason to exist.

This is the fundamental flaw in the convolution argument.

---

# 2. Why the eigenvalue factorization fails

The desired equality is

\[
\psi(c(\alpha_j+\beta_j))
=
\psi(c\alpha_j)\psi(c\beta_j).
\]

This equality of complex numbers is true because \(\psi\) is additive:

\[
\psi(x+y)=\psi(x)\psi(y).
\]

The problem is not the identity itself.

The problem is sheaf realization.

The two factors are functions of individual eigenvalues, but the geometric object naturally available is only

\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_j|M)=\alpha_j+\beta_j.
\]

The operation

\[
M\mapsto \text{``one eigenvalue of }M\text{''}
\]

is not a functor in the category of lisse sheaves.

---

# 3. The determinant observation

For a symplectic rank-two sheaf,

\[
\alpha_j\beta_j=\det(M),
\]

and after normalization one has

\[
\alpha_j\beta_j=p^w.
\]

Hence

\[
\beta_j=p^w/\alpha_j.
\]

One can rewrite

\[
\psi(c(\alpha_j+\beta_j))
=
\psi(c\alpha_j)\psi(cp^w/\alpha_j).
\]

This is suggestive, but it still does not create rank-one sheaves. The inverse eigenvalue function lives on the spectral cover of the sheaf, not on the original base.

The symmetric powers do not solve this either. Symmetric powers encode polynomial expressions such as

\[
\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2,
\]

not the exponential function

\[
\psi(c(\alpha+\beta)).
\]

The exponential is a genuinely non-algebraic operation from the sheaf-theoretic point of view.

---

# 4. The correct sheaf construction: Artin--Schreier pullback by the trace function

There is a correct replacement.

Suppose

\[
f:X\rightarrow \mathbf A^1
\]

is the trace function

\[
f(x)=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|M).
\]

Let \(\mathcal L_\psi\) be the Artin--Schreier sheaf on \(\mathbf A^1\).

Then form

\[
\mathcal F_c=f^*\mathcal L_{\psi(c\cdot)}.
\]

Its Frobenius trace is exactly

\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|\mathcal F_c)
=
\psi(c f(x)).
\]

Therefore

\[
S_c
=
\sum_x \operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|\mathcal F_c).
\]

Now Deligne's Riemann hypothesis gives

\[
|S_c|\leq C(\mathcal F_c)\sqrt p,
\]

where the constant depends on the conductor.

This is the correct route.

---

# 5. What remains: conductor of the pullback sheaf

The problem is now transformed into:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Bound the conductor of }f^*\mathcal L_{\psi(c\cdot)}.
}
\]

For a rational function \(f\), the Swan conductor is controlled by pole orders.

For a general trace function of a rank-two sheaf, one needs local monodromy information.

The required statement is something like:

\[
\operatorname{cond}(f^*\mathcal L_\psi)=O(1)
\]

uniformly in \(p\).

If true, then immediately

\[
S_c=O(\sqrt p).
\]

This would indeed imply very strong anti-concentration for the Mellin coefficients.

---

# 6. Relation to Katz

This is exactly in the territory of Katz's work.

Katz studies Fourier transforms of sheaves and the operation of composing trace functions with additive characters. The relevant philosophy is:

- start with a geometrically irreducible lisse sheaf \(M\);
- apply Fourier transform / additive convolution;
- control geometric monodromy and conductor.

The object here is essentially an additive Fourier transform of the trace distribution of \(M\).

However, Katz's theorems do not say that every trace composition automatically has bounded conductor. One must verify that the pullback sheaf is not wildly ramified at new points.

The dangerous points are precisely:

1. singularities of the Mellin sheaf;
2. points where the trace map has poles or critical behavior;
3. points introduced by compactification.

---

# 7. Consequence for the Apéry zero problem

If one could prove the uniform estimate

\[
\left|
\sum_j\psi(c b_j)
\right|
\ll \sqrt p
\]

for all \(c\neq0\), then Fourier inversion gives

\[
|Z_p|
\leq
\frac{|Z_p|}{p}
+
O(\sqrt p).
\]

More precisely, the zero indicator satisfies

\[
1_{b_j=0}
=
\frac1p\sum_c\psi(cb_j),
\]

so

\[
|Z_p|
=
1+\frac1p\sum_{c\neq0}S_c.
\]

The square-root estimate gives only

\[
|Z_p|=O(\sqrt p),
\]

not \(O(1)\).

To obtain \(|Z_p|\le2\), one would need cancellation beyond square-root scale, or additional algebraic structure.

So the proposed implication is also too strong: Deligne square-root cancellation is enough for a strong zero-density theorem, but not the absolute bound of two zeros.

---

# Final verdict

The proposed convolution argument is close to a valid strategy, but the rank-one factorization is the wrong object.

The correct statement is:

\[
\boxed{
\psi(c\operatorname{Tr}(M))
\text{ is represented by the Artin--Schreier pullback } 
(\operatorname{Tr}M)^*\mathcal L_\psi.
}
\]

Then Deligne gives square-root cancellation provided the conductor is uniformly bounded.

The remaining Apéry-specific theorem is therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{cond}\left((\operatorname{Tr}M)^*\mathcal L_\psi\right)=O(1).
}
\]

That is the precise sheaf-theoretic bottleneck. It is a Katz-style Fourier/convolution problem, but it is not solved merely by splitting the Frobenius eigenvalues.
