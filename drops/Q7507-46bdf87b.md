# Two-prime attack on the all-index Apéry smooth radical

## Verdict

I do **not** obtain a new unconditional two-prime dispersion theorem strong enough to close the all-index conjecture. I do obtain three exact results that sharply delimit what a successful two-prime theorem must contain.

1. **Pointwise pair energy is equivalent to the remaining theorem, not weaker.** If
   \[
   \mathcal P(n)=\{p>\sqrt n:\ p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\},\qquad
   W(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P(n)}\log p,
   \]
   then, with ordered pair energy
   \[
   E_2(n)=\sum_{\substack{p,\ell\in\mathcal P(n)\\p\ne\ell}}
              \log p\,\log\ell,
   \]
   one has
   \[
   \boxed{W(n)=o(n)\iff E_2(n)=o(n^2).}
   \]
   Thus merely rephrasing the hard radical as a pair count does not lower the difficulty. A useful two-prime theorem has to be an **interval/shell dispersion theorem with cancellation or an aggregate low-height carrier**, not the raw pair energy at one index.

2. **Every fixed-order two-prime Lucas/supercongruence attack is CRT-saturated.** A target at distinct primes \(p,\ell>\sqrt n\) gives \(p\ell\mid b_n\). Any separate congruences modulo \(p^A\) and \(\ell^B\) that compute finitely many local \(p\)-adic digits of \(b_n\) impose no cross-characteristic compatibility condition: after writing \(b_n=p\ell M\), they merely prescribe \(M\) modulo \(p^{A-1}\) and modulo \(\ell^{B-1}\), and CRT always combines those prescriptions. This remains true even if a general Gessel--Lucas congruence modulo \(p^2\) is proved. The repository's `cron_supercong_check.py` currently supplies computational verification of a top-half formula, not a theorem, and I do not use it as one.

3. **The natural prime-free two-local-divisibility class has an exact product-ideal saturation.** Put
   \[
   A=n-r,\quad B=b_r,\qquad C=n-s,\quad D=b_s.
   \]
   A pair of target primes gives
   \[
   p\mid A,B,\qquad \ell\mid C,D.
   \]
   For a fixed polynomial \(F\in\mathbf Z[A,B,C,D]\), the universal implication
   \[
   p\mid A,B,\ \ell\mid C,D\Longrightarrow p\ell\mid F(A,B,C,D)
   \]
   for all sufficiently large distinct primes and all integer specializations holds **if and only if**
   \[
   \boxed{F\in(A,B)(C,D)=(AC,AD,BC,BD).}
   \]
   Since the kinematic factor \(AC=(n-r)(n-s)\) already lies in this ideal,
   \[
   \boxed{(A,B)(C,D):(AC)^\infty=\mathbf Z[A,B,C,D].}
   \]
   Hence after saturating away the divisibility that is present before one uses the Apéry zero conditions, this entire natural Bézout/product-ideal class has **no residual invariant at all**.

The most obvious genuine characteristic-zero pair certificate illustrates the theorem:
\[
\boxed{
F_{r,s}(n)=(n-r)b_s-(n-s)b_r.
}
\]
If \(r<s\), then \(F_{r,s}(n)>0\), and any target pair \((p,\ell)\) attached to \((r,s)\) satisfies \(p\ell\mid F_{r,s}(n)\). But
\[
F_{r,s}=AD-CB\in(A,B)(C,D),
\]
so its divisibility is exactly the product-ideal tautology. Its logarithmic height is \(\Theta(s)\) in the hard range and gives no summable pair saving.

The transfer/Casoratian and gap-resultant variants do not escape this saturation. A gap resultant requires a common root **in one characteristic**; a root modulo \(p\) and a different root modulo \(\ell\) do not imply any resultant divisibility. Moreover, for the reflected gap naturally attached to a target \(p\), the centered continuant factor is already \(\pm(2a+1)p\) for \(n=ap+r\), so that local gap divisibility is itself kinematic and holds for arbitrary residues.

So the requested two-prime theorem is not proved here. The exact surviving target is narrower: one needs a **genuinely cross-characteristic, Apéry-specific invariant which remains nontrivial after both kinematic saturation and CRT saturation**, or equivalently the signed/centered pair-dispersion estimate already isolated in `proof.tex`. A relation obtained only by putting two one-prime congruences side by side cannot do it.

# 2. Exact hard channel and pair-energy equivalence

Define
\[
\mathcal P(n)=
\{p:\sqrt n<p\le n,\ p\text{ prime},\ p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\},
\]
\[
W(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P(n)}\log p.
\tag{2.1}
\]
By (1.1), proving \(W(n)=o(n)\) for every \(n\) proves the remaining channel, hence the all-index conjecture after the already established \(O(n^{2/3})\) pieces.

Put
\[
Q_2(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P(n)}(\log p)^2,
\]
and define the **ordered** pair energy
\[
E_2(n)=
\sum_{\substack{p,\ell\in\mathcal P(n)\\p\ne\ell}}
\log p\,\log\ell.
\tag{2.2}
\]
Then exactly
\[
\boxed{W(n)^2=Q_2(n)+E_2(n).}
\tag{2.3}
\]
Since every target prime is at most \(n\),
\[
Q_2(n)\le (\log n)W(n).
\tag{2.4}
\]
Also every target prime divides \(b_n\): this follows from Gessel--Lucas in Section 3 below. Hence
\[
W(n)\le\log b_n.
\]
The elementary binomial formula for \(b_n\) gives \(\log b_n=O(n)\) (for example, the crude bound \(b_n\le(n+1)64^n\) is enough). Therefore
\[
Q_2(n)=O(n\log n)=o(n^2).
\tag{2.5}
\]
Consequently (2.3) gives the exact equivalence
\[
\boxed{
W(n)=o(n)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_2(n)=o(n^2).
}
\tag{2.6}
\]

This matters methodologically. A pointwise “two-prime theorem” which is simply a bound for \(E_2(n)\) is not a softer intermediate statement; at the required scale it is the original theorem squared. The manuscript's useful two-prime formulation is different: it is a signed/centered **aggregate dispersion** statement whose \(L^2\)-norm controls every column.

# 3. What the two base expansions actually force

Take distinct target primes
\[
p,\ell>\sqrt n.
\]
Write the two base expansions
\[
n=ap+r=c\ell+s,
\qquad
0\le r<p,\quad0\le s<\ell.
\tag{3.1}
\]
Because \(p,\ell>\sqrt n\),
\[
a<p,\qquad c<\ell.
\tag{3.2}
\]
The target conditions are
\[
p\mid b_r,\qquad \ell\mid b_s.
\tag{3.3}
\]
Gessel--Lucas gives
\[
b_n\equiv b_ab_r\pmod p,
\qquad
b_n\equiv b_cb_s\pmod\ell.
\tag{3.4}
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{p\ell\mid b_n.}
\tag{3.5}
\]

Equation (3.5) is the first genuine characteristic-zero common carrier. But it is exactly the prime-free reduction already found in the pointwise report: all hard target primes are, up to the controlled companion exceptions, large prime divisors of the single Apéry integer \(b_n\). Since \(\log b_n=\Theta(n)\), (3.5) is compatible with \(\asymp n/\log n\) prime divisors of size \(\asymp n\). Size alone gives no saving.

The equality of the two base expansions adds only
\[
a p-c\ell=s-r.
\tag{3.6}
\]
It does not turn either local zero into a zero in the other residue characteristic.

# 4. Fixed-order \(p\)-adic/Lucas refinements are CRT-saturated

This is the cleanest exact obstruction to using separate Gessel--Lucas congruences modulo \(p^2\) and \(\ell^2\).

## Proposition 4.1 — two-prime local-jet saturation

Let \(N\in\mathbf Z\), let \(p\ne\ell\) be primes with \(p\ell\mid N\), and put
\[
M=\frac{N}{p\ell}.
\]
Suppose some local calculation at \(p\) determines
\[
\frac Np\equiv U_p\pmod{p^A},
\tag{4.1}
\]
and an independent local calculation at \(\ell\) determines
\[
\frac N\ell\equiv U_\ell\pmod{\ell^B}.
\tag{4.2}
\]
Then (4.1)--(4.2) are equivalent to
\[
M\equiv \ell^{-1}U_p\pmod{p^A},
\qquad
M\equiv p^{-1}U_\ell\pmod{\ell^B}.
\tag{4.3}
\]
There is **no compatibility condition between the two right-hand sides**: CRT gives a unique class
\[
M\pmod{p^A\ell^B}
\]
for every pair of residues.

### Proof

Since \(N/p=\ell M\) and \(\ell\) is a unit modulo \(p^A\), (4.1) is exactly the first congruence in (4.3). The second is identical. The moduli \(p^A\) and \(\ell^B\) are coprime, so CRT is surjective onto their product. ∎

## Consequence for Apéry supercongruences

Set \(N=b_n\). Once (3.5) is known, **any finite amount of separate \(p\)-adic information about \(b_n/p\) and \(b_n/\ell\)** merely determines the quotient \(b_n/(p\ell)\) in two independent residue rings. It cannot produce a cross-prime equation.

The repository contains `research/scripts/cron_supercong_check.py`, which computationally checks
\[
b_{p+r}\equiv5b_r+10pD_r\pmod{p^2}
\]
for its test set. That file is an audit, not a proof, so I do not use this congruence as a theorem. But even granting this formula, Proposition 4.1 shows that pairing it with the analogous \(\ell^2\)-formula cannot by itself constrain \((p,\ell)\): it only gives the two residues of \(b_n/(p\ell)\).

The same argument applies to **any fixed orders** \(p^A\), \(\ell^B\). To turn the CRT class of \(M\) into an equality one would need an Archimedean uniqueness bound comparable with \(p^A\ell^B\). For fixed \(A,B\), this is polynomial in \(n\), while \(b_n/(p\ell)\) has exponential-scale height. Thus fixed-order local jets cannot close the pointwise radical through CRT uniqueness.

This proposition does **not** rule out a relation involving a second independent global integer of genuinely small height. It rules out the natural class “compute more and more local digits of the same global integer \(b_n\), then compare the two primes.”

# 5. Exact product-ideal saturation of the two local target conditions

There is an independent algebraic obstruction before any recurrence manipulation.

For two remainders \(r,s\), put
\[
A=n-r,\qquad B=b_r,
\qquad
C=n-s,\qquad D=b_s.
\tag{5.1}
\]
A target pair supplies
\[
p\mid A,B,
\qquad
\ell\mid C,D.
\tag{5.2}
\]

## Theorem 5.1 — universal polynomial pair certificates

Let
\[
R=\mathbf Z[A,B,C,D].
\]
For \(F\in R\), the following are equivalent.

1. For every sufficiently large pair of distinct primes \(p,\ell\) and every integer specialization satisfying
   \[
   p\mid A,B,\qquad \ell\mid C,D,
   \]
   one has
   \[
   p\ell\mid F(A,B,C,D).
   \]

2. One has
   \[
   \boxed{
   F\in(A,B)(C,D)=(AC,AD,BC,BD).
   }
   \tag{5.3}
   \]

### Proof

The implication (2) \(\Rightarrow\) (1) is immediate: every generator in (5.3) contains one factor divisible by \(p\) and one factor divisible by \(\ell\).

For the converse, first show \(F\in(A,B)\). Fix a sufficiently large prime \(p\), choose any distinct prime \(\ell\), and specialize \(A=B=0\). By taking \(C,D\) to be multiples of \(\ell\), their residues modulo \(p\) can be arbitrary because \(\ell\) is invertible modulo \(p\). The hypothesis therefore gives
\[
F(0,0,C,D)\equiv0\pmod p
\]
for every \((C,D)\in\mathbf F_p^2\). Taking \(p\) larger than the total degree of \(F\), a polynomial that vanishes on all of \(\mathbf F_p^2\) is the zero polynomial modulo \(p\). Since this holds for infinitely many primes, every integer coefficient of \(F(0,0,C,D)\) is zero. Hence \(F\in(A,B)\).

The same argument with the roles exchanged gives \(F\in(C,D)\). Finally the two monomial ideals involve disjoint variable sets, so
\[
(A,B)\cap(C,D)=(A,B)(C,D).
\]
This proves (5.3). ∎

## Saturation

The product ideal already contains
\[
AC=(n-r)(n-s),
\]
which is divisible by \(p\ell\) before using either Apéry zero. Hence
\[
\boxed{
(A,B)(C,D):(AC)^\infty=R.
}
\tag{5.4}
\]
Indeed \(AC\in(A,B)(C,D)\), so \(1\) belongs to the saturation.

Equation (5.4) is a precise no-go statement: **within the class of prime-free polynomial expressions whose only hypotheses are the two local divisibility pairs, every forced pair divisor disappears after the obvious kinematic factor is saturated away.**

The theorem continues to hold after adjoining passive parameters such as \(n,r,s\) or the quotients \(a,c\), provided no additional cross-relation between the Apéry values is imposed.

# 6. The obvious nonzero two-prime certificate is exactly tautological

A natural attempt is the determinant
\[
\boxed{
F_{r,s}(n)=(n-r)b_s-(n-s)b_r=AD-CB.
}
\tag{6.1}
\]
From (5.2),
\[
p\ell\mid F_{r,s}(n).
\tag{6.2}
\]
This is a genuine characteristic-zero relation using both local data.

It is also nonzero whenever \(r\ne s\). Suppose \(r<s\). Apéry numbers are positive and strictly increasing, so \(b_s>b_r>0\), while \(n-r>n-s>0\). Therefore
\[
(n-r)b_s>(n-s)b_r,
\]
and
\[
F_{r,s}(n)>0.
\tag{6.3}
\]

However, (6.1) is literally a linear combination of the product-ideal generators \(AD\) and \(CB\). Its divisibility contains no information beyond (5.2).

Its height is also at the wrong scale. The elementary exponential growth bound for \(b_s\) gives
\[
\log F_{r,s}(n)=O(s+\log n).
\tag{6.4}
\]
In the hard quotient ranges, \(s\) can be a fixed positive fraction of \(n\). Thus one gets only a linear-height carrier for a pair whose modulus has logarithm \(O(\log n)\). Summing such pair-specific carriers is much worse than the required pair-energy scale.

The cross gcd
\[
\gcd((n-r)b_s,(n-s)b_r)
\]
is not better: it contains
\[
\gcd(n-r,b_r)\,\gcd(n-s,b_s),
\]
so controlling it already contains the two original one-point shifted-gcd problems.

# 7. Why the Apéry transfer/Casoratian does not create the missing cross relation

Let \(c_m\) be the standard companion solution with \((c_0,c_1)=(0,1)\). The exact Casoratian is
\[
b_{m-1}c_m-b_mc_{m-1}=\frac1{m^3}.
\tag{7.1}
\]
If \(p>r\) and \(p\mid b_r\), then (7.1) shows
\[
p\nmid c_r.
\tag{7.2}
\]
For two indices \(r,s\), the transfer determinant
\[
b_rc_s-c_rb_s
\tag{7.3}
\]
is a normalized gap continuant. But modulo the target prime \(p\),
\[
b_rc_s-c_rb_s\equiv-c_rb_s\pmod p,
\]
and (7.2) makes \(c_r\) a unit. There is no reason for \(p\mid b_s\). Therefore a zero at \(r\) modulo \(p\) does **not** make (7.3) vanish modulo \(p\); similarly for \(\ell\).

To force both prime divisibilities one must multiply the unwanted term by a kinematic factor such as \(n-r\) or \(n-s\). That puts the resulting expression back into the product ideal of Theorem 5.1.

So the exact transfer matrix gives a strong **same-characteristic** relation among two returns. It does not turn two zeros in different residue characteristics into one common algebraic condition.

# 8. Reflection/gap continuants are even more strongly saturated

The natural same-characteristic second zero is supplied by reflection:
\[
b_r\equiv b_{p-1-r}\pmod p.
\tag{8.1}
\]
Let
\[
h=|p-1-2r|.
\]
The canonical gap polynomial satisfies the centered parity identity. For even \(h\),
\[
2^{3(h-1)}N_h(x)
=(2x+h+1)\Phi_{h/2}((2x+h+1)^2)
\tag{8.2}
\]
with \(\Phi_{h/2}\in\mathbf Z[Z]\).

Now write \(n=ap+r\). If \(r<(p-1)/2\), evaluate at \(x=n\); if \(r>(p-1)/2\), use the reflected coordinate \(x=-n-1\). In either case the centered factor is
\[
\boxed{
2x+h+1=\pm(2a+1)p.
}
\tag{8.3}
\]
Therefore
\[
p\mid N_h(x)
\tag{8.4}
\]
for **every** noncentral residue \(r\), whether or not \(p\mid b_r\).

Thus the most obvious attempt to attach one gap continuant to each of the two target primes produces a local certificate whose \(p\)-factor is already forced by the base-expansion geometry. Combining the \(p\)- and \(\ell\)-continuants cannot create cross-prime information from these factors.

This is stronger than saying that a resultant is too tall: the local factors one would feed to the resultant have already forgotten the distinguished zero fibre.

# 9. Cross-characteristic resultants: exact compatibility failure

For integer polynomials \(F,G\), a prime \(q\) divides
\[
\operatorname{Res}(F,G)
\]
when the reductions of \(F\) and \(G\) acquire a common root in the **same characteristic** (with the usual harmless leading-coefficient qualifications).

A root of \(F\) modulo \(p\) and a root of \(G\) modulo a different prime \(\ell\) imply nothing about the resultant.

The minimal counterexample is
\[
F(X)=X,\qquad G(X)=X-1.
\]
For every prime \(p\), \(F\) has the root \(0\) modulo \(p\); for every prime \(\ell\), \(G\) has the root \(1\) modulo \(\ell\). But
\[
\operatorname{Res}(F,G)=-1.
\tag{9.1}
\]

Applied to the present problem: a target \(p\) may provide a root of a gap/Cartier polynomial over \(\mathbf F_p\), and a target \(\ell\) a root of another polynomial over \(\mathbf F_\ell\). Unless one first produces a **single characteristic-zero algebraic point** whose reductions are the two local points, or makes both polynomials vanish at one point in one residue field, there is no resultant divisibility to exploit.

The equality
\[
n=ap+r=c\ell+s
\]
does not provide such a common root. It supplies only the kinematic linear relation (3.6).

Evaluating two polynomials at the same integer \(n\) does not fix the issue either: from
\[
p\mid F(n),\qquad \ell\mid G(n)
\]
one gets
\[
p\ell\mid F(n)G(n),
\]
which is again the product-ideal construction, not a resultant theorem.

# 10. Constant-term model: the same saturation in another language

The project uses a fixed Laurent polynomial \(\Lambda\) with
\[
b_m=\operatorname{CT}\Lambda^m.
\tag{10.1}
\]
For \(n=ap+r\), Freshman/Cartier reduction gives the Lucas factorization of the constant term modulo \(p\):
\[
\operatorname{CT}\Lambda^n
\equiv b_ab_r\pmod p.
\tag{10.2}
\]
Thus two targets give precisely
\[
p\ell\mid\operatorname{CT}\Lambda^n=b_n.
\]

A fixed collection of higher Cartier coefficients or local Frobenius corrections at \(p\) and \(\ell\) again supplies two separate local jets of the same global period and falls under Proposition 4.1 unless it produces a second, independent small-height global period relation.

There is also no static Laurent-gcd shortcut: constant term is linear, not multiplicative and not an ideal homomorphism. A Laurent-polynomial Bézout identity does not turn into a scalar Bézout identity among its constant terms without keeping every shifted numerator created by the multipliers.

This matches the current dispersion audit's scoped conclusion: bounded-complexity linear Mellin/Cartier channels exist, but exact zero detection is the nonlinear step whose complexity is not controlled by the standard fixed-rank calculus.

# 11. What a successful genuinely two-prime theorem would have to look like

The two saturation theorems leave a precise escape hatch.

A successful object must satisfy **all** of the following.

1. It must depend on the distinguished Apéry orbit, not merely on
   \[
   p\mid(n-r),b_r,\qquad \ell\mid(n-s),b_s.
   \]

2. It must remain nontrivial after removing the kinematic factors \(n-r\), \(n-s\) (or, equivalently, \(p\) and \(\ell\) themselves).

3. It must not be only a finite-order local expansion of the single integer \(b_n\); otherwise Proposition 4.1 applies.

4. If it is a resultant, the construction must create a common root in one characteristic, or a single characteristic-zero algebraic point whose reductions yield both local conditions. Merely having separate local roots is insufficient.

5. Its **aggregate height**, not just its individual nonvanishing, must be at the dispersion scale.

The manuscript's signed two-prime term already has exactly this status: it is a horizontal correlation of the actual target rows and is not implied by one-prime cardinality bounds.

One possible algebraic target would be a global pair carrier \(\mathfrak C_n\ne0\) with the valuation amplification
\[
v_p(\mathfrak C_n)\ge |\mathcal P(n)|-1
\qquad(p\in\mathcal P(n)),
\tag{11.1}
\]
so that
\[
(|\mathcal P(n)|-1)W(n)\le\log|\mathfrak C_n|.
\tag{11.2}
\]
Since
\[
|\mathcal P(n)|\ge \frac{W(n)}{\log n},
\]
a height estimate
\[
\boxed{
\log|\mathfrak C_n|=o\!\left(\frac{n^2}{\log n}\right)
}
\tag{11.3}
\]
would imply \(W(n)=o(n)\): unless \(W=O(\log n)\) already, (11.2) gives
\[
\frac{W(n)^2}{2\log n}\le\log|\mathfrak C_n|.
\]

But the natural product-ideal constructions do not produce (11.1) with a globally compressed carrier. Taking products of pair-specific tautological certificates simply reconstructs the pair energy or powers of the original radical.

Thus (11.1)--(11.3) are a useful specification, not a theorem presently supplied by the recurrence.

# 12. Exact scope of the negative result

The negative result here is deliberately scoped.

It **does rule out** the following as standalone proofs of the all-index theorem:

- juxtaposing Gessel--Lucas congruences at \(p\) and \(\ell\);
- any fixed finite lift of those congruences to \(p^A\) and \(\ell^B\), followed only by CRT comparison of \(b_n\);
- prime-free polynomial Bézout expressions whose only inputs are the two divisibility pairs \((n-r,b_r)\) and \((n-s,b_s)\);
- multiplying or taking determinants of such local expressions without an additional cross-Apéry relation;
- pairing the two reflection-gap continuants through their forced centered factors;
- a “two-characteristic resultant” formed from unrelated roots modulo \(p\) and \(\ell\);
- a fixed constant-term/tensor construction which only computes separate local jets of the same scalar period.

It **does not rule out**:

- a new Apéry-specific horizontal identity coupling two residue characteristics before reduction;
- a characteristic-zero object with two genuinely independent global period coordinates and a small determinant;
- a defining-characteristic phase module outside the bounded-complexity tensor operations already audited;
- a bad-diagonal inverse theorem converting persistent pair concentration into a forbidden self-twist;
- the signed two-prime dispersion estimate in `proof.tex` itself.

Those are precisely the classes that survive both CRT saturation and product-ideal saturation.

E_2(n)=o(n^2)
\]
is equivalent to \(W(n)=o(n)\), so it is not a weaker theorem waiting to be extracted from the recurrence.

The two most natural ways to manufacture such a theorem both saturate:

\[
\boxed{
\text{fixed local }p\text{-adic jets at }p,\ell
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{independent CRT data for }b_n/(p\ell),
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\text{two local divisibility ideals}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(A,B)(C,D),\qquad
(A,B)(C,D):(AC)^\infty=R.
}
\]

The transfer/gap/constant-term models do not evade these facts merely by being Apéry-specific: their known same-characteristic identities do not create a shared root across two different residue characteristics, and the reflected gap factor is already kinematic.

Therefore I cannot honestly promote a new unconditional pair-collision saving. The smallest genuinely new theorem that could still close the problem is a **cross-characteristic Apéry identity surviving both saturations**, or the equivalent signed/centered two-prime dispersion theorem already isolated in the manuscript. No random-model independence assumption is used in this conclusion.
