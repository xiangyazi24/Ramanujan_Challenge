# Apéry complementary-pair common divisors: exact desaturation, and the remaining saturated obstruction

## Verdict

There is a clean Apéry-specific theorem here, but it does **not** come from the raw recurrence continuant/Casoratian. The natural recurrence eliminant is structurally saturated by the complementary reflection factor, and the smallest nontrivial gap already shows that dividing out that factor destroys target-prime divisibility.

The useful exact statement instead comes from combining Apéry--Lucas with complementarity. If a prime `p` divides `m=r+s+1`, write `m=qp` with `q<p`. Then, writing

\[
r=a+up,\qquad 0\le a<p,
\]

one has

\[
s=(p-1-a)+(q-1-u)p,
\]

and hence

\[
b_r\equiv b_a b_u,\qquad b_s\equiv b_a b_{q-1-u}\pmod p.
\]

Using the ordinary reflection `b_{p-1-a}\equiv b_a (mod p)`, this gives the exact dichotomy

\[
\boxed{
 p\mid\gcd(b_r,b_s)
 \iff
 \bigl(p\mid b_a\bigr)
 \ \lor\ 
 \bigl(p\mid b_u\ \text{and}\ p\mid b_{q-1-u}\bigr).
}
\tag{1}
\]

This survives saturation in the strongest possible sense: **after excluding the universal first branch `p|b_a`, every complementary common zero descends to two Apéry indices `<q`.** For the large-prime range `p>n^{2/3}`, one has `q=m/p<n^{1/3}`. Therefore every nonsaturated target prime divides the single nonzero integer

\[
\mathcal C_n:=\prod_{1\le j\le \lceil n^{1/3}\rceil} b_j,
\]

whose logarithmic height is

\[
\log \mathcal C_n=O(n^{2/3})=o(n).
\tag{2}
\]

So the nonsaturated complementary-pair class has exactly the requested low-height certificate.

However, the first branch of (1) is genuinely unavoidable. If `p|b_a`, complementarity imposes **no condition at all** on the upper base-`p` digits: every admissible complementary pair with that residue is automatically a common zero. Thus no theorem depending only on the fact that the two indices are complementary can remove that class. In the fixed-`n` carrier, `a=n mod p`, so the narrow remaining statement is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{\substack{p>n^{2/3}\\ p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}\log p=o(n),
}
\tag{3}
\]

(or only over the primes actually admitted by the carrier, which in the large-prime range makes no material difference). Together with (2) and the elementary `O(n^{2/3})` contribution of primes `p\le n^{2/3}`, (3) suffices for the full complementary carrier.

Thus the common-divisor problem really splits into:

1. a **solved desaturated complementary branch**, with a global `O(n^{2/3})` certificate; and
2. the **reflection-saturated pointwise branch** (3), which complementary-pair rigidity cannot shrink further.

This is not inferred from scans. The small cases below are used only to falsify stronger proposed continuant/rank laws.

## 1. Repo state used

I inspected the current repository state, in particular `problems/3.2/proof.tex`. The ingredients already established there that matter here are:

- the Apéry recurrence
  \[
  (k+1)^3b_{k+1}-C(k)b_k+k^3b_{k-1}=0,
  \qquad
  C(k)=34k^3+51k^2+27k+5;
  \tag{4}
  \]
- Apéry--Lucas digit factorization;
- the no-consecutive-zero argument modulo a prime when the recurrence coefficients are units;
- the gap-polynomial/continuant machinery and its centered symmetry, whose forced central factor is the same saturation phenomenon isolated below.

A useful factorization, immediate from (4), is

\[
C(k)=(2k+1)(17k^2+17k+5),
\tag{5}
\]

and therefore

\[
C(-k-1)=-C(k).
\tag{6}
\]

Equation (6) is the recurrence-level source of the complementary reflection saturation.

## 2. The natural recurrence continuant is exactly saturated

Let `u<v`. Define the cleared transfer continuant by

\[
K_{u,u+1}=1,\qquad K_{u,u+2}=C(u+1),
\]

and, for `t\ge u+2`,

\[
K_{u,t+1}=C(t)K_{u,t}-t^6K_{u,t-1}.
\tag{7}
\]

Repeated use of (4) gives the exact implication

\[
p\mid b_u,\quad p>v
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\left(\prod_{j=u+2}^{v}j^3\right)b_v
\equiv K_{u,v}b_{u+1}\pmod p.
\tag{8}
\]

If also `p|b_v`, then `p\nmid b_{u+1}`: otherwise the recurrence can be propagated backwards (all indices are `<p`) to force `p|b_0=1`. Hence

\[
p\mid b_u,\quad p\mid b_v,\quad p>v
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p\mid K_{u,v}.
\tag{9}
\]

So at first sight `K_{u,v}` looks like the desired common-zero certificate.

### Determinant form

`K_{u,v}` is the determinant of the symmetric tridiagonal matrix indexed by

\[
k=u+1,u+2,\ldots,v-1
\]

with diagonal entries `C(k)` and off-diagonal entry `-(k+1)^3` between the rows indexed by `k` and `k+1`.

It is a genuinely nonzero integer: for every `k\ge0`,

\[
C(k)-k^3-(k+1)^3
=4(2k+1)^3>0.
\tag{10}
\]

Thus this symmetric matrix is strictly diagonally dominant with positive diagonal, hence positive definite; in particular

\[
K_{u,v}>0.
\tag{11}
\]

So nonvanishing is not the problem. The problem is target blindness.

### Complementary anti-symmetry

Take the cleanest complementary case `m=p` with `p` odd prime and

\[
v=p-1-u.
\]

Reversing the internal index interval sends

\[
k\longmapsto p-1-k\equiv -1-k\pmod p.
\]

By (6), the diagonal entry changes sign modulo `p`. The off-diagonal also changes sign because

\[
-(p-1-k)^3\equiv +(k+1)^3
\equiv -\bigl(-(k+1)^3\bigr)\pmod p.
\]

If `J` is the reversal matrix and `T` the tridiagonal matrix above, then

\[
JTJ\equiv -T\pmod p.
\tag{12}
\]

The number of internal rows is

\[
L=v-u-1=p-2u-2,
\]

which is odd. Therefore

\[
K_{u,v}=\det T
=\det(JTJ)
\equiv\det(-T)
=(-1)^L\det T
=-K_{u,v}\pmod p.
\]

Since `p` is odd,

\[
\boxed{p\mid K_{u,p-1-u}\quad\text{for every }u,}
\tag{13}
\]

whether or not either endpoint is an Apéry zero.

This is precisely the problem visible in the repo's centered gap-polynomial symmetry: for even gap, the natural cleared eliminant has the complementary linear factor `r+s+1`. It is a recurrence symmetry factor, not a common-zero rigidity factor.

## 3. Smallest explicit failure after desaturation

The first Apéry numbers are

```text
b_0 = 1
b_1 = 5
b_2 = 73
b_3 = 1445 = 5 * 17^2
b_4 = 33001
b_5 = 819005
```

Take

\[
r=1,\qquad s=3,\qquad m=r+s+1=5.
\]

Then

\[
5\mid b_1,\qquad 5\mid b_3.
\]

The gap is two, so there is one internal recurrence row and the continuant is simply

\[
K_{1,3}=C(2)=535=5\cdot107.
\tag{14}
\]

Thus the target prime `5` is exactly the forced complementary factor. Once that universal factor is removed,

\[
K_{1,3}/5=107\not\equiv0\pmod5.
\tag{15}
\]

This is an explicit counterexample to the hoped-for statement

> “after dividing the natural continuant/resultant by its reflection factor, a complementary common-zero prime still divides the quotient.”

In fact the entire gap-two family shows why the factor is built in. If `s=r+2`, then `m=2r+3` and

\[
K_{r,r+2}=C(r+1)
=(2r+3)\bigl(17(r+1)^2+17(r+1)+5\bigr).
\tag{16}
\]

The complementary modulus itself is the first factor.

This does **not** disprove every imaginable resultant certificate. It does kill the canonical one obtained by eliminating through the order-two recurrence, and it shows that merely improving its height without changing the arithmetic information cannot work.

## 4. Ordinary Casoratians are saturated for the same reason

For two solutions `x_k,y_k` of (4), put

\[
W_k=x_k y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}y_k.
\]

A one-line subtraction of the two recurrences gives

\[
(k+1)^3W_k=k^3W_{k-1}.
\tag{17}
\]

Hence `(k+1)^3W_k` is constant in `k`.

If modulo `p` one chooses the reflected solution

\[
y_k=b_{p-1-k},
\]

then the ordinary Apéry reflection gives `y_k\equiv b_k (mod p)`. Consequently

\[
W_k\equiv0\pmod p
\]

identically. Thus the obvious two-solution Casoratian contains no information beyond reflection. This is another form of the saturation in (13).

## 5. Strong divisibility and a single rank of apparition fail outright

The Apéry sequence is not a strong divisibility sequence in the usual sense. Already

\[
\gcd(b_1,b_2)=\gcd(5,73)=1
\ne b_{\gcd(1,2)}=b_1=5.
\tag{18}
\]

A Lucas-sequence-style single rank of apparition also fails. Modulo `5`, among one-digit indices,

\[
(b_0,b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4)\equiv(1,0,3,0,1)\pmod5.
\]

Since Apéry--Lucas is digitwise, this gives the exact infinite description

\[
5\mid b_N
\iff
\text{some base-5 digit of }N\text{ is }1\text{ or }3.
\tag{19}
\]

Thus zeros are not the multiples of one minimal positive rank (the smallest zero is `1`, but `2` is not a zero). Any viable “rank” theory would have to be a finite-state/digitwise one, not the standard strong-divisibility mechanism.

## 6. Exact complementary two-digit theorem

Here is the useful positive result.

### Theorem (desaturated complementary Lucas rigidity)

Let `p` be prime and let

\[
m=qp,\qquad 1\le q<p.
\]

Let `r,s\ge0` satisfy

\[
r+s=m-1.
\]

Write

\[
r=a+up,\qquad 0\le a<p.
\]

Then automatically `0\le u\le q-1`, and with

\[
w=q-1-u
\]

we have

\[
s=(p-1-a)+wp.
\tag{20}
\]

Moreover

\[
\boxed{
 p\mid\gcd(b_r,b_s)
 \iff
 \left[p\mid b_a\right]
 \lor
 \left[p\mid b_u\ \text{and}\ p\mid b_w\right].
}
\tag{21}
\]

#### Proof

Because `u,w<p`, the two indices have at most two base-`p` digits. Apéry--Lucas gives

\[
b_r=b_{a+up}\equiv b_a b_u\pmod p.
\tag{22}
\]

Similarly,

\[
b_s=b_{(p-1-a)+wp}
\equiv b_{p-1-a}b_w
\equiv b_a b_w\pmod p,
\tag{23}
\]

where the last congruence is the ordinary reflection.

Writing `A=b_a`, `B=b_u`, `C=b_w` in `F_p`, simultaneous vanishing is

\[
AB=AC=0.
\]

Over a field this is equivalent to

\[
A=0\quad\text{or}\quad(B=C=0),
\]

which is exactly (21). QED.

### Why this is genuinely stronger than reflection alone

Reflection explains only the common first-digit factor `b_a`. Equation (21) says that **once this factor is nonzero, common divisibility has no freedom left in the large indices**: it forces a common zero at two indices `<q`.

That is the required survival after saturation.

## 7. Specialization to the fixed-`n` carrier

Now return to the user's carrier. Let

\[
r=n-m,\qquad s=m-1-r,
\]

and suppose a prime `p|m` lies in the range

\[
p>n^{2/3}.
\]

Write

\[
m=qp.
\]

Then

\[
q=\frac mp\le\frac np<n^{1/3}<p,
\tag{24}
\]

so the theorem applies.

Write

\[
n=a+vp,\qquad a=n\bmod p,
\]

so `v=floor(n/p)`. Since `r=n-qp`,

\[
r=a+(v-q)p.
\]

Thus in (21)

\[
u=v-q,
\qquad
w=q-1-u=2q-v-1.
\tag{25}
\]

Consequently every large target prime satisfies the exact alternative

\[
\boxed{
 p\mid d_m
 \Longrightarrow
 \left[p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}\right]
 \lor
 \left[p\mid b_{v-q}\ \text{and}\ p\mid b_{2q-v-1}\right].
}
\tag{26}
\]

In fact, for the `b_r,b_s` part, this is an equivalence.

The second branch has

\[
0\le v-q<q<n^{1/3},
\qquad
0\le2q-v-1<q<n^{1/3}.
\tag{27}
\]

If it occurs and the first branch does not, neither small index can be zero, because `b_0=1`.

There is also an exact interval description. If we rename

\[
u=v-q,\qquad w=2q-v-1,
\]

then

\[
q=u+w+1,
\qquad
v=2u+w+1.
\tag{28}
\]

Since `v=floor(n/p)`, a residual prime attached to `(u,w)` lies in

\[
\frac{n}{2u+w+2}<p\le\frac{n}{2u+w+1}.
\tag{29}
\]

I do not need (29) for the height bound, but it may be useful if one later attacks the residual primes by interval/energy methods.

## 8. A single `o(n)` certificate for every nonsaturated large target prime

Set

\[
Q=\lceil n^{1/3}\rceil,
\qquad
\mathcal C_n=\prod_{j=1}^{Q}b_j.
\tag{30}
\]

Consider a prime `p>n^{2/3}` dividing some `d_m`, and suppose it is **not** in the saturated branch

\[
p\nmid b_{\,n\bmod p}.
\]

Then (26) forces

\[
p\mid b_u,\qquad p\mid b_w
\]

for indices `1\le u,w<Q`. Therefore

\[
\boxed{p\mid\mathcal C_n.}
\tag{31}
\]

Also `p^2>n\ge m`, so such a prime can occur in `d_m|m` only to the first power. Hence the entire squarefree large-prime nonsaturated part of

\[
\operatorname{lcm}_m d_m
\]

divides `rad(\mathcal C_n)`, and therefore divides `\mathcal C_n`.

### Height

For `0\le k\le j`,

\[
\binom jk\le2^j,
\qquad
\binom{j+k}{k}\le2^{j+k}\le2^{2j}.
\]

Hence every summand in `b_j` is at most `2^{6j}`, and

\[
b_j\le(j+1)2^{6j}.
\tag{32}
\]

Therefore

\[
\log b_j\le6j\log2+\log(j+1)=O(j),
\]

and

\[
\log\mathcal C_n
\le
\sum_{j\le Q}\bigl(6j\log2+\log(j+1)\bigr)
=O(Q^2)
=O(n^{2/3}).
\tag{33}
\]

This is the requested nonzero integer certificate for the desaturated class, with an aggregate logarithmic height that is not merely `o(n)` but `O(n^{2/3})`.

## 9. The `2/3` scale is the natural balance of this reduction

The same argument works parametrically. Choose any fixed

\[
\frac12<\alpha<1.
\]

For primes

\[
p>n^\alpha,
\]

we have

\[
q=m/p<n^{1-\alpha}<p.
\]

Every nonsaturated large prime divides

\[
\mathcal C_{n,\alpha}
:=\prod_{1\le j\le\lceil n^{1-\alpha}\rceil}b_j,
\]

and

\[
\log\mathcal C_{n,\alpha}
=O\bigl(n^{2(1-\alpha)}\bigr).
\tag{34}
\]

On the other hand, primes `p\le n^\alpha` contribute at most

\[
\sum_{p\le n^\alpha}
\left\lfloor\frac{\log n}{\log p}\right\rfloor\log p
\le \pi(n^\alpha)\log n
=O(n^\alpha)
\tag{35}
\]

by the standard Chebyshev bound `\pi(x)=O(x/\log x)`.

Balancing

\[
\alpha=2(1-\alpha)
\]

gives

\[
\alpha=\frac23.
\]

So the `n^{2/3}` cutoff is not accidental for this two-digit reduction: it balances the elementary small-prime mass against the height of the small-index Apéry certificate.

## 10. The residual branch is real: a small exact example

The theorem is not just saying that all common divisors are reflection saturation. There are genuine second-digit common zeros.

Take

\[
p=5,\qquad m=15=3p,\qquad r=5,\qquad s=9.
\]

Then `r+s=m-1`. In base `5`,

\[
r=0+1\cdot5,
\qquad
s=4+1\cdot5.
\]

Here `a=0`, so the saturated first factor is

\[
b_a=b_0=1\not\equiv0\pmod5,
\]

but `u=w=1` and

\[
b_1=5\equiv0\pmod5.
\]

Indeed

```text
b_5 = 819005
b_9 = 468690849005
```

are both divisible by `5`. Thus this common divisor is exactly the second branch of (21), and it descends to the small index `1` as claimed.

This example is only a check of the exact identity; no asymptotic conclusion is drawn from it.

## 11. Why the saturated branch cannot be removed by complementary-pair rigidity

This is the key negative conclusion.

Suppose `m=qp`, `q<p`, and `r+s=m-1`. If

\[
p\mid b_a,
\qquad a=r\bmod p,
\]

then (22)--(23) give

\[
b_r\equiv b_a b_u\equiv0,
\qquad
b_s\equiv b_a b_w\equiv0\pmod p
\]

**for every admissible `u,w`**, with no condition on `b_u,b_w`.

So after the first-digit factor vanishes, the complementary pair has no extra arithmetic rigidity left to exploit: the common divisor is automatic.

For fixed `n` and `p|m`,

\[
a=r\bmod p=(n-m)\bmod p=n\bmod p.
\tag{36}
\]

Hence the unavoidable saturated set is

\[
\mathcal S_n
:=
\{p>n^{2/3}: p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}\}.
\tag{37}
\]

Moreover, for a prime `p\le n` there is a multiple of `p` in `(n/2,n]` (take the largest multiple `\le n`, with the trivial endpoint case handled by the next multiple when needed). Thus in the large-prime/two-digit range the condition `p|b_{n mod p}` is not an artificial enlargement: it is precisely the reflection-saturated obstruction that the top-half complementary carrier sees.

This proves a methodological impossibility statement:

> A common-divisor theorem whose only extra input is `r+s+1\equiv0 (mod p)` cannot by itself control the saturated primes, because for those primes simultaneous vanishing is an algebraic consequence of the common first base-`p` digit factor.

A successful next argument must use information **outside** that saturated complementary relation: e.g. a pointwise estimate for `b_{n mod p}`, a global interaction between different primes, or an additional invariant not shared by the two reflected factors.

## 12. Narrowest remaining statement that suffices

Let

\[
D_n=\operatorname{lcm}_{n/2<m\le n} d_m.
\]

The preceding analysis gives, without any scan heuristic:

- primes `p\le n^{2/3}` contribute `O(n^{2/3})` to `\log D_n`, by (35);
- nonsaturated primes `p>n^{2/3}` contribute `O(n^{2/3})`, by the certificate `\mathcal C_n` in (30)--(33);
- the only remaining large-prime contribution is the saturated set (37).

Therefore the following statement is sufficient:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{\substack{n^{2/3}<p\le n\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}\log p=o(n).
}
\tag{38}
\]

One may equivalently ask for `O(n^{2/3})` here if the project is aiming for the balanced power-saving scale.

This is strictly narrower than an arbitrary complementary-pair gcd assertion: all genuinely two-digit residual common divisors have already been packed into a single `O(n^{2/3})`-height integer.

It is also essentially the pointwise reflection-saturated obstruction. Indeed Apéry--Lucas gives, for `n=a+vp` in the same large-prime range,

\[
b_n\equiv b_a b_v\pmod p,
\qquad v<n^{1/3},
\]

so the `b_v` branch is again controlled by the same small-index product, leaving `p|b_a=b_{n mod p}` as the hard branch. Thus the complementary carrier does not evade the pointwise obstruction; it isolates it sharply.

## 13. Route-selection conclusion

The requested candidate classes resolve as follows.

### Continuant / recurrence resultant

**Fails after desaturation.** The natural nonzero determinant `K_{r,s}` is divisible by the complementary modulus for symmetry reasons. The explicit `(r,s,p)=(1,3,5)` example shows that its quotient by the forced factor need not contain the target prime.

### Ordinary Casoratian

**Fails.** The reflected solution is congruent to the original one, so the Casoratian vanishes identically modulo the target prime; it is another expression of the universal reflection factor.

### Strong divisibility / classical rank of apparition

**Fails.** Equation (18) rules out strong divisibility, and (19) gives an exact digitwise zero set for `p=5` incompatible with a single rank.

### Aggregate complementary energy

There is a **positive exact substitute**: after saturation, every large prime descends to indices `<n^{1/3}` and hence the entire residual large-prime mass divides the single product `\mathcal C_n` of log height `O(n^{2/3})`.

### What remains

Only the reflection-saturated prime mass (38). Complementarity gives no further constraint on that branch, so the next attack should be aimed directly at (38), not at a more elaborate recurrence resultant for the pair.

That is the sharpest conclusion I can justify from the current recurrence/Lucas structure: the complementary-pair route **does produce a rigorous low-height certificate for every genuinely new common-divisor prime, but it also proves that the remaining saturated class is irreducible by complementarity alone.**
