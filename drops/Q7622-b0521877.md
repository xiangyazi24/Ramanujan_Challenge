# Audit of the rank-five operator, row content, and minimality

## Verdict

With the convention

\[
\mathcal L_5=\sum_{s=0}^5 P_s(t)\Theta^s,\qquad \Theta=t\frac d{dt},
\]

with the polynomial coefficients on the **left**, the claimed result is correct, with two important proof repairs.

1. The displayed rank-five operator is the scalarization of the five-dimensional differential module
   \[
   \boxed{\chi_D\otimes \operatorname{Sym}^4(M)},
   \]
   where \(M\) is the rank-two module of the displayed equation and \(\chi_D\) is the quadratic Kummer module generated by \(\sqrt D\). It is not literally the untwisted \(\operatorname{Sym}^4(M)\). Exact five-state elimination gives the displayed \(P_5,\ldots,P_0\).

2. The determinant
   \[
   \det\bigl(\text{coordinate rows of }W,\Theta W,\ldots,\Theta^4W\bigr)=27648
   \]
   is correct. It is an **observability/cyclic-covector determinant in the formal five-state module**. It is not, by itself, a Wronskian proof that the particular scalar function \(W\) has no lower-order equation. Minimality additionally needs irreducibility/Galois orbit information.

3. The row-content theorem is correct, including composite common divisors and higher valuations:
   \[
   \boxed{\gcd_{0\le \ell\le 10}K_\ell(m)=\gcd(3,m)\qquad(m\ge1).}
   \]
   The same formula holds for the content of the **active recurrence row** \(0\le\ell\le\min(10,m)\). There is a short symbolic proof modulo \(3\); no scan over \(m\) is needed.

4. The local exponent data are correct:
   \[
   t=0:(0,0),\qquad D(t)=0:(0,\tfrac12),\qquad t=\infty:(\tfrac12,\tfrac12).
   \]
   The repeated exponent at \(0\) really has a logarithmic second solution, hence a nontrivial unipotent local monodromy. The base rank-two module is irreducible over \(\mathbf C(t)\) by a clean rational-Riccati residue contradiction. Irreducibility plus the nontrivial unipotent forces the differential Galois group to contain \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), so \(\operatorname{Sym}^4(M)\) is irreducible. The rank-one Kummer twist preserves irreducibility. This supplies the missing ingredient needed to turn the cyclic determinant into a genuine minimal-order theorem for the chosen \(W\).

5. A determinant correction is essential if the proposed proof says the base Galois group is contained in \(\mathrm{SL}_2\). It is not. The determinant character of \(M\) is the quadratic character \(\chi_D\). In fact the corrected argument gives
   \[
   \mathrm{SL}_2\subset G_M,\qquad \det(G_M)=\{\pm1\},
   \]
   hence \(G_M=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_2:\det(g)=\pm1\}\). The untwisted fourth symmetric power has trivial determinant, while
   \[
   \det(\chi_D\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^4M)=\chi_D.
   \]
   This agrees exactly with the \(\Theta^4\)-coefficient of the displayed operator.

No finite-scan inference is used below.

## 2. Exact five-state elimination

## 4. Exact row content

Let

\[
g(m)=\gcd_{0\le\ell\le10}K_\ell(m).
\]

We prove

\[
\boxed{g(m)=\gcd(3,m)\qquad(m\ge1).}
\tag{4.1}
\]

### 4.1 Every common prime is 3 or 43, and 43 is impossible

Since only \(P_5\) has a nonzero constant term,

\[
\boxed{K_0(m)=m^5.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

For \(\ell=1\), direct extraction from (2.14) gives

\[
\boxed{
K_1(m)=
-170m^5+765m^4-1410m^3+1350m^2-657m+129.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Let \(q\) be a prime divisor of \(g(m)\). From (4.2), \(q\mid m\). Reducing (4.3) modulo \(q\) then gives

\[
0\equiv K_1(m)\equiv129\pmod q.
\]

Hence

\[
q\in\{3,43\}.
\tag{4.4}
\]

To remove \(43\), the \(t^2\)-coefficients of \((P_5,P_4,P_3,P_2,P_1,P_0)\) are

\[
(11565,11565,8250,2970,3429,2721).
\]

Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
K_2(0)
&=11565(-2)^5+11565(-2)^4+8250(-2)^3\\
&\quad+2970(-2)^2+3429(-2)+2721\\
&=-243297.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.5}
\]

Since

\[
-243297\equiv-3\not\equiv0\pmod{43},
\tag{4.6}
\]

if \(43\mid m\) then the integer polynomial \(K_2(m)\) satisfies

\[
K_2(m)\equiv K_2(0)\equiv-3\pmod{43},
\]

contradicting \(43\mid g(m)\). Consequently **3 is the only possible prime divisor of the row content**.

This already handles arbitrary composite common divisors at the prime-support level; the only remaining question is the exact \(3\)-adic valuation.

### 4.2 Structural proof that 3 divides every row coefficient when 3 divides m

The cleanest part of the audit is that no list of \(K_\ell\)'s is needed.

Modulo \(3\),

\[

\tag{4.7}

The four inner polynomials in \(P_3,P_2,P_1,P_0\) reduce as

\begin{aligned}

\tag{4.8}

Hence the entire operator has the exact reduction

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L_5\equiv

+t(2\Theta^4+\Theta^3+\Theta^2+2\Theta+1)\Bigr)

Suppose \(m\equiv0\pmod3\). For a fixed \(\ell\), put

\[

From (3.2) and (4.9),

\[
K_\ell(m)\equiv

In characteristic \(3\), Frobenius gives

\[
(t+1)^9=t^9+1.
\tag{4.11}

The right side of (4.10) can therefore have support only in degrees

0,1,9,10.

For \(2\le\ell\le8\), it is automatically zero. For \(\ell=0,9\), one has \(x=0\), so the relevant coefficient is zero. For \(\ell=1,10\), one has \(x=-1\) and

\[

Thus

\[
\boxed{3\mid K_\ell(m)\quad\text{for every }0\le\ell\le10
\quad\text{whenever }3\mid m.}
\tag{4.12}

This proves the lower bound \(3\mid g(m)\) in the divisible case by a symbolic factorization, not by checking finitely many values of \(m\).

## 5. Minimality: corrected proof

The order-five annihilation above does not by itself exclude an accidental lower-order annihilator of the particular chosen scalar solution. Here is a proof that does.

### 5.1 Local exponents of the rank-two equation

Expanding \(\Theta=t\,d/dt\) and dividing by \(t\) gives the ordinary form

\[
4tD\,y''+4(2t^2-51t+1)y'+(t-10)y=0.
\tag{5.1}
\]

At \(t=0\), substitution \(y=t^\rho\) gives

\[
4\rho^2=0,
\]

so the exponents are

\[
(0,0).
\tag{5.2}
\]

Let \(\alpha\) be a simple root of \(D\). For a second-order Fuchsian equation \(a y''+b y'+cy=0\) at a simple zero of \(a\), the exponents are

\[
0,\quad 1-\frac{b(\alpha)}{a'(\alpha)}.
\]

Here

\[
a=4tD,\qquad b=4(2t^2-51t+1).
\]

Using \(\alpha^2=34\alpha-1\),

\[
2\alpha^2-51\alpha+1=17\alpha-1
=\alpha(\alpha-17),
\]

while

\[
a'(\alpha)=4\alpha D'(\alpha)=8\alpha(\alpha-17).
\]

Thus

\[
\frac{b(\alpha)}{a'(\alpha)}=\frac12,
\]

and each root of \(D\) has exponents

\[
(0,\tfrac12).
\tag{5.3}
\]

At infinity put \(y\sim t^{-\rho}\). The leading terms of (5.1) give

\[
4\rho(\rho+1)-8\rho+1
=(2\rho-1)^2,
\]

hence

\[
(\tfrac12,\tfrac12).
\tag{5.4}
\]

These are exactly the exponent sets recorded by the current symbolic local-monodromy source.

### 5.2 Repeated exponent is not enough; nontrivial unipotence must be proved

A repeated exponent does **not** by itself imply a logarithm. Here it is easy to prove the logarithm at \(t=0\).

For a holomorphic solution of (5.1), evaluating the lowest-order term at \(t=0\) gives

\[
4y'(0)-10y(0)=0,
\qquad	ext{i.e.}\qquad y'(0)=\frac52y(0).
\tag{5.5}
\]

Thus the local holomorphic solution space is one-dimensional: once \(y(0)\) is fixed, the differential equation recursively fixes the germ, and if \(y(0)=0\) then also \(y'(0)=0\), hence the solution is zero. Since the indicial root \(0\) has multiplicity two but only one logarithm-free germ exists, the second Frobenius solution contains a logarithm.

Therefore the local monodromy at \(0\) is a **nonidentity unipotent** matrix.

### 5.3 Irreducibility over C(t): rational-Riccati residue contradiction

Suppose the rank-two differential module were reducible over \(\mathbf C(t)\). Equivalently, the scalar operator would have a first-order right factor over \(\mathbf C(t)\), so there would be a nonzero solution \(f\) with

\[
r(t):=\frac{f'(t)}{f(t)}\in\mathbf C(t).
\tag{5.6}
\]

Because (5.1) is regular singular at infinity, \(r(t)=O(1/t)\); in particular \(r\) has no polynomial part.

Now inspect residues of this rational logarithmic derivative.

- At an ordinary finite point, a nonzero solution of a regular linear ODE is holomorphic. A pole of \(r\) there can only come from a zero of \(f\), hence has a positive integer residue. There can be no negative residue, because that would make \(f\) have a pole at an ordinary point.
- At \(t=0\), the available exponent is \(0\), so the finite residue is nonnegative (in fact it is \(0\) for a nonzero Frobenius solution).
- At either root of \(D\), the available exponents are \(0\) and \(1/2\); again the residue is nonnegative.

Thus the sum of all finite residues of \(r\) is nonnegative.

On the other hand, the infinity exponent is \(\rho=1/2\) (or a faster-decaying nonnegative integer shift of it), so

\[
r(t)=-\frac{\rho}{t}+O(t^{-2})
\quad(t\to\infty),
\qquad \rho>0.
\tag{5.7}
\]

For a rational function with no polynomial part, the coefficient of \(1/t\) in its expansion at infinity is exactly the sum of its finite residues. Equation (5.7) therefore says that this sum is \(-\rho<0\), a contradiction.

Hence

\[
\boxed{M\text{ is irreducible over }\mathbf C(t).}
\tag{5.8}
\]

This version is stronger and safer than an argument that uses Galois conjugacy of the two roots of \(D\): the latter would only immediately constrain a line defined over \(\mathbf Q(t)\), whereas minimality needs irreducibility after extending constants to \(\mathbf C\).

### 5.4 Differential Galois group and the determinant character

In the \((y,z=\Theta y)\) first-order system, the trace is \(-c\). Hence the determinant of a fundamental matrix satisfies

\[
\Theta\log\det Y=-c=-\frac12\Theta\log D,
\]

so

\[
\det Y=\text{constant}\cdot D^{-1/2}.
\tag{5.9}
\]

Thus the determinant character of the rank-two monodromy is precisely the quadratic Kummer character \(\chi_D\). In particular, local monodromy around a root of \(D\) has determinant \(-1\). Therefore the base group is **not** a subgroup of \(\mathrm{SL}_2\).

Let \(G\) be the Zariski closure of monodromy. By §5.2, \(G\) contains a nontrivial unipotent. The Zariski closure of the powers of such an element contains a one-parameter unipotent subgroup \(U\). It has a unique fixed line in \(\mathbf C^2\). By irreducibility (5.8), some \(g\in G\) moves that line. Then \(U\) and \(gUg^{-1}\) are two one-parameter unipotent subgroups with distinct fixed lines; after a change of basis they generate the two opposite root groups, whose Zariski closure contains \(\mathrm{SL}_2\). Hence

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{SL}_2\subset G.}
\tag{5.10}
\]

Since (5.9) makes the determinant image exactly \(\{\pm1\}\), and a local loop about a root of \(D\) realizes \(-1\), one may sharpen this to

\[
\boxed{
G=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf C):\det(g)=\pm1\}.
}
\tag{5.11}
\]

The equality (5.11) is not needed for minimality; the inclusion (5.10) is enough.

### 5.5 Symmetric fourth power and the Kummer twist

The restriction of \(\operatorname{Sym}^4(\mathbf C^2)\) to \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) is the irreducible highest-weight-four representation of dimension five. From (5.10),

\[
\operatorname{Sym}^4(M)
\]

is therefore irreducible.

The function in the question is not \(y^4\) but

\[
W=\sqrt D\,y^4.
\]

The line generated by \(\sqrt D\) is the rank-one Kummer module \(\chi_D\), with

\[
\frac{\Theta\sqrt D}{\sqrt D}=c.
\]

Thus the actual rank-five module is

\[
\boxed{
M_5=\chi_D\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^4(M).
}
\tag{5.12}
\]

Tensoring an irreducible representation by a one-dimensional character preserves irreducibility, so \(M_5\) is irreducible.

This is the twist hypothesis that must be explicit in a correct proof.

The determinant bookkeeping is also informative. For a two-dimensional representation,

\[
\det(\operatorname{Sym}^4M)=(\det M)^{10}.
\]

Since \(\det M=\chi_D\) has order two,

\[
\det(\operatorname{Sym}^4M)=1.
\]

Twisting a rank-five module by \(\chi_D\) multiplies its determinant by \(\chi_D^5=\chi_D\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{\det M_5=\chi_D.}
\tag{5.13}
\]

This exactly matches the scalar operator. A monic fifth-order equation

\[
\Theta^5+a_4\Theta^4+\cdots=0
\]

has companion trace \(-a_4\). Here \(a_4=5c\), so its determinant solution is

\[
\exp\!\left(-\int5c\,d\log t\right)=D^{-5/2},
\]

which has the same quadratic monodromy character \(\chi_D\).

Accordingly:

- a proof that asserts the **base** group is \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) is missing/correcting the determinant character;
- a proof that identifies the rank-five object with plain \(\operatorname{Sym}^4M\) is missing the \(\chi_D\) twist;
- neither defect harms irreducibility once stated correctly.

### 5.6 Minimal scalar order of W

Let \(y\) be a nonzero solution (in the Apéry application, the normalized germ has \(y(0)=1\)). Since \(G\) contains \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), the \(G\)-span of the nonzero pure fourth power represented by \(y^4\) is all of \(\operatorname{Sym}^4(\mathbf C^2)\). Multiplication by the one-dimensional character \(\chi_D\) does not change the span dimension. Thus the differential-Galois conjugates of

\[
W=\sqrt D\,y^4
\]

span a five-dimensional vector space over the constants.

Any scalar differential equation over \(\mathbf C(t)\) annihilating \(W\) must annihilate all of its differential-Galois conjugates. Therefore its order is at least five. Section 2 supplies an equation of order five. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{\min,\mathbf C(t)}(W)=5.
}
\tag{5.14}
\]

A fortiori the minimal order over \(\mathbf Q(t)\) is also five.

This is the rigorous place where irreducibility is used. The constant determinant \(27648\) in (2.6) is the exact cyclic scalarization certificate, but it should not be asked to do the Galois-orbit job by itself.

## 7. What should be changed in a theorem statement/proof

A safe theorem-grade formulation is:

> **Proposition.** Let \(y\ne0\) solve
> \[
> 4D\Theta^2y+4t(t-17)\Theta y+t(t-10)y=0,
> \qquad D=t^2-34t+1,
> \]
> and choose either local branch of \(\sqrt D\). Put \(W=\sqrt D\,y^4\). In left \(\Theta\)-normal form, \(W\) is annihilated by
> \[
> \mathcal L_5=\sum_{s=0}^5P_s(t)\Theta^s
> \]
> with the six displayed polynomials \(P_s\). This operator has minimal order five over \(\mathbf C(t)\), hence over \(\mathbf Q(t)\). If \(W=\sum_{m\ge0}h_mt^m\) and
> \[
> K_\ell(m)=\sum_{s=0}^5[t^\ell]P_s(t)(m-\ell)^s,
> \]
> then, for every \(m\ge1\),
> \[
> \gcd_{0\le\ell\le10}K_\ell(m)
> =\gcd_{0\le\ell\le\min(10,m)}K_\ell(m)
> =\gcd(3,m).
> \]

The proof should explicitly contain the following four ingredients.

1. **Five-state connection (2.3)** and the cyclic determinant \(27648\). This proves the displayed order-five scalarization.
2. **A real nontrivial-unipotent argument**, e.g. (5.5). Repeated exponents alone are insufficient.
3. **Irreducibility over \(\mathbf C(t)\)**, e.g. the rational-Riccati residue contradiction in §5.3, followed by the two-unipotent-subgroups argument giving \(\mathrm{SL}_2\subset G\). Merely saying "rank two irreducible, therefore Sym4 irreducible" is not logically sufficient for an arbitrary irreducible rank-two group; the unipotent is what rules out finite/dihedral-type projective monodromy and forces the \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) derived group.
4. **The Kummer twist** \(\chi_D\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^4M\) and determinant bookkeeping (5.9)--(5.13). Do not state that the base group lies in \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), and do not call \(W\) an untwisted fourth symmetric power.

For the row-content theorem, replace the phrase "if \(3\mid m\), every \(K_\ell(m)\) is visibly divisible by 3" with the exact operator factorization (4.9)--(4.11). That turns the proof into a short symbolic argument and removes any appearance of a finite scan.
