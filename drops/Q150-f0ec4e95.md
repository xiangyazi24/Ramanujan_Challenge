# Non-stationary expansion on \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\): Varjú, Bourgain–Gamburd, and the Apéry cocycle

## Executive verdict

The cited expansion results do **not** apply to the actual Apéry orbit. The decisive mismatch is more basic than checking a Bourgain–Gamburd subgroup hypothesis:

- Varjú's *Random walks in compact groups* studies convolution powers of a **fixed probability measure**, equivalently products of independent identically distributed random increments. It does not prove mixing for a prescribed deterministic sequence of matrices.
- The Apéry recurrence supplies exactly one matrix at time \(n\). Its law is a Dirac mass. Products of Dirac masses remain Dirac masses, and the corresponding operators are unitary permutations. There is no probabilistic smoothing to which a spectral-gap theorem could be applied.
- Even after artificially randomizing the parameter \(n\), polynomial dependence does not make Bourgain–Gamburd non-concentration automatic. In the natural \(\mathrm{SL}_2\)-gauge, the entire one-step Apéry family lies in a single Borel coset, so the strongest one-step escape condition fails maximally.
- Known multi-modulus expansion theorems do give joint mixing modulo \(q=\prod p_i\), but only for random words in one **fixed finite integral generating set**. They do not control the Apéry clock, its prescribed moving generators, its coordinate-specific singular/reset times, or the incomplete CRT interval \([0,X^2)\) that appears in \((\mathrm{HM})_k\).
- Kowalski's definable-set theorem is potentially relevant for a **fixed-length block**—precisely the regime of the current gap polynomials \(N_h(x)\)—but not for a product whose length grows with \(p\), and not for the horizontal initial-segment problem.

Thus the live statement would be a genuinely new **deterministic clocked-cocycle discrepancy theorem**, and for the full P3.2 problem a genuinely new **horizontal clocked super-approximation theorem**. Neither is a formal consequence of Varjú, Bourgain–Gamburd, Bourgain–Varjú, or Kowalski.

Repository audit basis: [`proof.tex`](https://github.com/xiangyazi24/Ramanujan_Challenge/blob/83428bfff93966b8655f5eae6d1e41fc33b892a2/problems/3.2/proof.tex) and [`AVENUES.md`](https://github.com/xiangyazi24/Ramanujan_Challenge/blob/83428bfff93966b8655f5eae6d1e41fc33b892a2/problems/3.2/AVENUES.md) at main commit `83428bfff93966b8655f5eae6d1e41fc33b892a2`. The current manuscript itself describes the deterministic interval-product problem as requiring a non-concentration theorem for the Apéry cocycle; it does not contain a theorem converting the moving cocycle into an expanding random walk.

## 1. First put the cocycle in the correct group

Write

$$
P(n)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5
$$

and recall

$$
(n+1)^3u_{n+1}=P(n)u_n-n^3u_{n-1}.
$$

On the projective state \([u_n:u_{n-1}]\), the displayed transition is represented by

$$
A_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)&-n^3\\
(n+1)^3&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det A_n=n^3(n+1)^3.
$$

There are two immediate corrections to the formulation in the question.

1. As written, \(A_n\) is a \(\mathrm{PGL}_2\)-element only when \(n\not\equiv0,-1\pmod p\). In particular, \(A_0\) is rank one. Therefore the literal family \(T_0,T_1,\ldots\) is not a subset of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\), and no set containing \(T_0\) can generate \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\).
2. The current repository uses the factorial gauge, which removes the \((n+1)^3\) denominator and is the clean way to enter \(\mathrm{SL}_2\).

Put

$$
c_n=(n!)^3u_n.
$$

For \(n<p\), multiplication by \((n!)^3\) does not change whether \(u_n\) vanishes modulo \(p\). The recurrence becomes

$$
c_{n+1}=P(n)c_n-n^6c_{n-1},
$$

so

$$
M_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)&-n^6\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det M_n=n^6.
$$

For \(n\ne0\), define

$$
S_n=n^{-3}M_n
=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)/n^3&-n^3\\
n^{-3}&0
\end{pmatrix}
\in\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p).
\tag{1.1}
$$

Projectively, \(S_n\) and \(M_n\) induce the same map. If

$$
v_n=[c_n:c_{n-1}],
$$

then

$$
v_{n+1}=S_nv_n,
\qquad
v_n=S_{n-1}\cdots S_1[5:1].
\tag{1.2}
$$

In this convention, \(b_n\equiv0\pmod p\) means \(v_n=[0:1]\). In the shifted convention used in the question, \([b_{n+1}:b_n]\), the target is \([1:0]\). It is one point of \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\), not a positive-density subset.

I did not find in the audited repository a proved statement that the corrected set \(\{S_1,\ldots,S_{p-2}\}\) generates \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) for almost all \(p\). That assertion may be a computational observation or a separate target. Nothing below depends on denying it: even granting full generation, the expansion argument still does not go through.

## 2. What Varjú's theorem actually says

Varjú's paper is [*Random walks in compact groups*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.1745), submitted in 2012 and published in *Documenta Mathematica* 18 (2013), 1137–1175. Its opening setup is explicit: \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) are **independent identically distributed** random elements of a compact group, and the object is the law of

$$
X_\ell\cdots X_1.
$$

Equivalently, for one fixed probability measure \(\mu\), it studies convolution powers \(\mu^{*\ell}\) and the spectral gap of the associated convolution operator. The profinite results also concern a fixed measure or a fixed generating set projected through a family of quotients. They are not theorems about an arbitrary deterministic sequence

$$
S_1,S_2,S_3,\ldots.
$$

The identifiable Breuillard–Varjú cutoff paper is [*Cut-off phenomenon for the ax+b Markov chain over a finite field*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09053), *Probability Theory and Related Fields* 184 (2022), 85–113. It studies

$$
x_{n+1}=ax_n+b_n,
$$

where \(a\) is fixed and the \(b_n\) are i.i.d. random variables. I could not identify a Breuillard–Varjú paper with the exact title “Cut-off phenomenon for random walks on compact groups.” In any case, the joint cutoff result also contains fresh independent randomness at every step and does not cover the deterministic Apéry schedule.

### 2.1 The exact non-stationary operator test

There is an elementary inhomogeneous statement that clarifies the boundary. Let \(G\) be finite and let \(\mu_j\) be probability measures on \(G\). If the centered convolution operators satisfy

$$
\|T_{\mu_j}\|_{L^2_0(G)\to L^2_0(G)}\le1-\delta
$$

uniformly in \(j\), then

$$
\|T_{\mu_\ell}\cdots T_{\mu_1}\|_{L^2_0\to L^2_0}
\le(1-\delta)^\ell.
\tag{2.1}
$$

That is the elementary form of non-stationary mixing one could hope to use.

For the actual Apéry sequence, however,

$$
\mu_j=\delta_{S_j}.
$$

Right or left translation by a single group element is unitary, so

$$
\|T_{\delta_{S_j}}\|_{L^2_0\to L^2_0}=1,
$$

and

$$
\delta_{S_\ell}*\cdots*\delta_{S_1}
=
\delta_{S_\ell\cdots S_1}.
\tag{2.2}
$$

There is no flattening at all. On \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\), every \(S_j\) is likewise a permutation; a point mass remains a point mass.

Randomizing only the starting index does not repair the hypothesis. The block

$$
S_{x+\ell-1}\cdots S_x
$$

then depends on one random variable \(x\); its \(\ell\) factors are maximally dependent, not independent increments.

Therefore the answer to Question 1 is **no**: the cited Varjú and Breuillard–Varjú theorems do not apply to the actual Apéry product.

## 3. Bourgain–Gamburd: what is required and why polynomiality is not enough

The 2008 Bourgain–Gamburd theorem, [*Uniform expansion bounds for Cayley graphs of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\)*](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2008.167.625), is usually stated as follows: take a **fixed** finite set of integral matrices whose subgroup in \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf Z)\) is non-elementary; then, away from finitely many primes, the corresponding Cayley graphs of the reductions are expanders. The final theorem is not literally “every step distribution whose support generates a free subgroup.” Freeness or large girth is one mechanism used in the expansion machine to obtain early escape, together with product growth and quasirandomness.

A useful abstract formulation of the Bourgain–Gamburd input is an escape estimate such as

$$
\sup_{H<G_p}\mu_p^{(*\ell)}(H)\le |G_p|^{-\kappa}
\tag{3.1}
$$

for all proper subgroups \(H<G_p\) at an appropriate pre-mixing time \(\ell\), followed by an \(L^2\)-flattening step. The classification of approximate subgroups and the large dimensions of nontrivial representations then amplify (3.1) into a spectral gap.

Two points are crucial here.

1. **Generation is not expansion.** Knowing that the union of the matrices \(S_n\) generates \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) proves reachability by arbitrary words in that alphabet. It gives no quantitative information about a random walk unless a probability measure on the alphabet is specified, and it gives no information at all about the one prescribed word \(S_{n-1}\cdots S_1\).
2. **Polynomial dependence is not subgroup escape.** A polynomial or rational map can lie identically in a proper subgroup, a proper subgroup coset, or another exceptional subvariety. Even a Zariski-dense union of values does not automatically give a uniform estimate for all proper subgroups after the needed number of convolutions.

### 3.1 An explicit Apéry-specific one-step obstruction

The normalized matrix (1.1) satisfies, for every \(t\ne0\),

$$
S_t[0:1]=[1:0].
\tag{3.2}
$$

Let \(B\) be the Borel subgroup stabilizing \([0:1]\). From (3.2), for any \(s,t\ne0\),

$$
S_s^{-1}S_t\in B.
\tag{3.3}
$$

Thus the entire one-parameter family \(\{S_t:t\in\mathbf F_p^\times\}\) is contained in one left coset of a proper Borel subgroup.

Suppose one invents the randomized surrogate

$$
\mu_p=\frac1{p-1}\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}\delta_{S_t}.
\tag{3.4}
$$

Then

$$
\operatorname{supp}(\widetilde\mu_p*\mu_p)\subset B.
\tag{3.5}
$$

Consequently the one-step convolution operator has singular norm one on the nonconstant subspace: \(B\)-coset functions provide nonconstant invariant vectors for the symmetrized operator. So even the artificially randomized one-step family does **not** satisfy an automatic one-step spectral-gap condition.

This does not prove that \(\mu_p^{*L}\) can never mix. Nonnormal products may escape after blocking. It says that a successful randomized argument would first have to prove a genuinely nontrivial block theorem. For independent parameters, one would study the word map

$$
W_L(t_1,\ldots,t_L)=S_{t_L}\cdots S_{t_1}.
\tag{3.6}
$$

Since \(\dim \mathrm{SL}_2=3\), a dimension count already says that \(L=1\) or \(2\) cannot give a dominant map onto the group; \(L\ge3\) is the first possible group-level dominance range. One would then need to prove dominance, control exceptional fibers, and prove uniform escape from every proper subgroup and subgroup coset.

But this randomized model is not the Apéry orbit. The actual consecutive block

$$
S_{x+L-1}\cdots S_x
\tag{3.7}
$$

depends on the single parameter \(x\), so as \(x\) varies it remains a one-dimensional algebraic family inside the three-dimensional group. The current repository correctly treats such interval products through gap polynomials and incidence/non-concentration questions, not as an i.i.d. Cayley walk.

Therefore the answer to Question 2 is:

- the stated “free subgroup” summary is not the exact final hypothesis of the Bourgain–Gamburd theorem;
- full generation modulo \(p\) is far from sufficient;
- algebraic non-concentration is **not** automatic for polynomial cocycles;
- the Apéry one-step family has an explicit Borel-coset concentration that must first be overcome by a new blocked analysis.

## 4. Why random mixing would not prove equidistribution of the deterministic Apéry orbit

Let

$$
G_p(n)=S_{n-1}\cdots S_1,
\qquad
v_n=G_p(n)[5:1].
$$

The zero set is, up to the harmless endpoint convention,

$$
Z_p=\{1\le n<p:v_n=[0:1]\}.
\tag{4.1}
$$

A random-walk theorem would concern a random word

$$
S_{T_{n-1}}\cdots S_{T_1}
$$

for independent random choices \(T_i\), or a convolution power of a fixed measure. It does not imply anything about the single word

$$
S_{n-1}S_{n-2}\cdots S_1.
$$

This is the standard annealed-versus-quenched distinction: branching over all random words may expand while one selected branch is arbitrarily biased. An expander Cayley graph controls the ensemble of paths generated by the transition kernel, not every deterministic path through that graph.

### 4.1 The correct target density

Uniform measure on \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\) assigns mass

$$
\frac1{p+1}
$$

to the one-point zero target. Hence perfect equidistribution of the \(p+O(1)\) prefix states would predict

$$
|Z_p|\approx\frac{p}{p+1}\approx1,
\tag{4.2}
$$

not a positive constant fraction of \(p\). This agrees with the repository's computation that the mean of \(Z(p)\) is approximately one and that the reflection-pair count resembles \(\mathrm{Poisson}(1/2)\).

But even for a genuinely random rapidly mixing chain, endpoint mixing is weaker than an occupation theorem. To count visits to a rare target over \(p\) times, one must control correlations of the indicators

$$
\mathbf 1_{\{X_n=[0:1]\}}.
$$

For a random chain, a Poisson-type law is plausible; it is not a deterministic bound for every realization. For the actual Apéry path there is no ambient probability space at all.

A single-prime theorem that would really address the deterministic orbit has the form

$$
\sup_{y\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)}
\left|
\#\{1\le n<p:v_n=y\}-\frac{p-1}{p+1}
\right|
\ll p^{1-\delta}.
\tag{4.3}
$$

This would imply

$$
Z(p)\ll1+p^{1-\delta}.
$$

The current unconditional repository bound \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\) is on the scale \(\delta=1/3\). A square-root discrepancy theorem would correspond to \(\delta=1/2\). A uniform \(O(1)\) zero bound would require essentially constant discrepancy for this particular one-point target, much stronger than ordinary square-root cancellation.

Proving (4.3) is a new deterministic exponential-sum/incidence theorem for the ordered cocycle. It is not implied by an \(O(\log p)\) mixing time for a randomized product.

Therefore the answer to Question 3 is: random mixing gives the **heuristic** density \(1/(p+1)\), hence mean zero count about one, but it does not prove equidistribution or an occupation bound for the deterministic Apéry prefix sequence.

## 5. There is a multi-prime Bourgain–Gamburd theory—but it is the wrong dynamical object

There are strong multi-modulus expansion theorems.

- Bourgain–Varjú, [*Expansion in \(\mathrm{SL}_d(\mathbf Z/q\mathbf Z)\), \(q\) arbitrary*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.3365), fixes a finite symmetric set \(S\subset\mathrm{SL}_d(\mathbf Z)\) generating a Zariski-dense subgroup and proves expansion for its reductions modulo arbitrary \(q\).
- Salehi Golsefidy–Varjú, [*Expansion in perfect groups*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.4900), gives the corresponding squarefree-modulus criterion in terms of perfect connected Zariski closure.
- Varjú's [squarefree-ideal theorem](https://doi.org/10.4171/JEMS/302) supplies another relevant form over number fields.

For squarefree

$$
q=p_1\cdots p_k,
$$

CRT identifies, up to the relevant congruence image,

$$
\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf Z/q\mathbf Z)
\cong
\prod_{i=1}^k\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_{p_i}).
$$

Thus these theorems are legitimately a “multi-prime Bourgain–Gamburd”: a random word in one fixed alphabet becomes jointly equidistributed in the product quotient after \(O(\log q)\) steps when there is a uniform gap.

### 5.1 Why this does not model the Apéry events

The Apéry problem violates the setup in several independent ways.

1. **Moving alphabet.** The letter at time \(n\) is \(S_n\); there is no fixed finite set of integral generators from which letters are sampled independently.
2. **Prescribed word.** The actual orbit uses the unique schedule \(1,2,\ldots,n-1\). Expansion of all random words in an alphabet does not control this word.
3. **Different local prefix lengths.** For the horizontal event attached to one integer \(m\), the \(p\)-coordinate is tested at the residue \(m\bmod p\). For a tuple of primes, the coordinates therefore use different prefix lengths.
4. **Coordinate-specific singular/reset times.** The original projective transition degenerates at \(n\equiv0,-1\pmod p\), while the factorial gauge is unavailable at \(n=0\). Across a product of primes, these singular times occur in different coordinates. The Cartier/Lucas block law supplies arithmetic resets; this is not a walk in a fixed semisimple product group.
5. **Incomplete CRT interval.** The P3.2 moments average \(m\) only over \([0,X^2)\). For three or more shell primes, the full CRT modulus is already much larger than this interval.

One can expose the issue by adjoining a clock. Formally, away from singular residues, a local skew product would look like

$$
(r,g)\longmapsto(r+1,S_r g).
\tag{5.1}
$$

Across a modulus \(q\), the clock is \(r\in\mathbf Z/q\mathbf Z\) and each prime coordinate reads \(r\bmod p\). But (5.1) is one deterministic permutation. Its Koopman operator is unitary. A graph generated only by this update has no branching mechanism from which an expander gap could emerge. Adding the genuine Cartier resets yields a more accurate finite automaton, but it remains a deterministic clocked automaton rather than a random Cayley walk.

### 5.2 The exact horizontal target in the repository's notation

For shell primes \(X<p\le2X\), set

$$
K_X(m)=\#\{p\in(X,2X]:m\bmod p\in Z_p\},
$$

and

$$
\lambda_X=\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac{Z(p)}p.
$$

Then

$$
\sum_{m<X^2}(K_X(m))_k
=
\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\in(X,2X]\\
\text{ordered, distinct}}}
N(p_1,\ldots,p_k),
\tag{5.2}
$$

where

$$
N(p_1,\ldots,p_k)
=
\#\{m<X^2:m\bmod p_i\in Z_{p_i}\ \forall i\}.
$$

The independence-scale statement is

$$
\sum_{m<X^2}(K_X(m))_k
\ll X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^k.
\tag{5.3}
$$

For a fixed tuple and \(q=p_1\cdots p_k\), the expected fraction of zero-residue tuples whose CRT representative falls in \([0,X^2)\) is

$$
\frac{X^2}{q}.
\tag{5.4}
$$

At \(k=2\), \(q\asymp X^2\), so complete-period geometry is still available up to constants; this is why \((\mathrm{HM})_2\) is unconditional. At \(k=3\), \(q\asymp X^3\), and the missing factor is already \(X^{-1}\). Every relevant triple is a boundary/CRT-phase question.

Known expansion modulo \(q\) controls a random word in a fixed group quotient. It does not prove (5.4) for the clocked Apéry zero sets in the initial interval. A theorem that did prove (5.3)—for one fixed \(k>6\) under the currently proved \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\)—would essentially be the missing higher-moment theorem itself.

A suitable name for the genuinely new result is:

> **Horizontal clocked super-approximation for the Apéry cocycle.** Prove initial-segment discrepancy, averaged over shell-prime tuples, for the deterministic map
> 
> $$
> m\longmapsto
> \bigl(G_p(m\bmod p)[5:1]\bigr)_{p\in Q},
> $$
> 
> with an error strong enough to imply (5.3), or directly prove the equivalent atom-tail estimate.

This is much stronger and structurally different from existing super-approximation.

Therefore the answer to Question 4 is:

- **yes**, there is multi-prime expansion for fixed integral generators and random words;
- **no**, it does not give the needed horizontal Apéry theorem;
- adapting it to the moving clocked cocycle, the Cartier resets, and the incomplete CRT interval would be genuinely new.

## 6. Kowalski's definable-set theorem: fixed blocks yes, the full cocycle no

Kowalski's [*Exponential sums over definable subsets of finite fields*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0504316), *Israel Journal of Mathematics* 160 (2007), 219–251, treats subsets defined by a **fixed first-order formula in the language of rings**. The complexity of the formula is fixed while the finite field varies.

A matrix product does not become uniformly definable merely because each individual factor has polynomial coefficients. The issue is the number of iterations.

### 6.1 The regime where it can apply

For a fixed block length \(h\), the interval product

$$
M(x+h-1)\cdots M(x+1)
\tag{6.1}
$$

has entries that are polynomials in \(x\) of degree \(O(h)\). The repository's return polynomial \(N_h(x)\) is exactly a fixed matrix coefficient of such a product and has degree \(3(h-1)\). For **fixed \(h\)** and varying \(p\), the locus

$$
N_h(x)=0
$$

is an algebraic, hence definable, family. Kowalski/Weil/Deligne-style estimates may therefore be applicable to complete sums involving a fixed collection of such block equations, subject to the usual nondegeneracy and geometric irreducibility checks.

This is compatible with the current gap-polynomial and fixed-\(H\) Chebotarev work in the repository.

### 6.2 The regimes it does not cover automatically

It does not directly cover any of the following.

1. **Variable product length.** The condition that a product of \(n\) matrices sends \([5:1]\) to the target uses a formula whose number of factors and auxiliary variables grows with \(n\). First-order ring language has no transitive-closure operator that packages arbitrary recurrence iteration into one fixed formula.
2. **Mixing-scale blocks.** Even \(h=C\log p\) has complexity growing with \(p\). A theorem with constants depending on a fixed formula does not automatically remain useful in that range.
3. **Full prefixes.** For \(n\asymp p\), the degree and logical complexity are enormous; “polynomial coefficient at each step” supplies no bounded-complexity description of the prefix map \(n\mapsto G_p(n)\).
4. **Initial intervals.** Kowalski emphasizes as a first application that no fixed ring-language formula defines a nontrivial interval in \(\mathbf Z/p\mathbf Z\) for infinitely many primes. The horizontal set \([0,X^2)\subset\mathbf Z/q\mathbf Z\) is exactly an ordered initial segment, not an algebraic subset of a finite field.
5. **Cross-characteristic correlation.** A one-field exponential-sum estimate does not by itself correlate the distinguished Apéry orbits in different characteristics or produce the missing CRT factor \(X^2/(p_1\cdots p_k)\).

A plausible use of Kowalski's framework would be only one component of a larger new argument:

- encode a fixed-length block by a bounded-complexity algebraic family;
- prove the relevant geometric monodromy/nondegeneracy;
- obtain complete finite-field sums;
- then add a separate completion, dispersion, or compatible-system argument to handle growing blocks, incomplete intervals, and products of characteristics.

The last two steps are precisely the missing work. Thus the answer to Question 5 is: **fixed-block applicability is plausible; direct applicability to the full polynomial-coefficient product or the horizontal moment problem is no.**

## 7. Three theorem levels that must not be conflated

The discussion becomes clean if one separates three increasingly strong statements.

### Level A: annealed block expansion

Define the artificial random measure (3.4), choose independent parameters \(t_i\), and prove for some bounded block length \(L\) that

$$
\|\mu_p^{*L}*f\|_2\le(1-\varepsilon)\|f\|_2
$$

on an appropriate quotient or after further Bourgain–Gamburd flattening, uniformly in \(p\).

Because of the Borel-coset obstruction, this already requires a nontrivial word-map calculation. It would be an interesting benchmark and might reveal useful algebraic identities. It says nothing by itself about the deterministic ordered parameters \(1,2,3,\ldots\).

### Level B: quenched single-prime discrepancy

Prove a bound such as (4.3), or the corresponding deterministic matrix-coefficient/exponential-sum estimate, for the actual sequence

$$
G_p(n)=S_{n-1}\cdots S_1.
$$

This would be a genuinely new theorem on a non-autonomous polynomial cocycle. It could improve the current pointwise zero bound and the projective energy estimates. The current repository's incidence formulation and its request for non-concentration of interval products live at this level.

It still does not imply horizontal independence: sparse sets in each modulus can align at one common integer across many primes.

### Level C: horizontal quenched discrepancy

Prove (5.3), a conditioned variance contraction, an exponential-moment inequality, or the equivalent pointwise atom-tail estimate for the product of prime clocks.

This is the level needed for all \(n\) in P3.2. It must rule out cross-prime alignment of the distinguished orbit, not merely show that every individual orbit looks uniform. Known multi-modulus expanders do this for random words in fixed generators; doing it for the deterministic Apéry clock would be new.

The implications are only

$$
\text{Level C}\Longrightarrow\text{the required higher moments},
$$

not

$$
\text{Level A}\Longrightarrow\text{Level B}\Longrightarrow\text{Level C}.
$$

Each arrow would itself require a derandomization or cross-characteristic theorem.

## 8. Direct answers to the five questions

### 1. Does Varjú's theorem on varying products apply?

**No.** The cited Varjú paper uses a fixed law and i.i.d. increments. The actual Apéry product is deterministic, with step law \(\delta_{S_n}\) at time \(n\); every transition operator has norm one and the product law remains a point mass. The cited Breuillard–Varjú cutoff result likewise has i.i.d. noise.

### 2. Is Bourgain–Gamburd non-concentration automatic for a polynomial cocycle?

**No.** Generation is not a spectral gap, and polynomiality is not escape from subgroups. In the corrected \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) gauge, every one-step Apéry matrix sends \([0:1]\) to \([1:0]\), so all one-step matrices lie in one Borel coset and all pairwise differences lie in a proper Borel subgroup. A blocked randomized word-map theorem would have to be proved; it would not address the prescribed consecutive word without another argument.

### 3. Would \(O(\log p)\) mixing give \(|Z_p|\)?

It would predict target mass \(1/(p+1)\), hence about one visit in \(p\) times. But it would concern random endpoints and, with extra work, random occupation counts. It does not prove discrepancy for the deterministic prefix sequence. A deterministic estimate such as (4.3) is the needed new theorem.

### 4. Is there a multi-prime Bourgain–Gamburd?

**Yes for fixed generators and random words:** Bourgain–Varjú and Salehi Golsefidy–Varjú provide expansion modulo composite/squarefree moduli, hence joint product-group mixing through CRT. **No as an application to this problem:** the Apéry steps move with the clock, the local coordinates use different prefix lengths and reset at different residues, and \((\mathrm{HM})_k\) is an incomplete-CRT initial-segment statement. A clocked horizontal analogue would be new and essentially as strong as the desired higher moment.

### 5. Does Kowalski's definable-subset theorem apply?

**Only to fixed-complexity block loci.** For fixed \(h\), the gap-polynomial condition \(N_h(x)=0\) is algebraic and is a plausible input to Kowalski/Deligne methods. The full prefix product has growing iteration complexity; the initial interval is not ring-definable; and no cross-prime independence follows. Extending fixed-block estimates into the horizontal regime would require new sheaf/Cartier compatibility and incomplete-sum machinery.

- Varjú 2012/2013 applied directly to \(S_{n-1}\cdots S_1\);
- Breuillard–Varjú cutoff applied directly to the recurrence;
- “the matrices generate \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), therefore the prescribed orbit mixes”;
- “polynomial coefficients make subgroup non-concentration automatic”;
- random one-prime mixing as a proof of deterministic \(Z(p)\);
- existing composite-modulus super-approximation as a proof of \((\mathrm{HM})_3\) or higher;
- Kowalski definability applied to arbitrary-length prefix products or the CRT initial interval.

### Live, but genuinely new

1. **Randomized block benchmark.** Determine the least \(L\) for which the independent-parameter word map (3.6) is dominant and prove uniform subgroup escape. This tests whether the Apéry family has hidden group-theoretic degeneracy beyond the one-step Borel coset.
2. **Deterministic single-prime cocycle discrepancy.** Prove square-root-type cancellation, or the weaker energy estimate actually needed by the repository, for consecutive interval products (3.7). This is closer to the current projective-incidence route than a classical random-walk theorem.
3. **Horizontal clocked super-approximation.** Construct a common arithmetic object—possibly using Cartier correspondences, a compatible sheaf, or a transfer identity—that gives multilinear cancellation for distinct characteristics and the incomplete CRT interval. This is the route that could imply \((\mathrm{HM})_k\) or the atom-tail theorem.

The third item is the exact horizontal wall. Calling it “multi-prime Bourgain–Gamburd” is a useful aspiration, but it should be recorded as a new theorem to prove, not as an existing theorem whose hypotheses merely remain to be checked.
