## 1. Determinantal-divisor bookkeeping

Let
\[
\Delta_j(J_N)=\gcd\{\det A_{I,J}:|I|=|J|=j\},
\qquad \Delta_0=1.
\]
For the Smith invariants
\[
d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_N
\]
we have
\[
\Delta_j=\prod_{i=1}^j d_i.
\]
Set
\[
k=n-q,
\qquad
B_k=\prod_{i=1}^k\beta_i(n).
\]

### 1.1 Every \(M(c)\) is an actual \(k\times k\) principal minor

Delete from the vertex set \(\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}\) the \(q\) vertices
\[
c-1,\ 2c-1,\ldots,\ qc-1.
\]
For a valid cut \(qc\le n\), the retained tridiagonal matrix splits into the contiguous blocks
\[
[0,c-2],\ [c,2c-2],\ldots,
[(q-1)c,qc-2],\ [qc,n-1].
\]
Their lengths are
\[
c-1,\ldots,c-1,n-qc,
\]
so their total size is
\[
q(c-1)+(n-qc)=n-q=k.
\]
Thus the determinant of this principal minor is exactly
\[
M(c)=\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}K_{c-1}(jc)\,K_{n-qc}(qc),
\]
with \(K_0=1\) handling both endpoint possibilities \(c=1\) and \(qc=n\).

Therefore
\[
\boxed{\Delta_k(J_n)\mid M(c)\quad\text{for every valid }c.}
\tag{1}
\]
Taking the gcd over the moving cuts gives only
\[
\Delta_k(J_n)\mid\Gamma;
\]
no equality is asserted or needed.

### 1.2 Proof that \(B_k\mid\Gamma\)

By hypothesis \(\beta_i(n)\mid d_i(J_n)\) for every \(i\), hence
\[
B_k=\prod_{i=1}^k\beta_i(n)\mid
\prod_{i=1}^k d_i(J_n)=\Delta_k(J_n).
\]
Together with (1),
\[
\boxed{B_k\mid\Delta_k(J_n)\mid\Gamma.}
\tag{2}
\]
So the first proposed step is valid, and \(C\) is an integer.

## 2. The normalization really does retain \(D=d_k(J_n)\)

Write
\[
u_i:=\frac{d_i(J_n)}{\beta_i(n)}\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\]
From (2), write
\[
\Gamma=\Delta_k T=B_k\left(\prod_{i=1}^k u_i\right)T
\]
for some integer \(T\).  Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
C
&=\beta_k\frac{\Gamma}{B_k}\\
&=\beta_k\left(\prod_{i=1}^ku_i\right)T\\
&=d_k(J_n)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}u_i\right)T.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{D=d_k(J_n)\mid C.}
\tag{3}
\]

This is the key point in the proposed repair: dividing \(\Gamma\) by all of \(B_k\) would also divide away the guaranteed \(\beta_k\)-part of the target invariant; multiplying back by \(\beta_k\) restores exactly enough to make (3) automatic.

## 3. From \(D\mid C\) to \(H\mid\Sigma^*\)

Recall
\[
H=\frac{D}{\gcd(D,E)},
\qquad
\Sigma^*=\frac{C}{\gcd(C,E)}.
\]
For any prime \(\ell\), put
\[
d=v_\ell(D),\qquad c=v_\ell(C),\qquad e=v_\ell(E).
\]
By (3), \(c\ge d\).  Therefore
\[
v_\ell(H)=\max(d-e,0)
\le\max(c-e,0)=v_\ell(\Sigma^*).
\]
Prime by prime,
\[
\boxed{H\mid\Sigma^*.}
\tag{4}
\]
This implication is purely arithmetic and has no hidden coprimality assumption.

## 4. Candidate primes do not divide \(B_k\)

Let \(p\) be prime and
\[
q=\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor\ge1,
\qquad
n=qp+r,
\quad 0\le r<p.
\]
For \(i\le k=n-q\),
\[
n-i+1\ge q+1.
\]
Consequently
\[
\left\lfloor\frac n{n-i+1}\right\rfloor
\le
\left\lfloor\frac n{q+1}\right\rfloor.
\]
But \(q=\lfloor n/p\rfloor\) gives
\[
\frac n{q+1}<p,
\]
so
\[
\left\lfloor\frac n{q+1}\right\rfloor\le p-1.
\]
Every integer occurring in every lcm defining \(\beta_i(n)\), \(i\le k\), is therefore strictly smaller than \(p\).  Thus
\[
\boxed{p\nmid\beta_i(n)\quad(i\le k),\qquad p\nmid B_k.}
\tag{5}
\]
In particular \(p\nmid\beta_k\), and hence
\[
\boxed{v_p(C)=v_p(\Gamma).}
\tag{6}
\]
This proof is valid verbatim for \(p=2\) and \(p=3\).

## 5. Exact mod-\(p\) block structure of the Apéry Jacobi matrix

Use the standard indexing of \(J_n\) by \(0,\ldots,n-1\): the diagonal entry at index \(t\) is \(P(t)\), and the edge joining \(t-1\) to \(t\) has the cubic factor \(t^3\) (up to the harmless sign convention).  The determinant of the initial length-\(m\) block is
\[
K_m(0)=(m!)^3b_m.
\tag{7}
\]
Indeed the continuant recurrence divided by \((m!)^3\) is exactly the Apéry recurrence.

More generally, because all coefficients are integral polynomials in the starting index,
\[
\boxed{K_t(ap)\equiv K_t(0)\pmod p}
\tag{8}
\]
for every integer \(a\).

### 5.1 A full \(p\)-block has corank exactly one

The edge at every multiple of \(p\) vanishes modulo \(p\).  Hence the blocks
\[
[jp,(j+1)p-1]
\]
are disconnected modulo \(p\).

A full block has determinant
\[
K_p(jp)\equiv K_p(0)=(p!)^3b_p\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
On the other hand its leading \((p-1)\times(p-1)\) principal minor is
\[
K_{p-1}(jp)\equiv((p-1)!)^3b_{p-1}.
\]
For every prime \(p\),
\[
\boxed{b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.}
\tag{9}
\]
A direct proof from the Apéry sum is enough: for \(1\le j\le p-1\),
\(\binom{p-1+j}{j}\) is divisible by \(p\), whereas the \(j=0\) summand is \(1\).  Thus (9) also covers \(p=2,3\).

Since \((p-1)!\) is a \(p\)-unit, the \((p-1)\)-minor is nonzero.  Therefore
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{corank}_{\mathbb F_p}(\text{full }p\text{-block})=1.}
\tag{10}
\]

### 5.2 The residual block

The tail block has length \(r<p\) and starts at \(qp\), so
\[
\det(\text{tail})=K_r(qp)\equiv(r!)^3b_r\pmod p.
\tag{11}
\]
Thus it is nonsingular iff \(p\nmid b_r\).

If \(p\mid b_r\) and \(r\ge2\), then \(p\nmid b_{r-1}\).  The usual no-two-consecutive-zeros argument works for **every** prime in the range \(r<p\): if two consecutive \(b\)'s below \(p\) vanished, the recurrence could be run backwards through indices that are \(p\)-units until it contradicted \(b_0=1\).  Hence the leading \((r-1)\)-minor is nonzero, and the singular tail has corank exactly one.  The case \(r=1\) is immediate.

Therefore, with
\[
\varepsilon_r=
\begin{cases}
1,&p\mid b_r,\\
0,&p\nmid b_r,
\end{cases}
\]
(where \(b_0=1\), so \(\varepsilon_0=0\)), the block decomposition gives
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}J_n
=n-q-\varepsilon_r
=k-\varepsilon_r.
}
\tag{12}
\]

## 6. The target Smith invariant and the moving-cut gcd have the same candidate-prime support

For an integer matrix with Smith factors \(d_1\mid\cdots\), its rank modulo \(p\) is the number of Smith factors not divisible by \(p\).  From (12),
\[
\boxed{p\mid D=d_k(J_n)\iff p\mid b_r.}
\tag{13}
\]

Now prove the corresponding statement for \(\Gamma\), without replacing it by \(\Delta_k\).

### 6.1 Target case

If \(p\mid b_r\), then (12) says
\[
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}J_n=k-1.
\]
Hence **every** \(k\times k\) minor vanishes modulo \(p\).  In particular every moving-cut minor \(M(c)\) vanishes, so
\[
\boxed{p\mid\Gamma.}
\tag{14}
\]

### 6.2 Non-target case: the single cut \(c=p\) kills every phantom

If \(p\nmid b_r\), use the valid cut \(c=p\).  It is present because
\[
qp\le n.
\]
The corresponding minor is
\[
M(p)=\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}K_{p-1}(jp)\,K_r(qp).
\]
By (8), (9), and (11), every factor is a \(p\)-unit:
\[
K_{p-1}(jp)\not\equiv0\pmod p,
\qquad
K_r(qp)\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
Therefore
\[
p\nmid M(p),
\]
and since \(\Gamma\) is the gcd of all the \(M(c)\),
\[
\boxed{p\nmid\Gamma.}
\tag{15}
\]
Combining (14) and (15),
\[
\boxed{p\mid\Gamma\iff p\mid b_r.}
\tag{16}
\]
This is stronger than merely saying \(\Delta_k\mid\Gamma\): the reverse implication in (16) comes from the explicit moving cut \(c=p\).

## 7. Why the subtraction by \(E=d_{k-1}(J_{n-1})\) does not delete a genuine target prime

Suppose \(p\mid b_r\).  Then \(r\ne0\).

If \(r\ge2\), write
\[
n-1=qp+(r-1).
\]
Modulo \(p\), \(J_{n-1}\) consists of \(q\) full \(p\)-blocks and a tail of length \(r-1\).  Since consecutive residual zeros are impossible,
\[
p\nmid b_{r-1},
\]
so the tail is nonsingular.  Hence
\[
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}J_{n-1}
=q(p-1)+(r-1)
=n-q-1
=k-1.
\]
If \(r=1\), then \(n-1=qp\), so there are exactly \(q\) full blocks and again
\[
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}J_{n-1}=q(p-1)=k-1.
\]
Thus in every genuine target case,
\[
\boxed{p\nmid E=d_{k-1}(J_{n-1}).}
\tag{17}
\]

Consequently, using (5), (6), (16), and (17),
\[
\begin{aligned}
p\mid\Sigma^*
&\iff v_p(C)>v_p(E)\\
&\iff p\mid b_r.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
 p\mid\Sigma^*\iff p\mid b_{n-qp}
 \qquad\left(q=\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor\right).
}
\tag{18}
\]
No higher-valuation equality between \(\Gamma\) and a determinantal divisor is needed; only support modulo \(p\) is used.

As a by-product, (13) and (17) also give the exact candidate support of
\[
H=\frac{D}{\gcd(D,E)}:
\qquad
\boxed{p\mid H\iff p\mid b_r.}
\tag{19}
\]
Thus (4) is compatible with the exact selector rather than merely a one-way containment.

## 8. Explicit audit of \(p=2\) and \(p=3\)

Nothing in Sections 4--7 requires \(p\ge5\).

Here \(r\in\{0,1\}\), and
\[
b_0=1,\qquad b_1=5\equiv1\pmod2.
\]
So \(2\nmid b_r\) for every candidate cell.  The full two-block has rank one because
\[
K_1(0)=5\equiv1\pmod2,
\]
and the explicit cut \(c=2\) gives \(M(2)\) odd.  Hence
\[
2\nmid\Gamma,\ C,\ \Sigma^*
\]
for a \(p=2\) candidate.

### \(p=3\)

Here \(r\in\{0,1,2\}\), and
\[
(b_0,b_1,b_2)=(1,5,73)\equiv(1,2,1)\pmod3.

Again there is no target residual.  The full three-block has corank one because

K_2(0)=(2!)^3b_2\not\equiv0\pmod3,

and the cut \(c=3\) is a \(3\)-unit.  Hence
\[
3\nmid\Gamma,\ C,\ \Sigma^*
\]
for a \(p=3\) candidate.

Thus there is no small-prime exception to (18).

## 9. Exact valuation of \(B_k\) at \(2\)

For later use, first compute \(v_2(B_k)\) exactly.

For each \(i\),
\[
v_2(\beta_i(n))
=3\left\lfloor
\log_2\left\lfloor\frac n{n-i+1}\right\rfloor
\right\rfloor.
\]
For a fixed \(a\ge1\), the factor \(2^a\) occurs in the lcm defining \(\beta_i(n)\) iff
\[
2^a\le\left\lfloor\frac n{n-i+1}\right\rfloor,
\]
equivalently
\[
i\ge n-\left\lfloor\frac n{2^a}\right\rfloor+1.
\]
Among \(1\le i\le k=n-q\), the number of such indices is exactly
\[
\max\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac n{2^a}\right\rfloor-q,0\right).
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
 v_2(B_k)
 =3\sum_{a\ge1}
 \max\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac n{2^a}\right\rfloor-q,0\right).
}
\tag{20}
\]
This is precisely the first term in the claimed raw obstruction.

## 10. Proof of the upper bound for \(v_2(E)\)

This is the place where the \(n\mapsto n-1\) index shift matters.

Put
\[
m=n-1,
\qquad
s=k-1=n-q-1=m-q.
\]
Then
\[
E=d_s(J_m)=\frac{\Delta_s(J_m)}{\Delta_{s-1}(J_m)}.
\]
The hypothesis \(n\ge q+2\) gives \(s\ge1\), so this invariant is defined.

### 10.1 Every Apéry number is odd

From
\[
b_s=\sum_{j=0}^s\binom sj^2\binom{s+j}j^2,
\]
for \(j\ge1\) use
\[
\binom sj\binom{s+j}j
=
\binom{s+j}{2j}\binom{2j}j.
\]
The central binomial coefficient is even for \(j\ge1\):
\[
\binom{2j}j=2\binom{2j-1}{j-1}.
\]
Hence every \(j\ge1\) summand in \(b_s\) is divisible by \(4\), while the \(j=0\) summand is \(1\).  Thus
\[
\boxed{b_s\equiv1\pmod4,\quad v_2(b_s)=0.}
\tag{21}
\]

### 10.2 Upper-bound the numerator determinant divisor

The top-left \(s\times s\) principal minor of \(J_m\) is \(J_s\), so
\[
\Delta_s(J_m)\mid\det J_s=K_s(0)=(s!)^3b_s.
\]
By (21),
\[
\boxed{
 v_2(\Delta_s(J_m))
 \le3v_2(s!)
 =3\sum_{a\ge1}\left\lfloor\frac s{2^a}\right\rfloor.
}
\tag{22}
\]

### 10.3 Lower-bound the denominator determinant divisor

Apply the same supplied \(\beta\)-divisibility theorem at matrix size \(m=n-1\).  Since
\[
s-1=m-(q+1),
\]
we have
\[
B_{s-1}(m):=\prod_{i=1}^{s-1}\beta_i(m)\mid\Delta_{s-1}(J_m).
\]
The counting argument of Section 9, now with codimension \(q+1\), gives
\[
\boxed{
 v_2(B_{s-1}(m))
 =3\sum_{a\ge1}
 \max\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac m{2^a}\right\rfloor-(q+1),0\right).
}
\tag{23}
\]

Combining (22) and (23),
\[
\begin{aligned}
v_2(E)
&=v_2(\Delta_s(J_m))-v_2(\Delta_{s-1}(J_m))\\
&\le
3\sum_{a\ge1}
\left[
\left\lfloor\frac{m-q}{2^a}\right\rfloor
-
\max\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac m{2^a}\right\rfloor-q-1,0\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{24}
\]
For every \(a\), the bracket is at most \(q+1\).  For
\[
a>\lfloor\log_2m\rfloor
\]
both terms vanish.  Hence
\[
\boxed{
 v_2(E)
 \le3(q+1)\lfloor\log_2m\rfloor
 =3(q+1)\lfloor\log_2(n-1)\rfloor.
}
\tag{25}
\]
So the auxiliary estimate used in the proposed raw obstruction is sound.

## 11. The raw \(2\)-adic obstruction follows

Set
\[
R_{\rm raw}:=\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,E)}.
\]
Then
\[
v_2(R_{\rm raw})
=\max(v_2(\Gamma)-v_2(E),0).
\]
By (2),
\[
v_2(\Gamma)\ge v_2(B_k).
\]
Therefore, using (20) and (25),
\[
\begin{aligned}
v_2(R_{\rm raw})
&\ge v_2(B_k)-v_2(E)\\
&\ge
3\sum_{a\ge1}\max\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac n{2^a}\right\rfloor-q,0\right)
-3(q+1)\left\lfloor\log_2(n-1)\right\rfloor.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the claimed estimate is exactly valid:
\[
\boxed{
 v_2\!\left(\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,E)}\right)
 \ge
 3\sum_{a\ge1}\max\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac n{2^a}\right\rfloor-q,0\right)
 -3(q+1)\left\lfloor\log_2(n-1)\right\rfloor.
}
\tag{26}
\]
If the right-hand side is negative, (26) is of course only a vacuous lower bound; when it is positive it is a genuine forced \(2\)-power in the raw quantity.

Crucially, (26) does **not** imply the same lower bound for the repaired quantity.  Indeed
\[
v_2(C)
=v_2(\beta_k)+v_2(\Gamma)-v_2(B_k),
\]
so the universal \(B_k\)-mass responsible for (26) has explicitly been divided out.  Using (26) as an obstruction to \(\Sigma^*\) would be the first error; using it only against the raw \(\Gamma/\gcd(\Gamma,E)\) is correct.

## 12. Endpoint audit

The requested hypothesis
\[
n\ge q+2
\]
has exactly the needed consequences:

- \(k=n-q\ge2\), so \(D=d_k(J_n)\) and \(E=d_{k-1}(J_{n-1})\) are both positive-index Smith invariants.
- With \(m=n-1\), \(s=k-1=m-q\ge1\), so the proof of (25) includes the endpoint \(s=1\); there \(B_{s-1}=B_0=1\).
- The cut \(c=1\) is harmless because the first \(q\) blocks have length zero and \(K_0=1\).
- The endpoint \(qc=n\) is harmless because the final block has length zero and \(K_0=1\).
- For a candidate prime, \(c=p\) is always a valid cut since \(qp\le n\).  If \(r=0\), its final factor is exactly \(K_0=1\).
- If a genuine target has \(r=1\), the \(J_{n-1}\) residual block disappears; the rank computation still gives exactly \(k-1\), so (17) remains valid.
- The residual value \(r=0\) is never a target because \(b_0=1\).

The only structural assumption that must remain explicit is \(q\ge1\), as is standard for the fixed-quotient cells.  For \(q=0\), the phrase “delete \(q\) cuts” and the moving-cut range are a different degenerate setup and the candidate argument via \(c=p\) is not the statement audited here.

## 13. Final theorem-grade statement

**Proposition.**  Let \(q\ge1\), \(n\ge q+2\), \(k=n-q\).  Let \(J_n\) be the Apéry Jacobi tridiagonal and let \(d_i(J_N)\) denote its Smith invariants.  Assume for every \(N,i\)
\[
\beta_i(N)=\operatorname{lcm}\!\left(1,\ldots,
\left\lfloor\frac N{N-i+1}\right\rfloor\right)^3\mid d_i(J_N).
\]
For every \(1\le c\le\lfloor n/q\rfloor\), let \(M(c)\) be the principal \((n-q)\)-minor obtained by deleting
\(c-1,2c-1,\ldots,qc-1\), and set
\[
\Gamma=\gcd_c M(c),
\quad
B_k=\prod_{i\le k}\beta_i(n),
\quad
D=d_k(J_n),
\quad
E=d_{k-1}(J_{n-1}),
\]
\[
C=\beta_k(n)\frac{\Gamma}{B_k},
\qquad
H=\frac D{\gcd(D,E)},
\qquad
\Sigma^*=\frac C{\gcd(C,E)}.
\]
Then:

1. \(B_k\mid\Gamma\), so \(C\in\mathbb Z\).
2. \(D\mid C\).
3. \(H\mid\Sigma^*\).
4. If \(p\) is prime and \(q=\lfloor n/p\rfloor\), then \(p\nmid B_k\) and, with \(r=n-qp\),
   \[
   p\mid H\iff p\mid\Sigma^*\iff p\mid b_r.
   \]
5. The raw moving-cut quotient satisfies (26).
6. All assertions include \(p=2,3\); those two primes have no target residual in their candidate cells.

So the proposed Smith normalization passes this hostile audit.  The decisive points are (i) using only \(\Delta_k\mid\Gamma\), never equality; (ii) retaining the single \(\beta_k\) factor in \(C\); and (iii) using the explicit candidate cut \(c=p\) to rule out moving-cut phantom support.
