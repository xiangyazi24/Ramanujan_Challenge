# Verdict: NO APPLICABLE THEOREM (current state), with a LIVE REDUCTION target

## 1. Existing general gcd theorems do not apply

The relevant integer sequence is not a fixed P-recursive sequence in the usual sense. The carriers

\[
F_j=F_j(n),\qquad E_j(n)=\frac{F_{j-1}(n)F_{j+1}(n)-F_j(n)^2}{q_j(n)}
\]

are indexed by the moving terminal parameter n, and the underlying binomial/Newton endpoints move with n. A theorem about a single recurrence sequence \(a_m\) does not automatically give information about \(\gcd(a_m,a_{m+k})\) here.

### P-recursive / holonomic gcd results

A P-recursive sequence satisfies a fixed recurrence

\[
\sum_{i=0}^d P_i(m)a_{m+i}=0
\]

with fixed polynomials \(P_i\). Known results on divisibility, zero sets, and arithmetic growth of such sequences require the recurrence itself to be fixed. They do not cover a two-parameter family where the recurrence coefficients and the terminal summation range change with n.

The Apéry numbers themselves are P-recursive, but the objects \(E_1(n),E_2(n)\) are not obtained by sampling a fixed recurrence at neighboring indices. They are nonlinear determinants of moving finite hypergeometric sums.

Primary reference:

- R. P. Stanley, *Differentiably finite power series*, European J. Combin. 1 (1980), 175–188.

## 2. Arithmetic resultant and determinant methods

The expression

\[
F_{j-1}F_{j+1}-F_j^2
\]

is a discrete Wronskian/Hankel determinant. Resultant theory controls common roots of fixed polynomials, and determinant divisibility can detect rank drops over finite fields.

Here the obstruction is that the parameter n changes the polynomial objects. The candidate primes are exactly the primes producing a rank-one collapse modulo p:

\[
F_0,F_1,F_2,F_3 \pmod p
\]

lie on a one-dimensional subspace. Therefore the determinant vanishes modulo p by construction. This is a local rank statement, not a global resultant estimate.

No standard arithmetic resultant theorem gives an upper bound for the integer gcd after removing all known local rank-one factors.

## 3. Subspace theorem and Bugeaud--Corvaja--Zannier estimates

The closest general philosophy is that large gcds of values of multiplicatively independent objects are rare.

A representative statement is:

For suitable non-degenerate linear recurrence sequences, if \(a,b\) are multiplicatively independent, then

\[
\log\gcd(a^n-1,b^n-1)=o(n).
\]

This comes from the Subspace Theorem framework.

Primary reference:

- Y. Bugeaud, P. Corvaja, U. Zannier, *An upper bound for the GCD of \(a^n-1\) and \(b^n-1\)*, Math. Z. 243 (2003), 79–88.

The hypotheses fail here:

1. The sequences are not fixed exponential-polynomial sequences.
2. There is no fixed multiplicative independence problem.
3. The common divisors arise from a moving family of finite hypergeometric identities.
4. The known large divisors are not accidental: every target prime p is forced by exact congruence rank collapse.

Thus Bugeaud--Corvaja--Zannier is not a black-box replacement for this problem.

## 4. Why archimedean asymptotics are insufficient

The observation

\[
\log |E_j(n)|=\Theta(n)
\]

only gives the total height budget. It does not control the gcd.

An integer may have exponential size while sharing either:

- an exponential common factor, or
- almost no common factor,

with the same archimedean asymptotic size.

Example:

\[
X_n=2^n(2^n+1),\qquad Y_n=2^n(2^n+3)
\]

have exponential height and exponential gcd contribution, whereas replacing the common factor by unrelated primes destroys it. Absolute values cannot see the p-adic distribution.

Therefore an archimedean dominant-mode expansion of \(F_j\) is only useful after it is combined with nonarchimedean information.

## 5. The remaining Apéry-specific routes

The reduction is still meaningful because the local structure is unusually rigid.

### Route A: dominant-mode independence + p-adic control

A possible proof would need two ingredients:

1. A decomposition

\[
F_j(n)=\alpha_j(n)\lambda_1(n)+\beta_j(n)\lambda_2(n)+\cdots
\]

where the dominant modes are sufficiently independent.

2. A nonarchimedean theorem proving that a prime divisor of both determinants must lie in a controlled exceptional set.

The missing point is not asymptotic separation. It is a uniform statement over all primes p in the moving interval \(p=n-r>n/2\).

### Route B: modular parametrization

The Apéry recurrence has modular/geometric realizations. A successful approach would reinterpret

\[
(F_0,F_1,F_2,F_3)\pmod p
\]

as periods or Frobenius data of a fixed geometric object and prove that the determinant has a second independent obstruction.

The current rank-one saturation says the first determinant layer detects exactly the candidate primes. A second geometric invariant would need to distinguish genuine primes from accidental lifting.

### Route C: higher determinant tower

The two determinants only test rank \(<2\). A plausible strengthening is to construct

\[
\det(F_{i+j})_{0\le i,j\le m}
\]

or mixed Newton/Pascal minors. If every target prime forces vanishing of a whole tower, then one can attempt to show that a non-target common divisor would force an impossible high-rank degeneration.

This is the most concrete algebraic continuation of the current carrier construction.
