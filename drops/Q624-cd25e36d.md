# Katz equidistribution after double Mellin: what is true, what fails, and the exact missing theorem

## Executive verdict

The double-Mellin identity is correct, after fixing the multiplicative-character notation:

\[
\widehat{\widehat{\theta}}(s)=(p-1)\theta(-s)
\]

in exponent coordinates, up to the sign convention in the first transform.

But the proposed consequence is false:

> **Fourier inversion does not make the number of zero Fourier coefficients a function of the value distribution of the original function.**

It only says that the full ordered vector of Mellin coefficients determines the full ordered trace vector. A condition such as

\[
\widehat\theta(r)=0
\]

is one global linear relation among all values of \(\theta\); it is not a one-point condition on \(\theta(t)\), and it is not determined by the histogram of those values.

More importantly, Katz's Mellin equidistribution is an **archimedean/\(\ell\)-adic, macroscopic** statement about normalized complex Frobenius conjugacy classes. Apéry vanishing is the **defining-characteristic, microscopic** condition

\[
\mathfrak p_p\mid \mathscr M_{p,r},
\]

where \(\mathfrak p_p\) is the prime selected by the Teichmüller embedding. Complex Sato--Tate equidistribution does not detect that condition.

There is therefore no theorem converting ordinary Katz equidistribution, even fixed-\(k\) joint equidistribution, into

\[
\#\{r:p\mid b_r\}=O(1).
\]

In fact:

1. the repository's Jacobi-sum example gives a fixed-rank, fixed-conductor sheaf with Deligne size \(O(\sqrt p)\) but \(\gg p\) defining-characteristic-divisible Mellin transforms;
2. even perfect fixed-\(k\) joint Sato--Tate limits are insensitive to an exceptional set of size \(m_p=o(p)\), where \(m_p\to\infty\);
3. the project's Poisson model itself predicts that a **uniform pointwise constant** is false, because a Poisson law has unbounded support.

The correct missing input is an Apéry-specific **crystalline residual local limit theorem** or **crystalline Mellin anti-concentration theorem**, not a second application of Mellin inversion.

A concise implication diagram is:

```text
Katz character-aspect equidistribution
        => macroscopic complex distribution of normalized Mellin traces
        => fixed continuous moments / fixed-scale test sets
        =/=> reduction modulo the defining prime p
        =/=> probability 1/p for a residual zero
        =/=> O(1) zero count.

Needed instead:
Apéry crystalline Mellin trace
        + uniform residual anti-concentration at the prime above p
        => average O(1) zero count (and, with higher residual moments, Poisson).
```

# 1. The exact double-Mellin identity

Let

\[
G=\mathbf F_p^\times,
\qquad n=p-1,
\]

choose a generator \(g\) of \(G\), and let \(\zeta_n\) be a primitive \(n\)-th root of unity in the coefficient field. Write

\[
f(j)=\theta_p(g^j),
\qquad j\in\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z.
\]

The multiplicative characters are

\[
\chi_r(g^j)=\zeta_n^{rj}.
\]

With the convention

\[
b_r=\widehat f(r)=\sum_{j\bmod n}f(j)\zeta_n^{-rj},
\]

the second transform is

\[
\begin{aligned}
B_s
 &=\sum_{r\bmod n}b_r\zeta_n^{-rs}\\
 &=\sum_j f(j)\sum_r\zeta_n^{-r(j+s)}\\
 &=n f(-s).
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{B_s=(p-1)\theta_p(g^{-s}).}
\]

Changing the sign in one transform removes the minus sign. This works over \(\mathbf C\), an \(\ell\)-adic coefficient field, a \(p\)-adic coefficient field, or \(\mathbf F_p\), because \(p\nmid p-1\).

The notation in the question,

\[
\omega^{rt}\theta_p(t),
\]

should therefore be read with \(t\) replaced by the discrete logarithm of the element of \(\mathbf F_p^\times\), or more invariantly as \(\chi_r(t)\theta_p(t)\).

## What inversion does and does not say

It says that the linear map

\[
(f(j))_{j\bmod n}\longleftrightarrow (b_r)_{r\bmod n}
\]

is invertible.

It does **not** say that the zero pattern of \((b_r)\) is determined by the multiset of values \(\{f(j)\}\). The positions of the values matter.

A concrete example makes this sharp. Assume \(n\) is even and compare two \(0\)-\(1\) functions on \(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z\):

\[
f_1=\mathbf 1_{\{0,n/2\}},
\qquad
f_2=\mathbf 1_{\{0,1\}}.
\]

They have exactly the same value distribution: two values equal to \(1\), all others equal to \(0\). But

\[
\widehat f_1(r)=1+(-1)^r,
\]

so \(\widehat f_1\) vanishes at every odd \(r\), namely at \(n/2\) frequencies. On the other hand,

\[
\widehat f_2(r)=1+\zeta_n^{-r},
\]

which vanishes only at \(r=n/2\).

Hence “value distribution of \(\theta_p\)” and “zero distribution of its Mellin transform” are genuinely different data. Double Mellin inversion does not bridge that gap.

# 2. The random-function benchmark is exact—but it is a residual model

There is a clean random model over \(\mathbf F_p\).

Take a uniformly random vector

\[
f\in \mathbf F_p^{\,n}.
\]

The finite Fourier matrix

\[
( g^{rj})_{r,j\bmod n}
\]

is invertible over \(\mathbf F_p\). Consequently its transform \(\widehat f\) is also a uniformly random vector in \(\mathbf F_p^n\). In particular, its coordinates are independent and uniform, so

\[
Z=\#\{r:\widehat f(r)=0\}
  \sim \operatorname{Binomial}\!\left(n,\frac1p\right).
\]

Therefore

\[
\mathbf E Z=\frac{p-1}{p}\to1,
\qquad
Z\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1).
\]

This is not merely a heuristic: it is the exact law for a uniform random \(\mathbf F_p\)-valued function.

## Reflection and the observed \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)\)

The Apéry reflection law is

\[
b_r=b_{p-1-r}\pmod p.
\]

If one models the reflected pairs as independent uniform residual coordinates, then there are about \((p-1)/2\) independent pairs, each vanishing with probability \(1/p\). The number of **zero pairs** therefore tends to

\[
\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).
\]

The unfolded zero count has expected value about \(1\), because a vanishing noncentral pair contributes two indices. Thus the observed \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)\) is not evidence that “half of the characters are always nonzero”; it is the consequence of pairing the frequencies under reflection.

This random model explains the prediction. It does not identify a theorem that forces the Apéry Mellin vector to be residual-random.

# 3. What Katz's Mellin equidistribution actually gives

Katz's 2012 monograph is precisely about character-aspect finite-field Mellin transforms. Thus it is not correct to say that Katz only treats the geometric parameter \(t\). He constructs a Tannakian convolution category on \(\mathbf G_m\), and the normalized Mellin sums indexed by multiplicative characters give Frobenius conjugacy classes in the associated arithmetic group.

In its standard form, the theorem fixes a finite base field \(k\), lets \(E/k\) run through larger finite extensions, and lets \(\chi\) vary over the good characters of \(E^\times\). For every fixed continuous class function \(F\) on the relevant compact group \(K\), it yields a statement of the shape

\[
\frac1{|E^\times|}
 \sum_{\chi\ \mathrm{good}}F(\Theta_{E,\chi})
 \longrightarrow
 \int_KF(g)\,dg.
\]

Forey--Fresán--Kowalski generalize this framework to connected commutative algebraic groups. Their current formulation likewise gives equidistribution for bounded continuous test functions and explicitly notes that a horizontal family with \(p\to\infty\) requires additional quantitative uniformity that is not automatic in full generality.

So there are already two qualifications before applying the theorem to Apéry:

1. one must establish a uniform horizontal compatible family as \(p\to\infty\);
2. one must compute the **Mellin/convolution Tannakian group and its representation**, not merely the local geometric monodromy of the original Picard--Fuchs equation.

The repository proves that the rank-three Apéry Picard--Fuchs connection is of

\[
\operatorname{Sym}^2\operatorname{SL}_2\simeq\operatorname{SO}_3
\]

type. But the Mellin coefficient is an alternating Frobenius trace on global twisted cohomology,

\[
R\Gamma_c(U_{\overline{\mathbf F}_p},
   \mathcal V_\ell\otimes\mathcal L_\chi),
\]

and its Tannakian representation need not be the standard three-dimensional representation simply because the original local system has \(\operatorname{SO}_3\) geometric monodromy.

Even if all of those identifications are granted, Katz gives a macroscopic complex distribution—not a distribution modulo the defining prime.

# 4. The correct Haar trace density for the standard representation of \(\operatorname{SO}_3\)

Suppose, only for this calculation, that the relevant compact group really is \(\operatorname{SO}(3)\) in its standard representation.

A conjugacy class has eigenvalues

\[
1,e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta},
\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi,
\]

and trace

\[
x=1+2\cos\theta\in[-1,3].
\]

For Haar measure on \(\operatorname{SO}(3)\), the rotation-angle density is

\[
d\nu(\theta)=\frac2\pi\sin^2\!\left(\frac\theta2\right)d\theta.
\]

Pushing this forward under \(x=1+2\cos\theta\) gives

\[
\boxed{
 d\mu_{\operatorname{SO}_3}(x)
 =\frac1{2\pi}
   \sqrt{\frac{3-x}{x+1}}\,dx,
 \qquad -1<x<3.
}
\]

In particular, the density at \(x=0\) is

\[
\frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi},
\]

and there is no atom at zero.

This corrects the tentative density in the question. But it still does not address \(p\)-divisibility. A continuous density having no atom at zero only concerns exact complex trace zero at macroscopic scale.

# 5. The crucial error: \(p\)-divisibility is not a small complex window

There are three distinct objects that the heuristic conflates:

| object | where it lives | relevant statement |
|---|---|---|
| \(\bar b_r\) | \(\mathbf F_p\) | AFP asks whether \(\bar b_r=0\) |
| \(\mathscr M_{p,r}\) | a cyclotomic/algebraic integer ring | reduces to a unit times \(\bar b_r\) at a chosen \(\mathfrak p_p\mid p\) |
| normalized \(\iota(\mathscr M_{p,r})\) | \(\mathbf C\), after an embedding \(\iota\) | Katz/Deligne control its complex distribution and size |

The Deligne bound applies to the cohomological Mellin lift \(\mathscr M_{p,r}\), not to the enormous classical integer Apéry number \(b_r\) itself. The desired comparison has the form

\[
\mathscr M_{p,r}\equiv u_{p,r}b_r\pmod{\mathfrak p_p},
\qquad u_{p,r}\in\mathcal O_{\mathfrak p_p}^{\times}.
\]

Thus

\[
p\mid b_r
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathfrak p_p\mid\mathscr M_{p,r},
\]

up to the comparison's explicit exceptional twists. This is a \(p\)-adic statement at one prime of the coefficient field.

It does **not** imply

\[
\left|\iota(\mathscr M_{p,r})/\sqrt p\right|<p^{-1/2}
\]

for a complex embedding \(\iota\).

Indeed, the tame character values lie in \(\mathbf Q(\mu_{p-1})\). Since

\[
p\equiv1\pmod{p-1},
\]

the prime \(p\) splits completely in that cyclotomic field. Divisibility at one selected prime \(\mathfrak p_p\) places no smallness constraint on any chosen complex embedding. An algebraic integer may have every complex conjugate of size \(\sqrt p\) and still be divisible by \(\mathfrak p_p\).

## Even the rational-integer counting argument gives the wrong scale

Suppose, contrary to the actual setup, that \(M_r\in\mathbf Z\) and

\[
|M_r|\le C\sqrt p.
\]

For sufficiently large \(p\), one has \(C\sqrt p<p\). The only multiple of \(p\) in that interval is then \(0\). There are not “\(2C/\sqrt p\) multiples”; the integer count is exactly one. A uniform-lattice model would give probability about

\[
\frac1{2C\sqrt p},
\]

not \(1/p\), and would predict about \(\sqrt p\) exact zeros among \(p\) samples.

The probability \(1/p\) arises from a **uniform residual model in \(\mathbf F_p\)**. It is not derivable by combining a complex \(\sqrt p\)-bound with Sato--Tate density.

# 6. The repository's Jacobi counterexample is decisive

The current `proof.tex` gives a particularly clean demonstration that bounded sheaf complexity and Weil bounds do not control defining-characteristic Mellin divisibility.

Fix \(d\ge2\). For primes

\[
p\equiv1\pmod d,
\]

let

\[
\psi=\omega_p^{-(p-1)/d}
\]

be a character of order \(d\). A fixed rank-one Kummer sheaf on

\[
\mathbf P^1\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}
\]

has Mellin transforms

\[
M_j=J(\omega_p^{-j},\psi).
\]

For the nondegenerate characters, the classical complex Weil bound is actually an equality:

\[
|\iota(M_j)|=\sqrt p
\]

for every complex embedding \(\iota\).

On the other hand, Gross--Koblitz/Stickelberger and

\[
J(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{g(\alpha)g(\beta)}{g(\alpha\beta)}
\]

give

\[
v_{\mathfrak p_p}(M_j)=1
\qquad\text{whenever}\qquad
j\ge\frac{(p-1)(d-1)}d.
\]

Consequently at least

\[
\frac{p-1}{d}-1
\]

tame characters satisfy

\[
\mathfrak p_p\mid M_j.
\]

So one has simultaneously:

- fixed rank;
- fixed conductor;
- Deligne/Weil size \(\sqrt p\);
- a positive proportion of defining-characteristic-divisible Mellin values.

This does not prove that the Apéry sheaf has many bad twists. It proves something logically decisive for the proposed argument:

> No conclusion of the form “bounded complexity + complex Mellin equidistribution \(\Rightarrow O(1)\) defining-characteristic zeros” can be valid without additional, specifically \(p\)-adic arithmetic input.

# 7. Fixed-\(k\) joint equidistribution still cannot give \(O(1)\)

There are two separate issues.

## 7.1 Joint independence is not automatic

One-variable Katz equidistribution of

\[
\Theta_{p,r}
\]

does not automatically imply product equidistribution of

\[
(\Theta_{p,r+a_1},\ldots,\Theta_{p,r+a_k}).
\]

For such a statement one normally needs a separate geometric independence calculation: a direct-sum monodromy theorem, exclusion of accidental isomorphisms among twists, and a Goursat--Kolchin--Ribet argument.

But even granting the strongest possible fixed-\(k\) product equidistribution does not solve the problem.

## 7.2 Fixed-\(k\) limits are blind to growing zero-density exceptional sets

Let \(n_p=p-1\), and start with any array of conjugacy classes

\[
x_{p,1},\ldots,x_{p,n_p}
\]

that is Haar-equidistributed, with all desired fixed-\(k\) joint limits. Now replace

\[
m_p=\lfloor p^{1/3}\rfloor
\]

of the entries by classes having trace zero.

Since

\[
\frac{m_p}{n_p}\to0,
\]

the one-point empirical measure is unchanged in the limit. For any fixed \(k\), the proportion of sampled \(k\)-tuples touching a changed entry is at most

\[
O\!\left(\frac{k m_p}{p}\right)\to0.
\]

Hence every fixed-\(k\) joint limit remains the same, while the number of exact zero traces is

\[
m_p\to\infty.
\]

One may replace \(p^{1/3}\) by any \(m_p=o(p)\). Therefore fixed-\(k\) equidistribution, even for every fixed \(k\), cannot distinguish an \(O(1)\) exceptional set from an unbounded \(o(p)\) exceptional set.

This is not merely a missing “tail estimate.” The topology of weak equidistribution is intrinsically too coarse.

## 7.3 Poisson limits require rare-event factorial moments, not Sato--Tate moments

To prove a Poisson law for the residual zero count, one would need estimates of the form

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}
\sum_{p\le X}(K_p)_k
\longrightarrow \left(\frac12\right)^k,
\]

where \(K_p\) is the number of reflected zero pairs and \((K_p)_k\) is the falling factorial.

Expanding \((K_p)_k\) asks for simultaneous **residual congruences**

\[
\bar b_{r_1}=\cdots=\bar b_{r_k}=0
\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_p.
\]

These are not the fixed continuous moments of normalized complex traces controlled by Katz. They are microscopic events whose individual probability is expected to be \(1/p\).

Moreover, a genuine Poisson law has unbounded support. Thus it predicts bounded mean, not a universal deterministic constant valid for every prime.

# 8. The quantitative square-root barrier

Even if one made the unjustified replacement

\[
p\mid b_r
\quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
x_{p,r}\in I_p
\]

for a shrinking complex interval \(I_p\), standard equidistribution would still be too weak.

Suppose one had the optimistic discrepancy estimate

\[
\#\{r:x_{p,r}\in I\}
  =(p-1)\mu(I)+O(\sqrt p)
\]

for all fixed intervals \(I\). If \(I_p\) has Sato--Tate mass about \(1/p\), its main term is \(O(1)\), but the error is \(O(\sqrt p)\).

Approximating the indicator of an interval of width \(1/p\) by smooth test functions makes matters worse: the relevant Sobolev norms grow with \(p\), so the equidistribution error deteriorates.

Thus even a hypothetical archimedean encoding of the residual event would require a **microscopic discrepancy theorem with absolute error \(O(1)\)**. Katz's ordinary theorem is a fixed-test weak-equidistribution result and does not provide such an estimate.

For exact complex trace zero, the trace-zero hypersurface has Haar measure zero, so weak equidistribution can at most imply

\[
\#\{r:\operatorname{Tr}\Theta_{p,r}=0\}=o(p),
\]

assuming the relevant horizontal theorem. It cannot improve this to \(O(1)\).

And Apéry asks for \(p\)-adic divisibility, not exact complex trace zero.

# 9. The zero detector reveals the genuinely missing transform

The multiplicative Mellin transform acts on the **index group** \(\mathbf F_p^\times\). Detecting whether a Mellin value is zero in \(\mathbf F_p\) requires an additive Fourier transform on the **value group** \(\mathbf F_p\).

Let

\[
\bar M_{p,r}\in\mathbf F_p
\]

be the residual Mellin value. Then

\[
\mathbf 1_{\bar M_{p,r}=0}
 =\frac1p\sum_{u\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(u\bar M_{p,r}),
\]

and hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
Z(p)
 &=\sum_r\mathbf 1_{\bar M_{p,r}=0}\\
 &=\frac{p-1}{p}
   +\frac1p\sum_{u\ne0}
      \underbrace{\sum_r e_p(u\bar M_{p,r})}_{S_p(u)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
Z(p)=\frac{p-1}{p}+rac1p\sum_{u\ne0}S_p(u).
}
\]

This is the exact residual local-limit problem.

A pointwise Weil estimate

\[
|S_p(u)|\ll\sqrt p
\]

would only give

\[
Z(p)\ll\sqrt p.
\]

To obtain a pointwise \(O(1)\) result, one would need, for example,

\[
\sum_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|\ll p,
\]

or enough signed cancellation in the sum over \(u\) to give the same final scale. This is a two-level defining-characteristic cancellation problem. It is not simplified by applying the multiplicative Mellin transform twice.

Indeed,

\[
S_p(u)=\sum_r
 e_p\!\left(u\sum_t\theta_p(t)\chi_r(t)\right)
\]

is nonlinear in the Mellin coefficient. Fourier inversion of the inner linear transform does not linearize the outer exponential zero detector.

This explains structurally why the additive-large-sieve route can fail while the formal double-Mellin identity remains true.

# 10. The precise missing theorem

There are three natural strengths.

## 10.1 Pointwise residual local limit—stronger than the project should expect

After proving the exact integral comparison

\[
\mathscr M_{p,r}^{\mathrm{cris}}
 \equiv u_{p,r}b_r\pmod{\mathfrak p_p},
\]

a theorem sufficient for a universal pointwise constant would be:

> **Microscopic crystalline Mellin local limit.** There exists an absolute \(C\) such that for every good prime \(p\),
> \[
> \#\{r:\mathscr M_{p,r}^{\mathrm{cris}}\equiv0
>                    \pmod{\mathfrak p_p}\}\le C.
> \]

Equivalently, at the zero residue one needs

\[
\#\{r:\bar M_{p,r}=0\}
 =\frac{p-1}{p}+O(1).
\]

This is essentially the desired pointwise statement itself. It is far beyond Katz equidistribution and, according to the repository's Poisson model, is probably false as a uniform bound.

## 10.2 The natural AFP/Cesàro theorem

The statistically compatible first-moment target is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\le X}
 \#\{r:\mathscr M_{p,r}^{\mathrm{cris}}\equiv0
                 \pmod{\mathfrak p_p}\}
 \ll \pi(X).
}
\]

After folding under \(r\leftrightarrow p-1-r\), this is the average \(O(1)\) zero-pair statement and matches the observed mean.

In the language of `proof.tex`, this is an averaged **crystalline Mellin anti-concentration** theorem. It is the correct analogue of the random residual expectation \(1/p\) per frequency.

## 10.3 A sufficient slope-majorant theorem

Let \(E_p\) be the set of tame characters for which the relevant twisted overconvergent \(F\)-isocrystal fails to have the unique unit-root configuration that forces the Mellin trace to be a \(p\)-adic unit. Then the project currently has the implication

\[
\{r:p\mid b_r\}\subseteq E_p,
\]

not necessarily equality. Therefore the stronger estimate

\[
\sum_{p\le X}|E_p|\ll\pi(X)
\]

would imply the average AFP target.

This is a genuinely crystalline theorem about variation of Newton polygons or first Hasse invariants under all tame Kummer twists. It is not an \(\ell\)-adic Sato--Tate theorem.

# 11. Direct answers to (A)--(D)

## (A) Random functions

Yes. For a uniform random \(\mathbf F_p\)-valued function, invertibility of the finite Fourier transform makes the Mellin vector uniform, and the zero count tends exactly to \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\). Reflection changes the independent pair count to \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)\).

This is the correct probabilistic benchmark.

## (B) Bounded-conductor trace functions

Deligne gives square-root bounds and Katz gives character-aspect complex equidistribution under suitable monodromy hypotheses. Neither statement gives residual uniformity modulo the same prime \(p\). The Jacobi example in `proof.tex` shows that fixed rank, conductor, and Weil bounds can coexist with \(\gg p\) bad tame characters.

## (C) The Sato--Tate size heuristic

No. The step

\[
p\mid b_r
\Longrightarrow
b_r/\sqrt p\text{ is close to }0
\]

is false for the cohomological algebraic Mellin value and confuses a chosen \(p\)-adic prime with a complex embedding. If the value were instead a rational integer of size \(O(\sqrt p)\), the only divisible value would be exact zero and the naive lattice probability would be \(1/\sqrt p\), not \(1/p\).

The desired \(1/p\) probability is an independent residual-randomness assertion.

## (D) Fixed-\(k\) joint equidistribution

No. It is not automatically supplied by one-variable Katz equidistribution, and even if granted for every fixed \(k\), it is blind to any exceptional set of size \(o(p)\). It cannot imply a uniform \(O(1)\) zero count.

A rare-event Poisson theorem would require residual factorial-moment estimates at scale \(1/p\), plus tightness. Such a theorem would produce bounded average and a Poisson tail—not a deterministic universal bound.

---

# Final assessment

The double Mellin transform is an exact algebraic identity, but it is a tautological inversion, not a new source of anti-concentration. It does not convert Mellin zeros into the ordinary value distribution of the geometric trace function.

Katz's theory is highly relevant for understanding the **complex character-aspect distribution** and for identifying the correct Tannakian group. It may supply one ingredient in a future proof. But by itself it is categorically at the wrong place:

\[
\text{complex/}\ell\text{-adic and fixed-scale}
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\text{defining-characteristic and scale }1/p.
\]

The missing theorem is therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Apéry-specific crystalline Mellin anti-concentration.}}
\]

For the current project, the strategically correct target is the averaged first-moment bound

\[
\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|\ll\pi(X),
\]

or the corresponding average bound on nonordinary tame twists. Higher residual factorial moments would explain the observed \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)\) law. A uniform pointwise constant is both much stronger and in tension with that same Poisson prediction.
