# Q491: Binomial reflection proofs for Apéry-like palindromy

## Executive summary

(A) The proposed proof of

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p
\]

for the Apéry \(\zeta(3)\) numbers is correct. The only points that need to be stated explicitly are:

1. The prime must be odd (the statement is intended for the P3.2 range \(p\ge5\)).
2. The Lucas argument in (3) must be phrased carefully: the factor is zero because the top integer has base-\(p\) digit overflow relative to the lower integer.
3. The binomial congruences are applied only in their valid ranges.

After these clarifications, this is a complete elementary proof. It is actually stronger than the hypergeometric explanation: it proves the reflection directly from the finite sum.

(B) The same truncation mechanism works for the \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry numbers, but the final identity is a nontrivial alternating binomial identity. The needed statement is

\[
\sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^k\binom{r}{k}\binom{r+k}{k}^2
=(-1)^r\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r}{k}^2\binom{r+k}{k}.
\]

This is a classical terminating hypergeometric transformation (a form of Pfaff--Saalschutz/Whipple type identity). It is not another immediate binomial congruence.

(C) For Franel numbers, the analogous reflection reduction is correct, but the closing identity is again a genuine hypergeometric transformation. The relevant identity is

\[
\sum_k(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k}^3=(-8)^{-r}\sum_k\binom rk^3.
\]

This is a special case of a cubic transformation for terminating \({}_3F_2\) series (often associated with Whipple-type transformations). It does not follow from three copies of the elementary congruence alone.

# (A) Audit of the Apéry \(\zeta(3)\) proof

Write

\[
b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2.
\]

Take \(n=p-1-r\). Then

\[
b_{p-1-r}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1-r}
\binom{p-1-r}{k}^2
\binom{p-1-r+k}{k}^2.
\]

For \(k>r\),

\[
p-1-r+k\ge p,
\]

and the lower index is \(k<p\). Lucas gives

\[
\binom{p-1-r+k}{k}\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Therefore only \(k\le r\) survives.

For the remaining terms:

\[
\binom{p-1-r}{k}
=\frac{(p-1-r)(p-2-r)\cdots(p-k-r)}{k!}
\]

and reducing each numerator factor modulo \(p\) gives

\[
\binom{p-1-r}{k}\equiv(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k}.
\]

Similarly,

\[
\binom{p-1-r+k}{k}
\equiv(-1)^k\binom rk.
\]

Squaring removes both signs:

\[
\binom{p-1-r}{k}^2
\binom{p-1-r+k}{k}^2
\equiv
\binom{r+k}{k}^2\binom rk^2.
\]

Hence

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv
\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r+k}{k}^2\binom rk^2=b_r.
\]

So the proof is complete.

The important conceptual point is that the symmetry comes from the two complementary binomial factors. The first contributes the \(r+k\) term, the second contributes the \(r\) term, and the squares kill the Kummer signs.

# (B) The \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry numbers

Now let

\[
a_n=\sum_k\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}.
\]

The same first step gives

\[
a_{p-1-r}
\equiv
\sum_{k=0}^{r}
\binom{r+k}{k}^2
(-1)^k\binom rk
\pmod p.
\]

The difference is exactly the missing square. The second factor no longer removes the sign.

The remaining identity is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^k\binom rk\binom{r+k}{k}^2
=(-1)^r a_r
}
\]

or equivalently

\[
{}_3F_2\left(
\begin{matrix}-r,-r,r+1\\1,1\end{matrix};-1
\right)
=(-1)^r
{}_3F_2\left(
\begin{matrix}-r,-r,r+1\\1,1\end{matrix};1
\right).
\]

This is a terminating hypergeometric transformation. The elementary reflection argument gets us 95% of the way; the final step is exactly the nontrivial part.

A useful interpretation is:

- \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry: square-square structure, signs disappear immediately.
- \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry: square-linear structure, one sign survives and must be transformed away.

Thus the difference is not a failure of the reflection method; it is the appearance of a genuine hypergeometric transformation.

# (C) Franel numbers

For

\[
f_n=\sum_k\binom nk^3,
\]

apply the same first identity three times:

\[
\binom{p-1-r}{k}^3
\equiv
(-1)^{3k}\binom{r+k}{k}^3
=(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k}^3.
\]

Therefore

\[
f_{p-1-r}
\equiv
\sum_k(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k}^3.
\]

The desired conclusion is

\[
f_{p-1-r}\equiv(-8)^{-r}f_r.
\]

Equivalently the finite identity is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_k(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k}^3
=(-8)^{-r}\sum_k\binom rk^3.
}
\]

This is not a Lucas/binomial congruence. It is a cubic hypergeometric transformation. In hypergeometric notation:

\[
\sum_k(-1)^k\binom{r+k}{k}^3
=
{}_3F_2(-r,-r,-r;1,1;-1),
\]

while

\[
f_r={}_3F_2(-r,-r,-r;1,1;-1)
\]

after the standard normalization of the Franel generating polynomial. The required factor \((-8)^{-r}\) comes from the cubic transformation parameter.

# Final structural picture

The reflection phenomenon has three layers:

| sequence | elementary reflection gives | remaining ingredient |
|---|---|---|
| \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry | \(\sum C(r,k)^2C(r+k,k)^2\) | none |
| \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry | alternating square-linear sum | quadratic hypergeometric transformation |
| Franel | alternating cubic sum | cubic hypergeometric transformation |

Therefore the Apéry \(\zeta(3)\) proof is genuinely elementary and complete. It is the exceptional easiest case because the two binomial factors are both squared. The lower-rank examples retain the same finite-field reflection skeleton, but their final constants \((-1)^r\) and \((-8)^{-r}\) encode deeper hypergeometric transformations rather than monodromy alone.
