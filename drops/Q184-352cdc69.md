# Kloosterman analogy for ψ∘Tr: what is true and what fails

## Short answer

The analogy with Kloosterman sums is structurally correct, but the proposed conclusion is too strong. There is indeed a sheaf-theoretic construction behind

\[
S_c=\sum_j \psi(c\,b_j),\qquad b_j=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_j|M),
\]

but it is not simply an Artin–Schreier pullback of the original rank-2 Mellin sheaf, and bounded rank does not follow from the rank of \(M\). The resulting object is a Fourier transform of an Artin–Schreier twist, and its conductor/rank is controlled by the full geometry of \(M\), not merely by \(\operatorname{rank}(M)=2\).

Therefore this route does not currently prove \(|Z_p|\le 2\).

---

# 1. The correct sheaf construction

Let \(U\) be the character line on which the Mellin sheaf \(M\) lives. Its trace function is

\[
b(j)=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_j|M).
\]

Define the Artin–Schreier sheaf \(\mathcal L_{\psi(c\cdot)}\) on the parameter line. The sum

\[
S_c=\sum_{j\in U(\mathbf F_p)} \psi(c b(j))
\]

is the trace of Frobenius on compactly supported cohomology of the pullback sheaf

\[
\mathcal G_c=(c\cdot b)^*\mathcal L_\psi.
\]

Equivalently, if one forms the family over the c-line,

\[
\mathcal G=(c,b(j))^*\mathcal L_\psi
\]

then

\[
S_c=\sum_i(-1)^i\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_c|H_c^i(U,\mathcal G)).
\]

This is the general Katz formalism for exponential sums of trace functions.

However, this is a cohomological realization, not automatically a low-rank lisse sheaf.

---

# 2. Why Kloosterman is special

For Kloosterman,

\[
K(a)=\sum_x\psi(a(x+x^{-1})),
\]

one starts with the explicit morphism

\[
x\mapsto x+x^{-1}:\mathbf G_m\rightarrow \mathbf A^1.
\]

The fibers have controlled geometry. The Fourier transform of the rank-one Artin–Schreier sheaf along this map produces the rank-2 Kloosterman sheaf.

The rank 2 is a theorem about this specific geometric map.

In the Apéry case the map is

\[
j\mapsto b(j)=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_j|M).
\]

This is not an algebraic function of a geometric coordinate in the same way. It is itself a Frobenius trace function. Applying \(\psi\) to it is a nonlinear operation on trace functions.

There is no general theorem saying:

\[
\text{rank}(M)=2\implies \text{rank of }\psi(\operatorname{Tr}M)=O(1).
\]

---

# 3. What Katz's framework actually gives

The relevant machinery is:

1. Fourier transform of \(\ell\)-adic sheaves.
2. Artin–Schreier sheaves.
3. Exponential sums attached to trace functions.

The philosophy appears throughout Katz's work, including *Exponential Sums and Differential Equations* (Annals of Mathematics Studies 124), especially the chapters on Fourier transform and rigid local systems.

The key point is that if \(\mathcal F\) is a sheaf on a variety X and \(f:X\to\mathbf A^1\) is an algebraic function, then

\[
\sum_x\psi(cf(x))
\]

is controlled by the Fourier transform of \(f_*\mathbf Q_\ell\).

But here \(f=b(j)\) is not known as an algebraic morphism of bounded degree. It is a Frobenius trace function coming from \(M\).

The missing input is exactly a bounded-conductor description of the "exponential sheaf of a trace function".

---

# 4. The rank issue

The desired conclusion would be:

\[
|S_c|\ll\sqrt p.
\]

Deligne would give this if the cohomology realizing \(S_c\) had bounded total Betti number.

But the relevant bound is

\[
|S_c|\leq (\sum_i\dim H_c^i)\sqrt p.
\]

Thus the real problem is bounding

\[
\dim H_c^*(U,(cb)^*\mathcal L_\psi).
\]

This depends on the ramification of the exponential twist. It is not determined by rank 2 alone.

The phrase "rank depends on the ramification of the original sheaf" is the correct intuition. In general, exponential twisting can increase Swan conductors dramatically.

---

# 5. Relation to the Apéry zero problem

If one could prove a uniform bound

\[
\dim H_c^*(U,(cb)^*\mathcal L_\psi)=O(1),
\]

then indeed

\[
S_c=O(\sqrt p).
\]

Fourier inversion would then imply strong anti-concentration of the values \(b_j\). In particular, because

\[
|Z_p|=\frac1p\sum_c\sum_j\psi(cb_j),
\]

one would obtain

\[
|Z_p|\leq O(1).
\]

So the target is exactly right.

But the missing theorem is not the Artin–Schreier pullback itself. The missing theorem is a bounded-conductor theorem for the exponential transform of the Apéry Mellin trace function.

---

# 6. Precise verdict

The argument can be separated into true and false statements.

## True

- \(S_c\) has a cohomological interpretation.
- Artin–Schreier sheaves are the correct objects.
- Deligne's theorem would give square-root cancellation if bounded conductor were known.
- This is analogous in spirit to Kloosterman sums.

## False / unproved

- The resulting sheaf automatically has bounded rank.
- Rank 2 of \(M\) implies bounded rank after \(\psi\circ\operatorname{Tr}\).
- Katz's general formalism alone gives \(|S_c|\ll\sqrt p\).

The Kloosterman miracle comes from an explicit geometric map with rigid local monodromy. For the Apéry Mellin sheaf, establishing the analogous rigidity/bounded-conductor statement is essentially the missing theorem.

Hence this is a promising reformulation of the problem, but not yet a proof of \(|Z_p|\le2\).
