# Projective-orbit/Casoratian audit of the prime-size barrier

## Verdict

The projective formulation does not prove the desired implication
\[
 p>s^2 \Longrightarrow \text{no off-center primitive four-return orbit outside }\mathfrak U_s.
\]
The Casoratian and palindrome give strong constraints, but they do not force the visited projective point to be the distinguished point \([0:1]\), nor do they force a contradiction. The missing ingredient is a genuine orbit-rigidity statement for the Apéry projective dynamical system.

The obstruction is structural: the identities control a two-dimensional vector evolution, while a four-return configuration only imposes that four projective points coincide. Equality of projective points gives rank-one equations; the Casoratian supplies nonvanishing determinants, but it does not compare different return times strongly enough.

## 1. Exact projective equations

Let
\[
B_n=n!^3b_n,
\]
and let \(D_n\) be the companion solution normalized so that
\[
B_nD_{n+1}-D_nB_{n+1}=(n!)^6.
\]
Write
\[
\pi_n=[B_n:D_n]\in \mathbf P^1.
\]
The transfer matrix has the form
\[
\binom{B_{n+1}}{D_{n+1}}
=M_n\binom{B_n}{D_n},
\]
where
\[
M_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)&-n^6\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}
\]
(up to the chosen companion normalization).

A return
\[
\pi_{r+h}=\pi_r
\]
means
\[
B_{r+h}D_r-D_{r+h}B_r=0.
\tag{1}
\]
The Casoratian gives instead
\[
B_rD_{r+1}-D_rB_{r+1}=(r!)^6\neq0
\]
for primes outside the factorial carrier. Therefore adjacent returns are impossible, matching the no-two-consecutive-zero statement.

For four primitive returns
\[
\pi_r=\pi_{r+a}=\pi_{r+a+b}=\pi_{r+s},
\]
we obtain three independent equations
\[
\det(v_r,v_{r+a})=0,
\]
\[
\det(v_r,v_{r+a+b})=0,
\]
\[
\det(v_r,v_{r+s})=0,
\]
where \(v_n=(B_n,D_n)^T\).

These say all four vectors lie in the same one-dimensional subspace. They do not say which subspace.

## 2. Why the Casoratian does not force \([0:1]\)

Assume
\[
\pi_r=[u:v].
\]
A return after a transfer product \(T_h=M_{r+h-1}\cdots M_r\) gives
\[
T_h(u,v)^T=\lambda_h(u,v)^T.
\tag{2}
\]
Thus the common projective line is an eigenline of each return matrix.

The four-return condition becomes
\[
T_a w=\lambda_1w,
\quad
T_{a+b}w=\lambda_2w,
\quad
T_sw=\lambda_3w.
\tag{3}
\]
There is no identity forcing
\[
w=(0,1).
\]
The point \([0:1]\) corresponds to \(B_r=0\), i.e. an actual Apéry zero. But a projective coincidence only gives a repeated line, not vanishing of the first coordinate.

The Casoratian prevents two neighboring transfer images from collapsing, but it does not prevent a longer word in \(SL_2\)-type dynamics from returning to a nonzero eigenline.

## 3. Palindrome contribution

The Apéry reflection gives
\[
\pi_n=\pi_{p-1-n}.
\tag{4}
\]
This identifies the orbit under the involution
\[
n\mapsto p-1-n.
\]
For a four-return block of span \(s\), if it intersects the reflection center then additional equations appear. This is the mechanism behind the central-factor obstruction.

However, the condition
\[
p>s^2
\]
does not imply that the block meets the center. A block can sit in an off-center fiber and its reflected copy is a disjoint second block.

The Q7119 large-span example demonstrates this combinatorial possibility; changing to the mesoscopic regime removes that witness but does not prove the converse.

## 4. A model satisfying the exact identities

The identities alone admit the following abstract model.

Take a finite field \(\mathbf F_p\) and define vectors \(v_n\in\mathbf F_p^2\) satisfying
\[
\det(v_n,v_{n+1})=1.
\tag{5}
\]
Choose a nonzero vector \(w\), and choose transfer matrices
\[
M_n\in SL_2(\mathbf F_p)
\]
with
\[
M_nw=w
\]
at four separated times, while all intermediate matrices preserve determinant one.

Then:

* the Casoratian identity holds;
* four primitive projective returns occur;
* the common line is \([w]\), not necessarily \([0:1]\);
* no structural prime is required.

The only extra Apéry-specific condition not represented in this model is that the matrices are generated by the polynomial coefficient
\[
P(n)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5.
\]
Therefore the remaining possible route is not Casoratian algebra; it is a rigidity theorem exploiting this exact cubic transfer law.

## 5. What new invariant would close the gap?

A sufficient missing lemma would be:

**Projective orbit rigidity lemma.** For \(p\nmid\mathfrak U_s\), every projective point with four primitive visits in an interval of length \(s\) is either

1. the zero line \([0:1]\), or
2. supported by a structural prime.

Equivalently:
\[
\#\{n\in[r,r+s]:\pi_n=L\}\ge4
\Longrightarrow
L=[0:1]\ \text{or}\ p\mid\mathfrak U_s.
\]

No current consequence of the Casoratian, reflection, or degree-three transfer coefficient proves this.

A weaker useful invariant would be a height/energy bound for common eigenlines of short transfer products:
\[
\sum_{h_1,h_2,h_3\le s}
\#\{L:T_{h_i}L=L\ \forall i\}
\ll s^{3+o(1)}.
\]
This would be genuinely stronger than resultant height estimates because it counts projective orbit intersections directly.

## Final conclusion

The projective reformulation sharpens the problem but does not solve the size barrier.

The exact matrix equations show:

* four returns imply a common eigenline;
* the Casoratian only excludes adjacent collapse;
* palindrome only pairs fibers by reflection;
* neither forces the common line to be \([0:1]\).

Therefore the prime-size barrier is not broken by the existing projective identities. The missing theorem is a new Apéry-specific projective orbit rigidity or short-word eigenline-counting statement.
