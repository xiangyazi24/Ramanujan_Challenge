# P3.2 dynamical-orbit route: theorem audit for the Apéry character sum

## Executive verdict

**No existing Ostafe–Shparlinski/Bourgain–Chang orbit theorem gives an off-the-shelf bound**

$$
\max_{a\in\mathbf F_p^\times}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}e_p(a b_j)\right|
\le C p^{1-\delta}
$$

with fixed $\delta>0$ and a constant $C$ independent of $p$ for the Apéry sequence.

There are several independent obstructions.

1. The natural two-dimensional Apéry evolution is a **non-autonomous linear cocycle** $F_n$, whereas the pointwise Ostafe–Shparlinski bounds are for iterations of one autonomous map.
2. Ostafe–Shparlinski do have a theorem allowing a different map at every step, but it is for a very rigid **triangular family of permutation polynomial systems** and is a mean-square/almost-all-initial-values theorem. The Apéry companion maps are not in that triangular family, and the theorem does not control the distinguished seed $(5,1)$.
3. Adding the time coordinate makes the system autonomous, but the resulting map is **rational**, has a pole at $t=-1$, is not a global permutation of $\mathbf A^3(\mathbf F_p)$, and is not known to be rationally conjugate to the triangular systems treated in the literature. It is not a polynomial map of degree $3$.
4. Even if one ignores those structural failures and formally inserts $N=p$ into the published pointwise estimates in dimension $2$ or $3$, the estimates are **worse than the trivial bound**. Their stated nontrivial ranges begin at roughly $N\ge p^{1+\varepsilon}$, $p^{3/2+\varepsilon}$, $p^{2+\varepsilon}$, or $p^{5/2+\varepsilon}$, depending on the theorem and dimension.

The exponent $\delta=1/4$ does appear in this circle of ideas, but only as an **average-over-seeds exponent in dimension two**. It does not apply to the fixed Apéry seed. A $p^{3/4+o(1)}$ bound would instead follow from a new Apéry-specific square-root estimate for shifted correlations; this is a realistic formulation of the missing theorem, but it is not supplied by Ostafe–Shparlinski.

## 1. The correct dynamical formulation

Put

$$
P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5.
$$

For $1\le n\le p-2$, define

$$
M_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)/(n+1)^3 & -n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_n=\binom{b_n}{b_{n-1}}.
$$

Then

```text
v_1 = (5,1)^T,

v_{n+1} = M_n v_n       (1 <= n <= p-2).
```

Thus the genuinely correct non-autonomous maps are

$$
F_n(x,y)=\left(\frac{P(n)x-n^3y}{(n+1)^3},x\right).
$$

The seed should therefore be $(b_1,b_0)=(5,1)$, not $(b_0,b_1)=(1,5)$ if the first component is meant to be the current term. The complete sum can be written as

$$
S_p(a)=e_p(a b_0)+\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}e_p\bigl(a\pi_1(v_n)\bigr).
$$

There is no denominator problem in the transitions needed to produce $b_1,\ldots,b_{p-1}$: the last transition used is $n=p-2$. Moreover,

$$
\det M_n=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\ne0
\qquad(1\le n\le p-2),
$$

so every map in the relevant two-dimensional cocycle is an invertible linear map. The singular step $n=0$ can be discarded by starting at $v_1$, and the pole at $n=p-1$ occurs only when one attempts to continue past the required segment.

The relevant object is therefore

$$
v_n=M_{n-1}M_{n-2}\cdots M_1v_1,
$$

not a power $M^n v_1$ of one fixed matrix and not an iteration $F^n$ of one fixed map of $\mathbf A^2$.

## 2. What the Ostafe–Shparlinski theorems actually say

### 2.1 The triangular polynomial class

Ostafe and Shparlinski, *Pseudorandom Numbers and Hash Functions from Iterations of Multivariate Polynomials* (Cryptography and Communications 2 (2010), 49–67; arXiv:0908.4519), consider systems in $m+1$ variables of the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_0&=X_0G_0(X_1,\ldots,X_m)+H_0(X_1,\ldots,X_m),\\
F_1&=X_1G_1(X_2,\ldots,X_m)+H_1(X_2,\ldots,X_m),\\
&\hspace{15mm}\vdots\\
F_{m-1}&=X_{m-1}G_{m-1}(X_m)+H_{m-1}(X_m),\\
F_m&=g_mX_m+h_m.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each $G_i$ must have a prescribed unique leading monomial

$$
g_iX_{i+1}^{s_{i,i+1}}\cdots X_m^{s_{i,m}},
$$

and the crucial chain condition is

$$
s_{0,1}s_{1,2}\cdots s_{m-1,m}\ne0.
$$

This is much more restrictive than “a polynomial map of bounded degree.” It is the mechanism that makes the degrees of the iterates separate in a controlled way and makes the differenced phases nonzero polynomials.

### 2.2 Their pointwise theorem is autonomous

Theorem 4 of that paper assumes that **the same system is used at every step**, $\mathcal F_k=\mathcal F$. If the orbit is purely periodic of period $\tau$, $N\le\tau$, and $\mathbf a\in\mathbf F_p^m$ is nonzero, then for every fixed integer $\nu\ge1$,

$$
|S_{\mathbf a}(N)|
\ll
N^{1-\beta_{m,\nu}}p^{\alpha_{m,\nu}},
$$

where

$$
\alpha_{m,\nu}
=\frac{m^2+m\nu+m}{2\nu(m+\nu)},
\qquad
\beta_{m,\nu}=\frac1{2\nu}.
$$

The implied constant depends on the fixed degree, $m$, and $\nu$. The authors explicitly note that this estimate is nontrivial only in the range

$$
N\ge p^{m+\varepsilon}.
$$

Here the total state dimension is $m+1$. Consequently:

- a two-dimensional state has $m=1$ and needs $N\ge p^{1+\varepsilon}$;
- a three-dimensional state has $m=2$ and needs $N\ge p^{2+\varepsilon}$.

The Apéry sum has only $N=p$. Thus even a perfect structural match would not put it in the nontrivial range.

### 2.3 They do allow non-autonomous systems, but only in an average theorem

The same paper genuinely permits a sequence of distinct systems

$$
\mathcal F_k\in\mathfrak F(S,m),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,
$$

all belonging to the same triangular class and sharing the same exponent matrix $S$.

Theorem 8 assumes, in addition, that every $\mathcal F_k$ is a permutation polynomial system. For the truncated $m$ coordinates it bounds the second moment over all $p^{m+1}$ initial vectors:

$$
U_{\mathbf a,c}(M,N)
=
\sum_{\mathbf w\in\mathbf F_p^{m+1}}
\left|
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
 e_p\!\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}a_jF_j^{(n)}(\mathbf w)\right)e_M(cn)
\right|^2,
$$

and gives

$$
U_{\mathbf a,c}(M,N)
\ll
\begin{cases}
Np^{m+1},&N\le p^{1/(m+1)},\\[2mm]
N^2p^{m(m+2)/(m+1)},&N>p^{1/(m+1)}.
\end{cases}
$$

Equivalently, if $d=m+1$ is the state dimension, the long-range second-moment bound is

$$
U\ll N^2p^{d-1/d}.
$$

Corollary 9 converts this into a discrepancy bound for all but $O(\varepsilon p^{m+1})$ initial vectors. In the long range the saving is

$$
p^{-1/(2(m+1))}=p^{-1/(2d)}
$$

up to logarithms.

This answers question (1) precisely:

> Ostafe–Shparlinski do handle a non-autonomous sequence of maps, but only for their fixed triangular polynomial class, and the strong result is an average/almost-all-seeds result. Their pointwise theorem is autonomous.

It is not a theorem for arbitrary bounded-degree non-autonomous rational maps.

### 2.4 The rational-system paper supplies a nonconstancy lemma, not a general orbit bound

Ostafe and Shparlinski, *Degree Growth, Linear Independence and Periods of a Class of Rational Dynamical Systems* (Contemporary Mathematics 574 (2012), 131–143; arXiv:1109.0575), treat autonomous triangular rational systems of the shape

$$
F_i=X_i^{e_i}G_i(X_{i+1},\ldots,X_m)+H_i(X_{i+1},\ldots,X_m),
\qquad e_i\in\{1,-1\},
$$

with analogous leading-monomial and chain conditions.

Their Theorem 3 proves that, for $k\ne \ell$ and nonzero truncated coefficient vector $\mathbf a$, the rational function

$$
Q_{k,\ell,\mathbf a}
=
\sum_i a_i\bigl(F_i^{(k)}-F_i^{(\ell)}\bigr)
$$

is nonconstant. This is the algebraic input needed by standard character-sum arguments. It is not a theorem saying that every rational dynamical system with a nonconstant coordinate has cancellation along each fixed orbit.

### 2.5 The rational-conjugacy theorem is also autonomous and only nontrivial on very long orbits

Gómez-Pérez, Ostafe, and Shparlinski, *Algebraic Entropy, Automorphisms and Sparsity of Algebraic Dynamical Systems and Pseudorandom Number Generators* (Mathematics of Computation 83 (2014), 1535–1550, DOI 10.1090/S0025-5718-2013-02780-9), start with an autonomous triangular rational system $\mathcal F$ and conjugate it by a rational automorphism $\mathcal A$:

$$
\mathcal R=\mathcal A\circ\mathcal F\circ\mathcal A^{-1}.
$$

The automorphism must satisfy a specific **degree-separation property**: the selected leading-degree/leading-variable data of its supported components must be pairwise distinct.

Their Theorem 6 gives, for a trajectory of length $T$ and $N\le T$,

$$
|S_I(\mathbf a;N)|
\ll
p^{\alpha_{m,\nu}}N^{1-\beta_{m,\nu}},
$$

where now $m$ is the actual state dimension and

$$
\alpha_{m,\nu}
=
\frac{m}{2\nu}-\frac{1}{4(m+\nu-1)},
\qquad
\beta_{m,\nu}=\frac1{2\nu}.
$$

They state that, for fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and sufficiently large $\nu$, this becomes nontrivial only when

$$
N\ge p^{m-1/2+\varepsilon}.
$$

Thus it requires

- $N\ge p^{3/2+\varepsilon}$ in dimension $2$;
- $N\ge p^{5/2+\varepsilon}$ in dimension $3$.

Their Theorem 8 gives stronger estimates for almost every initial value when the system is a permutation. In the long range its discrepancy saving is only

$$
p^{-1/(4m)}
$$

up to logarithms: $p^{-1/8}$ in dimension $2$ and $p^{-1/12}$ in dimension $3$. Again, this is not a statement for the distinguished Apéry seed.

### 2.6 The “essentially arbitrary polynomial system” paper does not repair the mismatch

Ostafe, Pelican, and Shparlinski, *On Pseudorandom Numbers from Multivariate Polynomial Systems* (Finite Fields and Their Applications 16 (2010), 320–328, DOI 10.1016/j.ffa.2010.05.002), does treat essentially arbitrary **autonomous polynomial** systems without needing a useful degree-growth formula, provided that the orbit is sufficiently long.

It still does not directly apply here:

- the natural autonomous Apéry lift is rational, not polynomial;
- the natural polynomial rescaling described below produces $(n!)^3b_n$, not $b_n$;
- replacing inversion by a polynomial function over $\mathbf F_p$ introduces degree depending on $p$;
- the target segment has length only $p$.

So this broader paper is not a hidden route around the obstructions below.

## 3. Why the non-autonomous Apéry maps do not satisfy the triangular hypotheses

Write

$$
A(t)=\frac{P(t)}{(t+1)^3},
\qquad
B(t)=-\frac{t^3}{(t+1)^3}.
$$

The fiber map is

$$
(x,y)\longmapsto (A(t)x+B(t)y,x).
$$

In two variables the Ostafe–Shparlinski triangular form would be

$$
(X_0,X_1)\longmapsto
\bigl(X_0G_0(X_1)+H_0(X_1),\,g_1X_1+h_1\bigr).
$$

The Apéry second coordinate is the old first coordinate, not an affine function of the old second coordinate. Swapping the two coordinates changes the map to

$$
(X_0,X_1)\longmapsto
\bigl(X_1,\,B(t)X_0+A(t)X_1\bigr),
$$

which is still not triangular: the second output depends on the earlier coordinate $X_0$, while the first output has zero multiplier of its own coordinate.

This is not just a poor choice of coordinate order. For $p\ge7$, the family of companion matrices $M_t$ has no common invariant line, so it cannot be simultaneously triangularized by one fixed linear change of fiber coordinates.

Indeed, a finite-slope common invariant line would be spanned by $(r,1)$. Since

$$
M_t(r,1)^T
=
\left(\frac{P(t)r-t^3}{(t+1)^3},r\right)^T,
$$

invariance forces the polynomial identity

$$
rP(t)-t^3=r^2(t+1)^3.
$$

Comparing constant terms gives $5r=r^2$, hence $r=0$ or $r=5$. The choice $r=0$ contradicts the coefficient of $t^3$. For $r=5$, comparison of the $t^2$ coefficients gives $255=75$, impossible modulo every prime $p\ge7$. The line spanned by $(1,0)$ is not invariant either.

Therefore:

- the maps are permutations for the individual steps $1\le t\le p-2$;
- but they are not permutation systems in the **specific triangular class** required by Theorem 8;
- an $n$-dependent change of basis would not help, because the theorem requires all maps to lie in one fixed coordinate system and one fixed class $\mathfrak F(S,m)$.

There may conceivably be a more complicated rational conjugacy, but no such conjugacy is presently available, and the published theorem cannot be invoked until it is explicitly constructed and its degree-separation hypotheses are checked.

### A useful but insufficient direct average identity

Because the Apéry cocycle is linear in its initial vector, one can see especially clearly why an average-over-seeds theorem does not settle the fixed seed.

Let $\ell_n\in(\mathbf F_p^2)^*$ be the row vector such that, for an arbitrary seed $w\in\mathbf F_p^2$, the observed first coordinate at time $n$ is $\ell_n(w)$. Then orthogonality gives the exact identity

$$
\sum_{w\in\mathbf F_p^2}
\left|\sum_{n<N}e_p(a\ell_n(w))\right|^2
=
p^2\#\{(n,r):\ell_n=\ell_r\}.
$$

If the coefficient rows were all distinct, the root-mean-square size over seeds would even be $N^{1/2}$. But this identity says nothing pointwise about the exceptional seed $w=(5,1)$. It illustrates the central logical gap in trying to use the Ostafe–Shparlinski mean-square result for Apéry.

## 4. Autonomizing by adding the time variable

The natural autonomous lift is

$$
\mathcal T(t,x,y)
=
\left(
 t+1,
 \frac{P(t)x-t^3y}{(t+1)^3},
 x
\right).
$$

This is a perfectly useful way to regard the recurrence as a skew-product, but it does not put the problem inside the cited theorems.

### 4.1 It is rational, not polynomial

The numerator of the middle coordinate has total degree $4$ in $(t,x,y)$ and the denominator has degree $3$. Thus $\mathcal T$ is a fixed-degree rational map with a pole on $t=-1$, not a polynomial map of degree $3$.

On its domain it is birational: if

$$
(t',x',y')=\mathcal T(t,x,y),
$$

then $t=t'-1$, $x=y'$, and

$$
y=\frac{P(t)y'-(t+1)^3x'}{t^3}.
$$

The inverse has its own singular divisor $t=0$. Hence this is not a global polynomial automorphism or a permutation of all of $\mathbf A^3(\mathbf F_p)$.

Starting from $(t,x,y)=(1,5,1)$, the map reproduces the required segment through $t=p-1=-1$, but the next step is exactly at the pole. One can assign an arbitrary value at the pole, as some rational-dynamics papers do, but then one has introduced a patched finite-state map that is no longer the natural rational transformation and still does not satisfy the triangular-conjugacy hypotheses.

### 4.2 Fermat polynomialization has degree depending on $p$

For $t\ne-1$,

$$
(t+1)^{-3}=(t+1)^{p-4}.
$$

Thus one can represent the middle coordinate on the punctured fibers by

$$
\bigl(P(t)x-t^3y\bigr)(t+1)^{p-4}.
$$

But this polynomial has total degree about $p$. At $t=-1$ it collapses the fiber, so the resulting map is not a permutation. More importantly, the constants in the Ostafe–Shparlinski estimates depend on the degree of the map. Replacing a fixed rational map by a degree-$p$ polynomial therefore cannot yield a bound with a uniform constant $C$.

Any interpolation that patches the pole while prescribing values on all $t\in\mathbf F_p$ has the same basic problem: the algebraic complexity grows with $p$.

### 4.3 A polynomial rescaling changes the observable

There is a genuine fixed-degree polynomial recurrence after setting

$$
c_n=(n!)^3b_n.
$$

The Apéry recurrence becomes

$$
c_{n+1}=P(n)c_n-n^6c_{n-1}.
$$

Consequently

$$
(t,u,v)\longmapsto
\bigl(t+1,\,P(t)u-t^6v,\,u\bigr)
$$

is an autonomous polynomial map of total degree $7$ producing the $c_n$.

However, the required additive phase is

$$
e_p(a b_n)=e_p\bigl(a c_n(n!)^{-3}\bigr),
$$

not $e_p(a c_n)$. To recover $b_n$ as a coordinate, one must also evolve $q_n=(n!)^{-3}$ via

$$
q_{n+1}=\frac{q_n}{(n+1)^3},
$$

which reintroduces a rational coordinate, or use a degree-$p$ polynomial representation of inversion. The published orbit theorems concern linear combinations of coordinates of the polynomial orbit, not this $n$-dependent rational observable.

Thus the factorial rescaling does not transfer the desired character sum to a fixed-degree polynomial orbit sum.

### 4.4 What a triangularizing conjugacy would have to solve

A time-dependent fiber line spanned by $(r(t),1)$ would be invariant precisely when

$$
r(t)r(t+1)=A(t)r(t)+B(t).
$$

This is the Riccati equation associated with the Apéry recurrence. A rational solution $r(t)$ would amount to a rational/hypergeometric-ratio solution of the second-order recurrence and might lead to a triangularization of the fiber cocycle.

No such rational conjugacy is part of the existing P3.2 infrastructure, and it should not be assumed. Finding one would be a substantive new algebraic result; proving that none exists would be a useful way to close this particular triangularization loophole. Even if such a conjugacy existed, one would still face the published pointwise theorems' $N\gg p$ threshold.

## 5. What “non-degeneracy” means in these papers

There is no single general hypothesis called “the orbit is non-degenerate” that can be checked by observing that the sequence is nonconstant. The relevant condition is theorem-specific.

### 5.1 In the 2010 triangular polynomial theorem

The required conditions are:

1. triangular form;
2. a unique prescribed leading monomial in every multiplier $G_i$;
3. the nonzero chain $s_{0,1}\cdots s_{m-1,m}\ne0$;
4. fixed total degree and, for the pointwise theorem, one autonomous map;
5. pure periodicity/adequate trajectory length.

These conditions imply that after applying Hölder and differencing, the polynomial phase

$$
\sum_i a_i\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}
\bigl(F_i^{(k_j)}-F_i^{(\ell_j)}\bigr)
$$

is nonzero except for the diagonal permutations of the shift tuples. The zero-counting estimate then saves one power of $p$ in an average over the state variables.

The Apéry companion system fails the triangular and chain conditions before one reaches this analytic step.

### 5.2 In the rational/conjugated theorem

The base system must satisfy the triangular rational leading-term conditions, and the conjugating rational automorphism must have degree separation. These force the differenced rational phase to be nonconstant in at least one variable, after which a one-variable Weil bound is applied. Over general finite fields one must also rule out Artin–Schreier degeneracy of the form $H^p-H+c$; for fixed bounded degrees over prime fields this issue is normally absorbed by the stated hypotheses.

For the Apéry lift:

- no suitable triangular base system has been identified;
- no rational automorphism with degree separation has been constructed;
- the fixed linear simultaneous triangularization is impossible for $p\ge7$;
- therefore the theorem's non-degeneracy hypothesis is **not verified**.

It is true that generic differences of the Apéry iterates are nonconstant linear functions of the initial vector. That fact can help with averages over initial vectors, but it does not imply cancellation along the one fixed orbit $(5,1)$.

## 6. Exponent audit at the actual length $N=p$

The failure is quantitative even before the structural hypotheses are checked.

### 6.1 Ostafe–Shparlinski Theorem 4

At $N=p$, the claimed saving would be

$$
\delta=\beta_{m,\nu}-\alpha_{m,\nu}.
$$

For a hypothetical two-dimensional autonomous realization, $m=1$, and

$$
\delta=-\frac{1}{2\nu(\nu+1)}<0.
$$

Thus the theorem would give

$$
|S(a)|\ll p^{1+1/(2\nu(\nu+1))},
$$

which is weaker than $|S(a)|\le p$.

For the three-dimensional time lift, $m=2$, and

$$
\delta=-\frac{\nu+4}{2\nu(\nu+2)}<0.
$$

Again there is no saving. This agrees with the paper's explicit nontriviality threshold $N\ge p^{m+\varepsilon}$.

### 6.2 Gómez-Pérez–Ostafe–Shparlinski Theorem 6

Here

$$
\delta
=
\beta_{m,\nu}-\alpha_{m,\nu}
=
\frac{1-m}{2\nu}+\frac{1}{4(m+\nu-1)}.
$$

In dimension $2$,

$$
\delta=-\frac{\nu+2}{4\nu(\nu+1)}<0,
$$

and in dimension $3$,

$$
\delta=-\frac1\nu+\frac{1}{4(\nu+2)}<0.
$$

So this theorem is also trivial at $N=p$, in agreement with its threshold $N\ge p^{m-1/2+\varepsilon}$.

### 6.3 Where $\delta=1/4$ actually appears

Apply the 2010 non-autonomous mean-square theorem formally in state dimension $d=m+1=2$ and take $N=p$. Its long-range estimate gives

$$
U\ll p^2p^{3/2}=p^{7/2}.
$$

After division by the $p^2$ initial vectors and taking a square root, the root-mean-square orbit sum is

$$
p^{3/4}.
$$

This is exactly an average-seed exponent $\delta=1/4$.

In dimension $3$, the same calculation gives a root-mean-square scale

$$
p^{5/6},
$$

corresponding to $\delta=1/6$.

These exponents do **not** prove anything for the single seed $(5,1)$, and the Apéry maps do not satisfy the theorem's triangular hypotheses anyway. Thus $\delta=1/4$ is realistic as a heuristic/generic-seed scale, but not as a currently available theorem for Apéry.

## 7. Bourgain and Chang do not supply a generic orbit theorem here

A standard consequence of the Bourgain–Glibichuk–Konyagin sum-product theorem is the following qualitative subgroup estimate: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\eta(\varepsilon)>0$ such that, if $H\le\mathbf F_p^\times$ and $|H|\ge p^\varepsilon$, then

$$
\max_{a\ne0}
\left|\sum_{h\in H}e_p(ah)\right|
\ll |H|p^{-\eta(\varepsilon)}.
$$

Bourgain–Chang obtain analogous power/Gauss-sum estimates over arbitrary finite fields under hypotheses excluding concentration in proper subfields.

These results apply when the summation set has multiplicative-subgroup, power-map, or related sum-product structure. They do not say that every coordinate sequence of an algebraic dynamical system has cancellation.

The place where Bourgain-type input enters the 2010 Ostafe–Shparlinski paper is revealing: it is used for the **last affine coordinate**

$$
u_{n,m}=g_m^n u_{0,m}+\cdots,
$$

whose evolution is controlled by the multiplicative order of $g_m$. The Apéry coordinate is instead generated by the variable companion product

$$
M_{n-1}M_{n-2}\cdots M_1.
$$

There is no fixed element $g$, no fixed matrix power $M^n$, and no proved multiplicative subgroup or small-doubling description of the set $\{b_0,\ldots,b_{p-1}\}$. Consequently the Bourgain–Chang subgroup machinery has no direct object on which to act.

One could attempt a new expansion theorem for the non-autonomous matrix cocycle, but that would be a new result, not an application of the cited theorems.

## 8. A realistic $\delta=1/4$ target: shifted correlations

Although the published dynamical-orbit bounds do not apply, they point to the right analytic scale. Define

$$
C_h(a)=
\sum_{n=0}^{p-1-h}
 e_p\bigl(a(b_{n+h}-b_n)\bigr).
$$

Suppose one could prove, uniformly for $a\ne0$ and
$1\le h\le p^{1/2}$,

$$
|C_h(a)|\ll p^{1/2+o(1)}.
$$

Van der Corput's inequality gives, for any $H\le p$,

$$
|S_p(a)|^2
\ll
\frac{p}{H}
\left(
 p+\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}|C_h(a)|
\right)
$$

up to harmless endpoint constants. Taking $H=p^{1/2}$ and using the proposed square-root correlation bound yields

$$
|S_p(a)|^2\ll p^{3/2+o(1)},
\qquad
|S_p(a)|\ll p^{3/4+o(1)}.
$$

Thus $\delta=1/4$ is a natural, concrete target, but the missing theorem is:

> **Apéry shifted-correlation theorem.** Uniform square-root cancellation for the phases $b_{n+h}-b_n$ for shifts $h\le p^{1/2}$.

That theorem is more naturally attacked using the Apéry diagonal/Picard–Fuchs/Mellin-sheaf structure and geometric irreducibility of shifted products than by generic algebraic dynamics. In particular, it asks for cancellation tied to the distinguished Apéry solution, exactly where average-over-seeds methods lose information.

## 9. Hypothesis checklist

| Requirement | Natural $2$D cocycle | Autonomous $3$D lift |
|---|---:|---:|
| One fixed autonomous map | **No** | Yes, as a rational map |
| Fixed-degree polynomial map | Each step is linear, but varies | **No** for $b_n$; rational with a pole |
| Global permutation of the state space | Each used fiber map is invertible | **No** naturally |
| Ostafe–Shparlinski triangular form | **No** | **No known conjugacy** |
| Nonzero leading-exponent chain | **Fails/not defined** | **Not verified** |
| Degree-separating rational conjugacy | Not applicable | **Not constructed** |
| Fixed distinguished seed covered | Pointwise theorem unavailable | Pointwise theorem quantitatively trivial |
| Length in nontrivial theorem range | $N=p$ is too short | $N=p$ is far too short |
| Uniform degree independent of $p$ after polynomialization | **No** | **No** |

The conclusion is robust: removing any one obstruction does not remove the others.

## 10. Direct answers to the four questions

### (1) Autonomous or non-autonomous?

The principal pointwise Ostafe–Shparlinski exponential-sum theorem is autonomous. Their 2010 paper does allow a different polynomial system at each step, but only in a fixed triangular class, and the resulting strong theorem is a second-moment/almost-all-initial-values statement. It does not provide a pointwise estimate for an arbitrary non-autonomous rational orbit or for the fixed Apéry seed.

### (2) Can one make the Apéry system autonomous?

Yes, formally, by adding time:

$$
(t,x,y)\mapsto
\left(t+1,\frac{P(t)x-t^3y}{(t+1)^3},x\right).
$$

But this is a rational map with numerator degree $4$, denominator degree $3$, and a pole at $t=-1$; it is not a polynomial map of degree $3$. Polynomializing inversion over $\mathbf F_p$ produces degree about $p$ and loses uniformity/permutation. The factorial rescaling gives a degree-$7$ polynomial system for $(n!)^3b_n$, but not for the desired phase $b_n$.

### (3) What is non-degeneracy, and does Apéry satisfy it?

In the relevant papers it means triangular leading-monomial/chain conditions, or after rational conjugacy, degree separation guaranteeing that all required differenced phases are nonconstant rational functions. The Apéry system does not satisfy the triangular condition in its natural coordinates, cannot be simultaneously linearly triangularized for $p\ge7$, and has no known degree-separating rational conjugacy. Therefore the published non-degeneracy hypotheses are not met.

### (4) What $\delta$ is available? Is $1/4$ realistic?

No positive $\delta$ follows from the published pointwise estimates at $N=p$; formal substitution gives a bound worse than $p$. The value $\delta=1/4$ occurs as the root-mean-square exponent for a two-dimensional triangular non-autonomous permutation system averaged over all seeds. It is not a fixed-seed theorem. For Apéry, $\delta=1/4$ is realistic as the consequence of a new square-root shifted-correlation theorem, but not as an immediate Ostafe–Shparlinski/Bourgain–Chang corollary.
