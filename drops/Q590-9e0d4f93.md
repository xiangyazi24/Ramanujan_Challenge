# HTO and why Apéry differs from Jacobi

## Summary

The Jacobi counterexample changes the target. Bounded rank, bounded conductor, overconvergence, and even a clean arithmetic Fourier transform do **not** imply AFP. The missing ingredient is genuinely horizontal: one must control the interaction of the Frobenius crystal with the entire tame-character direction.

For Apéry, the plausible theorem is indeed the proposed HTO statement:

\[
\sum_{p\le X}\#\{r:H_{p,r}=0\}\ll \pi(X),
\]

where \(H_{p,r}\) is the first-break Hasse scalar of the Gauss-normalized Kummer-twisted crystal.

However, this does not follow formally from the fact that the Apéry motive has \(SL_2\)/\(Sp_4\) monodromy. The difference from Jacobi is qualitative rather than an automatic theorem.

---

## (A) Why Jacobi mass vanishing occurs

For a rank-one Kummer object the Mellin transform is essentially one Gauss/Jacobi sum:

\[
M_j=J(\omega^{-j},\psi).
\]

Gross--Koblitz converts this into a single product of \(\Gamma_p\)-values. Stickelberger gives

\[
v_p(M_j)=\sum_i \{\alpha_i j\}-\sum_i\{\beta_i j\}.
\]

The right side is a deterministic piecewise-linear function of \(j\). A whole interval of characters can cross the threshold

\[
v_p(M_j)\ge1,
\]

so the reduction modulo \(p\) vanishes for a positive proportion of characters.

This is a rank-one phenomenon: there is a single slope contribution and no competing Frobenius directions.

---

## (B) The Apéry analogue of Stickelberger

For the Apéry crystal, after Kummer twisting by \(\omega^r\), one has a rank-three (or rank-four before reduction) crystalline object with Hodge slopes

\[
0,1,2 \quad (\text{effective rank-3 piece}),
\]

or

\[
0,1,2,3
\]

for the full Apéry motive.

The Frobenius trace is not one Gamma product. Gross--Koblitz gives a sum of terms of the form

\[
T_r=\sum_i c_i(r)\prod_m\Gamma_p(\alpha_{i,m}r+\beta_{i,m}),
\]

where the individual summands have controlled valuations.

The first-break scalar \(H_{p,r}\) is therefore not governed by one Stickelberger polygon. It is the determinant/minor measuring whether several Frobenius directions lose independence.

The bad event is:

\[
H_{p,r}=0,
\]

not merely

\[
v_p(T_r)\ge1
\]

for one explicit Gamma product.

---

## (C) Can one prove probability \(O(1/p)\)?

Not from monodromy alone.

The heuristic is strong:

- the Hasse scalar is a nonzero algebraic function of the tame parameter;
- an \(SL_2\) or \(Sp_4\) Frobenius orbit should equidistribute in a positive-dimensional group;
- hitting a codimension-one vanishing locus should have probability about \(1/p\).

Thus the expected bound is

\[
\Pr(H_{p,r}=0)\asymp p^{-1},
\]

which gives

\[
|Z_p|=O(1)
\]

on average.

But proving this requires a horizontal equidistribution theorem for the family of Kummer twists. Ordinary Deligne equidistribution at a fixed twist does not suffice.

The missing statement is precisely HTO.

---

## (D) What protects Apéry?

### 1. Large monodromy

This is the strongest candidate.

For Jacobi, the Frobenius parameter lives in essentially a one-dimensional torus. The zero condition can persist along an interval.

For Apéry, the relevant Frobenius data lies in a larger group:

\[
SL_2 \quad\text{or}\quad Sp_4.
\]

A vanishing Hasse scalar imposes a rank-drop condition, not a slope inequality. Rank drops are typically codimension one.

This is the geometric reason one expects \(O(1/p)\).

### 2. Hodge polygon

The slopes

\[
(0,1,2) 
\]

are important because there is a unique first-break direction. The Hasse invariant detects failure of the unit root line rather than arbitrary cancellation of a scalar sum.

### 3. Reflection symmetry

The symmetry

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p
\]

forces zeros to occur in pairs. It explains the observed evenness of \(|Z_p|\), but it does not by itself bound the number of pairs.

### 4. The \(\zeta(3)\) origin

The arithmetic origin explains why the motive is rigid, but irrationality of \(\zeta(3)\) does not directly imply AFP.

---

## (E) Can rank >= 2 still have many Mellin zeros?

Yes. Rank alone is not a theorem.

One can construct higher-rank sheaves with large Mellin zero sets by taking direct sums or induced objects with hidden toric factors. For example, if a rank-two representation contains a Kummer character as a summand, the Jacobi mechanism survives inside the trace.

Therefore the obstruction is not:

\[
\text{rank}\ge2.
\]

The obstruction is:

\[
\text{large monodromy + irreducibility + no toric quotient}.
\]

For Apéry, the desired theorem should show that the Kummer-twisted crystal family has no persistent toric component.

---

## Precise missing theorem

A useful formulation is:

**Horizontal Tame Ordinarity (HTO).** Let \(H_{p,r}\) be the first-break Hasse scalar of the Gauss-normalized Apéry Kummer twist. Then

\[
\sum_{p\le X}\sum_{r\bmod p}1_{H_{p,r}=0}\ll \pi(X).
\]

A proof would likely require:

1. a geometric family over tame weight space;
2. irreducibility of the associated unit-root F-crystal;
3. horizontal monodromy in the \(r\)-direction;
4. a large-sieve/equidistribution theorem for the Kummer-twist family.

The Jacobi example shows why steps (2)--(4) cannot be replaced by bounded conductor arguments.

The correct Apéry-specific principle is therefore not "higher rank prevents vanishing". It is:

> Apéry has a genuinely multidimensional Frobenius orbit, so zero of the first-break Hasse scalar should be a rare geometric incidence rather than a one-dimensional Stickelberger interval phenomenon.
