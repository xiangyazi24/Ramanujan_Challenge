# Q389 — Structural repulsion in CRT pair correlations

## Executive verdict

The negative shared-one-prime term is real and mathematically informative, but the proposed explanation must be separated into an **exact algebraic identity** and an **unproved arithmetic discrepancy statement**.

1. There is an exact graph identity. If

   \[
   Y_p:=\sum_{q\ne p}\varepsilon_{pq},
   \qquad
   A:=\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}^2,
   \]

   then the contribution of pairs of distinct edges sharing one prime to

   \[
   \left(\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}\right)^2
   \]

   is exactly

   \[
   \boxed{
   C_{\rm sh}=\sum_pY_p^2-2A.
   }
   \]

   Thus the shared term is negative exactly when the row-sum energy
   \(\sum_pY_p^2\) is smaller than \(2A\). This proves that the observed
   “repulsion” is equivalent to row balancing. It does **not** prove the row
   balancing itself.

2. The numerical table already determines the row \(L^2\) energy. Under the
   convention in which the displayed shared term is its full contribution to
   the square,

   \[
   \frac1N\sum_pY_p^2
   =2(0.169)-0.023
   =0.315.
   \]

   At \(N=5000\), there are
   \(\pi(5000)-\pi(\sqrt{5000})=650\) primes in the window, so the row RMS is
   about

   \[
   \sqrt{0.315\cdot5000/650}=1.56.
   \]

   This is strong evidence for an aggregate bound

   \[
   \sum_pY_p^2=O(N),
   \]

   but it is not evidence yet for the much stronger uniform bound
   \(|Y_p|=O(1)\).

3. A uniform constant bound on every row is **not a consequence of CRT**.
   The common-anchor model

   \[
   Z_p=\{a\bmod p\}
   \]

   has perfect one-prime Fourier behavior, yet every pair hits the same
   integer \(a\le N\); then \(Y_p\) has size comparable with the number of
   primes. Hence CRT permits maximal positive correlation as well as the
   observed negative correlation.

4. The fixed-\(p\) hit condition is not a prime-number-theorem-in-progressions
   condition. For singleton roots it is

   \[
   a_q\equiv a_p+jp\pmod q,
   \qquad 0\le j\le (N-a_p)/p,
   \]

   where the marked root \(a_q\) itself changes with \(q\). This is not
   \(q\equiv c_p\pmod p\). It is equivalently a large-prime-divisor/zero-residue
   count for the Apéry values with indices

   \[
   a_p,\ a_p+p,\ a_p+2p,\ldots\le N.
   \]

   That is essentially the original cross-prime atom problem, reorganized by
   rows.

5. Siegel–Walfisz does not apply for three independent reasons: there is no
   fixed residue class, the modulus \(p\) lies in \((\sqrt N,N]\) rather than
   the polylogarithmic range of uniformity, and its classical error term is
   not an absolute \(O(1)\) error anyway. Its main term would also be
   \(\asymp N/(p\log N)\), whereas the CRT row main term is
   \(\asymp(N/p)\log2\); the scales do not match.

6. The random-root heuristic does not predict \(Y_p=O(1)\) uniformly. It
   predicts

   \[
   \operatorname{Var}(Y_p)\asymp \frac Np,
   \qquad
   |Y_p|_{\rm typical}\asymp\sqrt{\frac Np}.
   \]

   In particular, for \(p\asymp\sqrt N\), the natural fluctuation is
   \(N^{1/4}\). Aggregating over \(p\) predicts

   \[
   \sum_pY_p^2\asymp N,
   \]

   exactly the scale suggested by the experiment.

7. A row-\(L^2\) theorem is already sufficient for the signed error. Since

   \[
   \sum_pY_p=2\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq},
   \]

   Cauchy–Schwarz gives

   \[
   \left|\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}\right|
   \le
   \frac12\sqrt{\#\mathcal P_N}
   \left(\sum_pY_p^2\right)^{1/2}.
   \]

   Hence

   \[
   \boxed{
   \sum_pY_p^2=O(N)
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   \sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}
   =O\!\left(\frac N{\sqrt{\log N}}\right)=o(N).
   }
   \]

   This is both weaker than a uniform constant bound and closer to the
   observed/random scale.

8. Conditional on a genuine bound \(|Y_p|\le C\), the final signed-sum
   argument is correct, with a factor \(1/2\):

   \[
   \left|\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}\right|
   \le \frac C2\#\mathcal P_N
   =O(N/\log N)=o(N).
   \]

   However, \(|Y_p|\le C\) by itself does **not** imply
   \(\operatorname{Var}(\sum_pY_p)\le C^2\#\mathcal P_N\); that variance
   conclusion would need orthogonality or negative dependence between the
   different rows.

Therefore the correct research target suggested by the discovery is not a
Siegel–Walfisz theorem and not necessarily a bounded-row theorem. It is the
Apéry-specific row-dispersion estimate

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}
\left|
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal P_N\\q\ne p}}
\varepsilon_{pq}
\right|^2
\ll N,
}
\tag{RLD}
\]

first for the suffix/suffix channel, and then with the other three Lucas
channels included.

## 1. Exact notation and scope

Let

\[
\mathcal P_N:=\{p\text{ prime}:\sqrt N<p\le N\}.
\]

For the suffix/suffix channel define

\[
I_p(n):=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p},
\qquad
R_{pq}:=\sum_{1\le n\le N}I_p(n)I_q(n),
\]

and use the normalization in the question,

\[
\varepsilon_{pq}
:=R_{pq}-\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq},
\qquad z_p:=|Z_p|.
\tag{1.1}
\]

The exact endpoint convention—\([0,N]\) versus \([1,N]\), and whether the
main term uses \(N\) or \(N+1\)—changes only the explicit boundary terms. It
does not change any structural point below. In a formal proof one should retain
the project’s exact convention rather than silently absorb those boundary
terms.

Because \(p,q>\sqrt N\),

\[
pq>N.
\tag{1.2}
\]

Consequently each residue class modulo \(pq\) has at most one representative
in the interval. If \([r,s]_{p,q}\in[0,pq)\) denotes the CRT representative of
\(r\bmod p\) and \(s\bmod q\), then

\[
R_{pq}
=\sum_{r\in Z_p}\sum_{s\in Z_q}
1_{1\le[r,s]_{p,q}\le N}.
\tag{1.3}
\]

This is the precise suffix/suffix object analyzed in
`drops/Q372-9aa1fae5.md`. The full divisibility event is larger: for
\(p>\sqrt N\), writing

\[
n=\lfloor n/p\rfloor p+(n\bmod p),
\]

the Apéry Lucas congruence gives

\[
p\mid b_n
\iff
n\bmod p\in Z_p
\quad\text{or}\quad
\lfloor n/p\rfloor\in Z_p.
\tag{1.4}
\]

Thus the conclusions below initially concern the suffix/suffix channel. A
full P3.2 closure must also handle suffix/leading, leading/suffix, and
leading/leading, as recorded in `drops/Q359-4a66b8d3.md` and
`drops/Q375-563302f3.md`.

## 2. The exact row identity behind the negative shared term

View \(\mathcal P_N\) as the vertex set of a complete graph and put the weight
\(\varepsilon_{pq}\) on the edge \(\{p,q\}\). Define

\[
S:=\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq},
\qquad
A:=\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}^2,
\tag{2.1}
\]

and the full incident row sum

\[
Y_p:=\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal P_N\\q\ne p}}
\varepsilon_{\min(p,q),\max(p,q)}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Let

\[
U_{m sh}
:=
\sum_p
\sum_{\substack{q<r\\q,r\ne p}}
\varepsilon_{pq}\varepsilon_{pr},
\tag{2.3}
\]

where the edge notation in the summand is understood symmetrically. Each
unordered pair of distinct edges sharing one vertex occurs exactly once in
\(U_{\rm sh}\). Expanding a row square gives

\[
Y_p^2
=\sum_{q\ne p}\varepsilon_{pq}^2
+2\sum_{\substack{q<r\\q,r\ne p}}
\varepsilon_{pq}\varepsilon_{pr}.
\]

Summing over \(p\), each edge square occurs in two rows. Therefore

\[
\sum_pY_p^2
=2A+2U_{\rm sh},
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
U_{\rm sh}=\frac12\sum_pY_p^2-A.
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

In the full square

\[
S^2=A+2U_{\rm sh}+2U_{\rm dis},
\tag{2.5}
\]

where \(U_{\rm dis}\) is the sum over unordered pairs of disjoint edges. Thus
the shared-one-prime **contribution to the square** is

\[
\boxed{
C_{\rm sh}:=2U_{\rm sh}
=\sum_pY_p^2-2A.
}
\tag{2.6}
\]

This is the rigorous version of the proposed “balance creates negative
covariance” explanation.

### What the sign means

Equation (2.6) gives the equivalence

\[
C_{\rm sh}<0
\iff
\sum_pY_p^2<2\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}^2.
\tag{2.7}
\]

Thus negative shared covariance is not an additional mysterious statistic.
It says exactly that the incident edge errors cancel substantially inside the
rows.

But there is no implication

\[
C_{\rm sh}<0
\Longrightarrow
|Y_p|=O(1)\quad\text{for every }p.
\]

The sign controls only an aggregate comparison. A small collection of
exceptional rows can still be large.

### Reading the \(N=5000\) table

The displayed entries add as contributions to \(S^2/N\), up to rounding:

\[
0.169-0.023-0.007=0.139\approx0.138.
\]

Therefore (2.6) gives

\[
\frac1N\sum_pY_p^2
=2(0.169)-0.023
=0.315.
\tag{2.8}
\]

There are \(650\) primes in \((\sqrt{5000},5000]\), so the mean row square is
about

\[
0.315\cdot5000/650=2.42,
\]

and the row RMS is about \(1.56\).

This is a useful new diagnostic. Future computations should report
\(\sum_pY_p^2/N\) directly; it is the quantity that tests the proposed
repulsion theorem most cleanly.

If a code path defines the “shared” number without the factor \(2\) occurring
in (2.5), then one should instead use (2.4). The identity itself is exact and
also provides a simple convention check.

## 3. Negative shared covariance is not forced by CRT

There is no universal CRT theorem making (2.6) negative.

Fix one integer \(a\in[1,N]\) and, abstractly, take

\[
Z_p=\{a\bmod p\}
\qquad(p\in\mathcal P_N).
\tag{3.1}
\]

Since \(pq>N\ge a\), the CRT representative of the two marked residues is
exactly \(a\). Hence

\[
R_{pq}=1
\]

for every pair. In the top dyadic region \(p,q\asymp N\),

\[
\varepsilon_{pq}
=1-\frac{N}{pq}
=1+O(1/N).
\tag{3.2}
\]

Thus every incident edge in every row has the same positive sign,

\[
Y_p\asymp\#\mathcal P_N,
\]

and the shared-edge contribution is strongly positive.

This example also has the strongest possible one-prime Fourier properties:

\[
S_p(u)=e_p(au),
\qquad |S_p(u)|=1.
\]

Each \(S_p\) is one rank-one bounded-conductor Artin–Schreier trace function.
Therefore neither CRT, bounded conductor, pointwise square-root cancellation,
Parseval, nor fourth moments can force row repulsion. What excludes (3.1), if
anything, must be an Apéry-specific theorem about the variation of the marked
roots across characteristics.

The opposite extreme is also possible abstractly: choose the marked residues
so that almost none of their CRT representatives enters \([1,N]\). Then most
errors in a row are negative. CRT itself accommodates both behaviors.

Hence the observation at \(N=5000\) is evidence for genuine Apéry arithmetic,
not a formal consequence of the Chinese remainder theorem.

## 4. The exact fixed-\(p\) row count

Assume first that

\[
Z_p=\{a_p\},
\qquad
Z_q=\{a_q\}.
\]

Every integer in \([1,N]\) congruent to \(a_p\pmod p\) has the form

\[
n=a_p+jp,
\qquad
j\in J_p:=
\{j\ge0:1\le a_p+jp\le N\}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

The pair \((p,q)\) hits if and only if there exists \(j\in J_p\) such that

\[
a_p+jp\equiv a_q\pmod q.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
j\equiv(a_q-a_p)p^{-1}\pmod q.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Because

\[
|J_p|\le N/p+1<\sqrt N+1<q+1,
\]

there is at most one such \(j\) for a fixed \(q\). Thus the exact row hit count
is

\[
\boxed{
H_p
:=\sum_{q\ne p}R_{pq}
=
\sum_{j\in J_p}
\#\left\{
q\in\mathcal P_N\setminus\{p\}:
(a_p+jp)\bmod q=a_q
\right\}.
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

The corresponding row error is

\[
\boxed{
Y_p
=H_p-rac Np
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal P_N\\q\ne p}}
\frac1q.
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

For general zero sets, the exact analogue is

\[
\boxed{
H_p
=
\sum_{r\in Z_p}
\sum_{\substack{j\ge0\\1\le r+jp\le N}}
\#\left\{
q\in\mathcal P_N\setminus\{p\}:
(r+jp)\bmod q\in Z_q
\right\},
}
\tag{4.6}
\]

with multiplicity exactly matching \(R_{pq}\). Its centered main term is

\[
M_p
=rac{Nz_p}{p}
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal P_N\\q\ne p}}
\frac{z_q}{q}.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Equations (4.4)–(4.7), not a prime-progressions formula, are the correct
formalization of Question 2.

### Relation to prime factors of Apéry values

If

\[
(r+jp)\bmod q\in Z_q,
\]

then the Apéry Lucas congruence implies

\[
q\mid b_{r+jp}.
\tag{4.8}
\]

Consequently

\[
H_p
\le
\sum_{r\in Z_p}
\sum_{\substack{j\ge0\\1\le r+jp\le N}}
\#\{q\in\mathcal P_N:q\mid b_{r+jp}\}.
\tag{4.9}
\]

For the suffix-only top-half channel, the implication is an equivalence: if
\(q>(r+jp)/2\), the leading base-\(q\) digit is \(0\) or \(1\), and for
\(q\ge7\) neither is an Apéry zero because \(b_0=1\) and \(b_1=5\).

Thus a uniform estimate

\[
H_p=M_p+O(1)
\tag{4.10}
\]

would be a very strong theorem about the distribution of large prime divisors
of the finite collection

\[
\{b_{r+jp}:r\in Z_p,\ 1\le r+jp\le N\}.
\]

It is not supplied by CRT. In the current repository architecture it is a
repackaging of the cross-prime atom problem rather than an independent
classical theorem.

### A second exact row interpretation

Let

\[
B^{(0)}_N(n):=\sum_{q\in\mathcal P_N}I_q(n)
\]

be the suffix-only prime count. Then

\[
\sum_{q\ne p}R_{pq}
=\sum_{n\le N}I_p(n)(B^{(0)}_N(n)-1).
\tag{4.11}
\]

So \(Y_p\) is the centered co-degree of the \(p\)-column in the incidence
matrix \((I_p(n))\). Bounding every \(Y_p\) is a conditional uniformity
statement for the same incidence matrix whose total energy P3.2 seeks to
control. This makes clear why the row reformulation is valuable but not
automatic.

## 5. The expected fixed-row main term

In the singleton model,

\[
M_p
:=\frac Np
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal P_N\\q\ne p}}
\frac1q.
\]

Mertens’ theorem for primes gives

\[
\sum_{\sqrt N<q\le N}\frac1q
=\log\log N-\log\log\sqrt N+o(1)
=\log2+o(1).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
M_p=\frac Np(\log2+o(1)).
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

With general sparse zero sets, replace the prime harmonic sum by

\[
\sum_{\sqrt N<q\le N}\frac{z_q}{q}.
\]

The large scan recorded in `drops/Q359-4a66b8d3.md` has unweighted mean
\(z_q\) close to \(1\), but a theorem should use the weighted sum explicitly
rather than infer it from the unweighted mean.

Two scales are important:

- If \(p\asymp N\), then \(M_p\asymp1\).
- If \(p\asymp\sqrt N\), then \(M_p\asymp\sqrt N\).

Thus the **count** \(H_p\) certainly should not be expected to be bounded for
all \(p\). The proposed statement concerns the discrepancy \(H_p-M_p\).
Even that discrepancy being \(O(1)\) uniformly would be much stronger than a
standard random model predicts.

## 6. Why this is not a PNT in arithmetic progressions

Question 3 proposes a count of the form

\[
\#\{q\in(\sqrt N,N]:q\equiv c_p\pmod p\}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

The CRT hit condition is not (6.1). It is

\[
a_q\equiv a_p+jp\pmod q
\]

for some short \(j\), where \(a_q\) is a marked Apéry zero depending on the
same prime \(q\) being counted. The varying inverse

\[
p^{-1}\pmod q
\]

and the varying root \(a_q\) cannot be replaced by one fixed residue
\(c_p\pmod p\).

The common-anchor counterexample makes the distinction transparent. If
\(a_q=a\bmod q\) for every \(q\), then **every** prime \(q\) hits the common
integer \(a\), even though those primes are spread among all residue classes
modulo \(p\). Therefore hit membership cannot in general be equivalent to one
prime progression modulo \(p\).

### The main terms do not match

Even an artificial prime progression modulo \(p\) would have expected size

\[
\frac{\operatorname{Li}(N)-\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt N)}{\varphi(p)}
\asymp \frac{N}{p\log N}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The CRT row main term is instead

\[
\frac Np\sum_{\sqrt N<q\le N}\frac1q
\sim\frac Np\log2.
\tag{6.3}
\]

These differ by a factor of order \(\log N\). This scale mismatch alone rules
out the proposed AP interpretation.

### Siegel–Walfisz is outside its range

A standard Siegel–Walfisz statement is uniform for moduli

\[
k\le(\log N)^A
\]

for any fixed \(A\). Here the proposed modulus is

\[
p>\sqrt N.
\]

Thus the theorem is not uniform in the required range. Treating \(p\) as
fixed does not help, because the desired result must hold simultaneously for
a growing family of \(p\)’s.

Moreover, the classical error

\[
O_A\!\left(Ne^{-c_A\sqrt{\log N}}\right)
\]

is a strong relative error but is not an absolute \(O(1)\) discrepancy; it
still tends to infinity. So even in its legitimate range Siegel–Walfisz would
not yield (4.10).

Bombieri–Vinogradov likewise supplies an average over ordinary fixed residue
classes and moduli only up to approximately the square-root level, after
logarithmic losses. It neither reaches the whole interval
\((\sqrt N,N]\), nor gives a uniform \(O(1)\) error, nor handles the
prime-dependent predicate \(a_q\in Z_q\).

The correct analytic analogy is closer to a bilinear/dispersion problem with
marked points in varying finite fields, or to the distribution of large prime
divisors of Apéry values along the short progression (4.1), not to the PNT in
one arithmetic progression.

## 7. What scale should one expect for \(Y_p\)?

Suppose heuristically that, for fixed \(p\), the different \(q\)-hits behave
like rare Bernoulli events with probabilities

\[
\mu_{pq}\approx\frac{N}{pq}
\]

in the singleton case. Since \(\mu_{pq}<1\), the row variance is expected to
be

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(Y_p)
&\approx
\sum_{q\ne p}\mu_{pq}(1-\mu_{pq})\\
&\asymp
\frac Np\sum_{\sqrt N<q\le N}\frac1q\\
&\asymp\frac Np.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Thus the natural pointwise fluctuation scale is

\[
\boxed{
|Y_p|_{\rm rms}\asymp\sqrt{N/p}.
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

For \(p\asymp N\), this is constant. For
\(p\asymp\sqrt N\), it is \(N^{1/4}\). Hence

\[
\sup_p|Y_p|=O(1)
\]

would exhibit rigidity substantially stronger than random behavior.

Aggregating (7.1) over \(p\),

\[
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}\frac Np
=N(\log2+o(1)),
\]

up to the extra factor \(\log2\) from the inner \(q\)-sum. More precisely the
singleton independent model predicts

\[
\sum_p\mathbb E Y_p^2
\sim N(\log2)^2.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_pY_p^2=O(N)
}
\]

is the natural random scale. The measured coefficient \(0.315\) at
\(N=5000\) is entirely compatible with this scale and is somewhat smaller
than the naive independent constant \((\log2)^2\approx0.480\), consistent
with additional repulsion.

### Fixed-row balancing heuristic

There is a standard finite-population mechanism for negative covariance. If a
row has \(m\) entries and, conditional on having exactly \(h\) hits, the hit
positions are uniformly distributed, then for two distinct positions

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)
=-\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{m-1},
\qquad \theta=h/m.
\tag{7.4}
\]

This precisely formalizes the intuition “one hit slightly reduces the room
for another hit.” But the Apéry row is not known to be a uniformly chosen
fixed-size subset. Establishing the required near-fixed row count or
exchangeability is the arithmetic theorem, not a consequence of (7.4).

## 8. Sufficient row theorems, from strongest to weakest

Let

\[
P_N:=\#\mathcal P_N\asymp\frac N{\log N}.
\]

Because every edge is incident to two vertices,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_pY_p=2S,
\qquad
S:=\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}.
}
\tag{8.1}
\]

This gives several sufficient targets.

### 8.1 Uniform bounded discrepancy

If

\[
|Y_p|\le C
\qquad\text{for every }p,
\tag{8.2}
\]

then

\[
|S|\le\frac12\sum_p|Y_p|
\le\frac C2P_N
=O(N/\log N)=o(N).
\tag{8.3}
\]

So the final signed-error implication proposed in Question 4 is correct,
subject to the factor \(1/2\) and subject to using the full symmetric rows.
If one defines a one-sided row using only \(q>p\), then the factor disappears,
but that one-sided row no longer governs the shared-edge identity (2.6) as
cleanly.

### 8.2 Natural pointwise square-root discrepancy

The weaker and more plausible estimate

\[
|Y_p|\ll\sqrt{N/p}
\tag{8.4}
\]

also gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
|S|
&\le\frac12\sum_p|Y_p|\\
&\ll
\sqrt N\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac1{\sqrt p}\\
&\ll\frac N{\log N}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.5}
\]

Here partial summation gives

\[
\sum_{p\le N}p^{-1/2}\sim\frac{2\sqrt N}{\log N}.
\]

Thus one does not need bounded rows; random-scale square-root control already
closes the signed sum at the desired strength.

### 8.3 Aggregate row \(L^2\)

By Cauchy–Schwarz and (8.1),

\[
|S|
\le
\frac12\sqrt{P_N}
\left(\sum_pY_p^2\right)^{1/2}.
\tag{8.6}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_pY_p^2\ll N
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S\ll\frac N{\sqrt{\log N}}=o(N).
}
\tag{8.7}
\]

This is the cleanest target matching both the numerical decomposition and the
random heuristic.

A stronger estimate

\[
\sum_pY_p^2\ll N/\log N
\]

would give \(S=O(N/\log N)\), but that strength is not needed merely to prove
\(S=o(N)\).

### 8.4 Repulsion plus self-energy

The exact identity (2.6) yields another concise sufficient package. If one can
prove

\[
A=\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}^2=O(N)
\tag{8.8}
\]

and

\[
C_{\rm sh}\le0,
\tag{8.9}
\]

then

\[
\sum_pY_p^2=2A+C_{\rm sh}\le2A=O(N),
\]

and (8.7) follows.

More generally it is enough to prove

\[
A=O(N),
\qquad
C_{\rm sh}=O(N).
\]

The negative sign is conceptually attractive but not logically necessary once
both terms are controlled at scale \(N\).

There is a caution: in the singleton regime, writing
\(\mu_{pq}=N/(pq)\) and \(R_{pq}\in\{0,1\}\),

\[
(R_{pq}-\mu_{pq})^2\le R_{pq}+\mu_{pq}^2.
\tag{8.10}
\]

Also

\[
\sum_{p<q}\mu_{pq}^2\ll N/\log^2N.
\]

Hence an \(O(N)\) self-energy theorem is already very close to an
\(O(N)\) bound for the total number of CRT hits. Structural repulsion explains
why the **signed total** is smaller, but it does not make the underlying
self/incidence estimate free.

## 9. The variance statement in Question 1 needs correction

At a fixed value of \(N\), the quantities \(\varepsilon_{pq}\), \(Y_p\), and
\(S\) are deterministic. The table is therefore an algebraic decomposition
of \(S^2\), not literally a variance decomposition unless a probability space
has separately been specified.

Even after introducing randomness, the bound

\[
|Y_p|\le C
\]

alone does not imply

\[
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_pY_p\right)
\le C^2P_N.
\]

In general,

\[
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_pY_p\right)
=\sum_p\operatorname{Var}(Y_p)
+2\sum_{p<r}\operatorname{Cov}(Y_p,Y_r),
\]

and the covariance sum can be positive. Bounded variables give only the crude
worst-case scale \(O(C^2P_N^2)\).

What the bounded-row hypothesis gives unconditionally is the deterministic
signed estimate (8.3). That is sufficient for the P3.2 second-moment
reduction if all other terms have already been bounded, but it is not a
standalone variance theorem.

Likewise, (RLD) gives the deterministic estimate (8.7); it does not assert
that the rows are independent.

## 10. Does the conditional argument close P3.2?

Suppose the project has an exact decomposition of the form

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
=	ext{main term}+2\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}
+\text{other-channel errors},
\tag{10.1}
\]

with

\[
\text{main term}=O(N)
\]

and the other-channel errors already proved to be \(O(N)\) or negligible.
Then either

\[
\sup_p|Y_p|=O(1)
\]

or the weaker row-\(L^2\) estimate (RLD) gives

\[
\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}=o(N),
\]

and therefore

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N).
\]

The usual nonnegative-energy argument then yields

\[
\max_{n\le N}B_N(n)
\le
\left(\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2\right)^{1/2}
=O(\sqrt N)
=o(N/\log N).
\]

So the **conditional reduction is valid**.

Three qualifications are essential.

1. The rows must correspond to the same \(\varepsilon_{pq}\) appearing in the
   exact second-moment decomposition.
2. A suffix/suffix row theorem does not automatically control the three
   leading-digit channels.
3. The argument controls the signed sum
   \(\sum\varepsilon_{pq}\), not
   \(\sum|\varepsilon_{pq}|\). It therefore proves the needed energy estimate
   only when the decomposition genuinely uses the signed sum.

## 11. The most focused next theorem

The discovery suggests promoting the following statement as the immediate
analytic target.

### Row-dispersion theorem, suffix/suffix form

Let

\[
Y_p^{00}
:=
\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal P_N\\q\ne p}}
\left(
R_{pq}^{00}-\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}
\right),
\]

with exact endpoint corrections if required. Prove

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}|Y_p^{00}|^2\ll N.
}
\tag{11.1}
\]

This theorem:

- exactly measures the row balancing detected by the negative shared term;
- is consistent with the independent-root heuristic;
- is weaker than \(Y_p=O(1)\) uniformly;
- is sufficient to give a signed suffix/suffix error \(o(N)\);
- cannot follow from CRT alone, as the common-anchor example shows.

### Equivalent shared-covariance form

Using (2.6), (11.1) is equivalent to

\[
2\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}^2+C_{\rm sh}=O(N).
\tag{11.2}
\]

Thus one may attack the theorem either directly through row sums or through a
quantitative shared-edge repulsion estimate.

### A pointwise alternative

A scale-sensitive theorem

\[
\boxed{
|Y_p^{00}|\ll\sqrt{N/p}
}
\tag{11.3}
\]

would be stronger than (11.1) in useful ways and still match the natural
row fluctuation. It is a more plausible pointwise conjecture than
\(|Y_p|=O(1)\).

### Full Lucas version

Ultimately define \(Y_p\) from the exact full events

\[
E_p
=E_p^{(0)}\cup E_p^{(1)}
\]

rather than from \(E_p^{(0)}\) alone, and prove

\[
\sum_p
\left|
\sum_{q\ne p}
\left(
|E_p\cap E_q|-\text{correct main}_{pq}
\right)
\right|^2
\ll N.
\tag{11.4}
\]

The inclusion–exclusion terms must be kept explicit. A negative covariance in
only the suffix/suffix submatrix does not preclude a leading-digit anchored
star.

## 12. Recommended computational audit

The next scan should record row data directly, not only the global edge-pair
categories. For each \(p\in\mathcal P_N\), compute

```text
Y_p       = sum_{q != p} epsilon_{p,q}
A_p       = sum_{q != p} epsilon_{p,q}^2
C_p       = 2 * sum_{q<r; q,r != p} epsilon_{p,q} epsilon_{p,r}
check_p   = C_p - (Y_p^2 - A_p)
```

Here \(C_p\) is the doubled cross-term inside the single row. The exact check
is

\[
C_p=Y_p^2-A_p.
\]

Globally, because each edge square appears in two rows,

```text
sum_p C_p = sum_p Y_p^2 - 2 * sum_{p<q} epsilon_{p,q}^2.
```

For increasing \(N\), report:

```text
sum_p Y_p^2 / N
max_p |Y_p|
max_p |Y_p| / sqrt(N/p)
quantiles of |Y_p| / sqrt(N/p)
sum_p |Y_p|
row statistics in dyadic p-blocks
```

The dyadic split is important. Most primes lie near the top of the interval,
where the natural row fluctuation is constant, while the relatively few
primes near \(\sqrt N\) have expected fluctuation \(N^{1/4}\). A global RMS
alone can hide growth in the low-\(p\) rows.

Also compute the four Lucas channels separately and together. This will show
whether the observed repulsion survives the union/inclusion–exclusion needed
for the actual divisibility event.

## 13. Direct answers to the four questions

### 1. Can CRT prove negative covariance or \(|Y_p|\le C\)?

- The negative shared term has the exact identity

  \[
  C_{\rm sh}=\sum_pY_p^2-2\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}^2.
  \]

  Thus its interpretation as row balancing is rigorous.
- CRT does not force the sign and does not imply \(|Y_p|=O(1)\). The common
  anchor \(Z_p=\{a\bmod p\}\) is a decisive counterexample.
- For the actual Apéry roots, a bounded-row theorem would be a new
  Apéry-specific result. The current data more naturally supports
  \(\sum_pY_p^2=O(N)\).

### 2. How many \(q\) satisfy \(\operatorname{CRT}(a_p,a_q)\le N\)?

Exactly

\[
H_p
=
\sum_{j\in J_p}
\#\{q:(a_p+jp)\bmod q=a_q\}.
\]

Its heuristic main term is

\[
\frac Np\sum_q\frac1q
=\frac Np(\log2+o(1))
\]

in the singleton model. No existing CRT identity bounds the discrepancy by
\(O(1)\). Proving such a bound would amount to strong control of the large
prime divisors/Apéry zero residues of the values \(b_{a_p+jp}\).

### 3. Is this Siegel–Walfisz/PNT in arithmetic progressions?

No.

- The condition contains the varying root \(a_q\) and inverse \(p^{-1}\bmod q\);
  it is not a fixed class \(q\equiv c_p\bmod p\).
- The proposed modulus \(p>\sqrt N\) is far outside the uniform
  Siegel–Walfisz range.
- Siegel–Walfisz does not give an absolute \(O(1)\) error.
- Its AP main term has the wrong scale by a factor of \(\log N\).

### 4. Does a bounded-row theorem give the desired signed sum?

Yes, conditionally and with the double-counting factor:

\[
2\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}=\sum_pY_p.
\]

Thus \(|Y_p|\le C\) gives

\[
\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}=O(N/\log N)=o(N).
\]

But the claimed variance bound does not follow from boundedness alone, and a
suffix-only theorem is not yet the full four-channel P3.2 theorem.

The weaker, more natural statement

\[
\sum_pY_p^2=O(N)
\]

already gives

\[
\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}
=O(N/\sqrt{\log N})=o(N),
\]

and is the recommended formalization of the observed structural repulsion.

## Final conclusion

The experiment has uncovered a useful new organization of the error matrix:
**the rows appear to have random-scale \(L^2\) discrepancy, and this forces a
negative shared-edge correction through an exact identity.** That is a real
structural signal.

What it has not uncovered is a classical PNT-in-AP shortcut. The fixed-row
hit count is a marked-root/large-prime-divisor statistic for Apéry values in a
short progression, and CRT alone allows the opposite common-anchor behavior.

The most credible next statement is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\in(\sqrt N,N]}
\left(
\sum_{\substack{q\in(\sqrt N,N]\\q\ne p}}
\varepsilon_{pq}
\right)^2
\ll N,
}
\]

with exact boundary terms and, ultimately, all four Lucas channels. Proving
this would convert the observed repulsion into

\[
\sum_{p<q}\varepsilon_{pq}=o(N),
\]

which is the signed-error estimate required by the current second-moment
reduction.
