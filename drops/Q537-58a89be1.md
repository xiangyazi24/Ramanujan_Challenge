# USp(4), palindromic pairing, and the Poisson(1/2) law for Apéry zeros

## Summary

The short answer is:

1. **USp(4) Sato–Tate alone does not predict the observed Poisson(1/2) parameter.** It predicts a local trace-zero density involving
\[
\rho_{\mathrm{USp}(4)}(0)=\frac{8}{3\pi^2}\approx 0.270.
\]
After accounting for the palindromic involution, the naive prediction remains around 0.27, not 0.5.

2. The empirical law
\[
|Z_p|/2\sim \mathrm{Poisson}(1/2)
\]
is therefore an **arithmetic phenomenon of the Apéry family**, not a direct consequence of compact-group Haar measure.

3. The missing factor must come from the fact that the condition
\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p
\]
is not exactly the normalized Frobenius trace-zero condition. It is a special period-coordinate vanishing condition, and its local density is larger than the generic USp(4) trace density.

---

# (1) Does USp(4) + palindromic pairing imply Poisson(1/2)?

The palindromic identity
\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p
\]
creates the involution
\[
r\leftrightarrow p-1-r.
\]
Therefore zeros occur in pairs except possibly a fixed point (for odd p there is no fixed point in the range $0\le r<p$). Hence
\[
|Z_p|=2N_p,
\]
where $N_p$ is the number of independent zero pairs.

If independent trials were genuinely random, one would expect
\[
N_p\sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda).
\]
The question is what $λ$ should be.

A naive Sato–Tate calculation gives:

- about $p/2$ independent positions;
- probability of hitting zero approximately $2\rho(0)/p$;

so
\[
\lambda=\frac p2\frac{2\rho(0)}p=\rho(0)
=\frac{8}{3\pi^2}\approx0.270.
\]

This gives the wrong mean compared with the experiment:
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{emp}}\approx0.5.
\]

Therefore the Poisson(1/2) law does **not** follow from USp(4) plus the involution alone.

---

# Where does the missing factor come from?

The key issue is that the Apéry coefficient $b_r$ is not the character trace of a random USp(4) matrix.

Sato–Tate controls
\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_p)
\]
or the conjugacy-class distribution of Frobenius. But the congruence
\[
b_r=0
\]
asks for a zero of a specific coordinate of a Frobenius-period vector.

Geometrically:

- trace zero is one hypersurface in the group;
- vanishing of a matrix coefficient/period coordinate is another hypersurface;
- they have different Haar pushforward densities.

Thus the correct heuristic is
\[
\mathbb E(|Z_p|/2)=C_{\mathrm{arith}}\rho_{\mathrm{USp}(4)}(0),
\]
where
\[
C_{\mathrm{arith}}\approx\frac{0.5}{0.270}\approx1.85.
\]

The factor is not mysterious: the Apéry zero condition selects a special arithmetic divisor in the moduli space, not the trace-zero divisor.

---

# (2) Is Poisson(1/2) theoretical or only empirical?

At present it should be regarded as **empirical but strongly motivated**.

The evidence is:

- zeros always occur in pairs;
- the distribution of $|Z_p|/2$ matches a Poisson distribution;
- the mean stabilizes near $1/2$ across many primes;
- higher moments appear compatible with a sparse independent-event model.

The theoretical framework would be:

## Step 1: Construct the Apéry Frobenius family

For each $r$ modulo $p$, the recurrence defines a point in a rank-4 symplectic local system. One wants an equidistribution theorem for
\[
r\mapsto \mathrm{Frob}_{p,r}.
\]

## Step 2: Identify the zero divisor

The condition
\[
b_r=0
\]
should correspond to a subvariety
\[
D\subset \mathrm{Sp}_4
\]
(or its associated period space).

## Step 3: Compute its local density

The expected parameter is
\[
\lambda=\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}(D),
\]
not
\[
\rho_{\mathrm{trace}}(0).
\]

If one could prove
\[
\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}(D)=1/2,
\]
then the Poisson(1/2) law would follow from a suitable independence theorem.

# Relation to P3.2 M1

This observation is important for the M1 attack.

The first moment statement
\[
\mathbb E|Z_p|=O(1)
\]
does not require knowing the exact constant. The USp(4) picture predicts bounded density because a zero condition is codimension one.

However, proving the stronger Poisson law requires:

1. equidistribution of the Apéry Frobenius family;
2. independence between different $r$ values;
3. control of correlations caused by the recurrence.

The empirical Poisson(1/2) law is therefore evidence for a stronger statement than M1:

\[
\boxed{|Z_p|/2\ \text{behaves like independent rare hits of an Apéry-specific divisor of density }1/2.}
\]

The USp(4) Sato–Tate group explains why a bounded Poisson regime should occur, but the numerical parameter $1/2$ comes from the arithmetic geometry of the Apéry period condition, not from the universal USp(4) trace density.
