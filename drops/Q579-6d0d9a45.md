# ZM₂ from the Apéry transfer matrix walk

## Main conclusion

The transfer matrix viewpoint is the correct geometric formulation, but SL₂ monodromy alone is not enough to prove ZM₂. The missing ingredient is not global monodromy over C; it is **finite-field equidistribution of the Frobenius-specialized transfer walk**.

The likely theorem needed is:

> The sequence of projective points generated by the Apéry transfer cocycle has bounded return statistics to a fixed point on P¹(F_p), uniformly on average over p.

This is a cocycle mixing statement, not a direct consequence of Zariski-density.

---

# (A) Does SL₂ monodromy imply random walk behavior?

The implication

$$
\mathrm{Mon}(L)=SL_2 \Rightarrow |Z_p|=O(1)
$$

is too strong.

Complex monodromy controls the algebraic differential equation, but the walk

$$
P_r=M_{r-1}\cdots M_1
$$

is a deterministic ordered product over the arithmetic progression n=1,...,p-2.

A random walk on SL₂(F_p) mixes because the increments are independent enough. Here the increments satisfy algebraic relations:

$$
M_n=\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)/(n+1)^3&-n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore SL₂ monodromy gives only the large algebraic envelope. It does not give a spectral gap for this particular ordering.

The missing statement would be analogous to a Bourgain-Gamburd expansion theorem for the subgroup generated by the matrices, but with a varying deterministic generating set.

---

# (B) Does the rational curve of matrices generate SL₂?

The curve

$$
 n\mapsto M_n
$$

is a rational curve in GL₂. The subgroup generated by all values M_n is expected to be large because the differential equation has SL₂ monodromy.

However there are two separate questions:

1. Algebraic generation:

$$
\langle M_n:n\in F_p\rangle=SL_2(F_p)?
$$

2. Dynamical mixing:

Does the ordered product

$$
M_{p-2}\cdots M_0
$$

behave like a random product?

The first does not imply the second.

A possible route is to study the cocycle as a lisse sheaf on A¹ and prove that the projective orbit has bounded discrepancy. This would be the finite-field analogue of Deligne equidistribution.

---

# (C) Ratio injectivity approach

The reformulation

$$
b_r=0\iff f_r/g_r=-5
$$

is very promising.

The Wronskian identity

$$
W_r=f_rg_{r+1}-f_{r+1}g_r=(-1)^r/(r+1)^3
$$

implies consecutive ratios are distinct whenever defined. Indeed, if

$$
f_r/g_r=f_{r+1}/g_{r+1},
$$

then the Wronskian vanishes, contradiction.

So the ratio map

$$
r\mapsto f_r/g_r
$$

has no adjacent collisions.

But this is much weaker than global injectivity. The palindromic symmetry

$$
R_r=R_{p-1-r}
$$

already forces about a factor-two collision.

The key computational question is therefore exactly the right one:

> Is R_r injective on 1≤r≤(p-3)/2?

If yes, then immediately

$$
|Z_p|\le 2
$$

(up to exceptional points where g_r=0).

This route is stronger than a mixing argument because it uses the one-dimensional geometry of the cocycle.

A possible proof strategy:

Assume

$$
R_r=R_s.
$$

Then the transfer matrix from r to s fixes the projective point corresponding to the initial vector. Therefore collisions correspond to special elements in the cocycle semigroup having prescribed eigenvectors.

One should study the polynomial condition

$$
(P_sP_r^{-1})([v])=[v].
$$

The degree in r,s may be low enough to exclude many collisions.

---

# (D) Sieve/GCD approach

The identity

$$
\sum_p |Z_p|^2
=
\sum_{r,s}\#\{p:p|\gcd(b_r,b_s)\}
$$

is correct, but the naive bound by prime divisors of gcds is not enough.

The required estimate is essentially:

$$
\sum_{r,s\le N}\omega(\gcd(b_r,b_s))\ll N.
$$

This would require very strong control of common prime divisors among Apéry numbers.

Known Apéry congruences suggest that gcds are structured, but not obviously small. In fact, for nearby indices there can be substantial common divisibility due to p-adic recurrences.

The right object is not the integer gcd alone, but the condition

$$
r,s<p.
$$

This is the same triangular-region phenomenon as M1. Integer factorization loses the finite-field geometry.

---

# Recommended attack order

## 1. First prove half-range ratio injectivity

This is the sharpest possible route. It would imply pointwise

$$
|Z_p|=O(1)
$$

and automatically gives ZM₂.

The target theorem:

$$
R_r=R_s,\quad 0\le r,s\le(p-3)/2
\Rightarrow r=s.
$$

## 2. If injectivity fails, classify collisions

Collisions should correspond to stabilizers of the transfer cocycle. A bounded number of collision classes would still prove ZM₂.

## 3. Use sheaf-theoretic equidistribution as the fallback

The strongest general theorem would be a finite-field cocycle expansion statement:

- SL₂ geometric monodromy;
- irreducibility of the projective cocycle;
- bounded conductor;
- Deligne bounds for correlation sums.

This is the correct analogue of a large sieve.

---

# Final assessment

(A) SL₂ monodromy gives the correct algebraic group but not mixing by itself.

(B) The matrix curve likely generates SL₂, but generation is weaker than ordered-product equidistribution.

(C) The ratio map is the most promising concrete route. The observed p/2 distinct values strongly suggest hidden injectivity after quotienting by the involution r↔p−1−r.

(D) The gcd sieve formulation is elegant but probably too lossy unless one proves a new strong theorem on common Apéry divisors.

The breakthrough theorem for ZM₂ is likely not a sieve theorem but:

**Projective cocycle injectivity (or bounded multiplicity) for the Apéry transfer ratio map over F_p.**
