# Erdős–Kac theorems for specific sequences: literature audit and the exact Apéry comparison

## Executive verdict

There are genuine unconditional Erdős–Kac theorems for many nontrivial **siftable families**: shifted primes, Beatty sequences, values of polynomials on suitable varieties, integral points on algebraic varieties, and some automorphic-coefficient constructions. Several of these results prove a full central limit theorem, a multivariate central limit theorem, or even total-variation Poisson approximation; they go substantially beyond a second-moment/Chebyshev estimate.

However, the recurrence itself is not what makes those theorems work. Their common arithmetic input is quantitative distribution modulo squarefree moduli:

\[
\#\{n\le N:d\mid a_n\}
=N g(d)+r_d,
\]

with enough aggregate control of \(r_d\) when \(d\) is a product of all primes occurring in the moment under consideration.

For the named exponential or holonomic recurrences, the literature is much weaker:

- Fibonacci and Mersenne numbers have strong primitive-divisor and almost-all **lower bounds** for \(\omega(a_n)\), but I did not locate an unconditional Gaussian law or a critical-dyadic-shell factorial-moment theorem.
- For \(n!+1\), the strongest nearby results concern large prime factors, not the distribution of \(\omega(n!+1)\).
- I did not locate a published Erdős–Kac theorem for \(\omega(p(n))\), where \(p(n)\) is the partition function.
- Central binomial coefficients and Catalan numbers are not random-looking in the index-tied shell \((n,2n]\): essentially every prime in that shell divides them.
- The direct Apéry literature proves density-one lower bounds for the number and size of prime divisors, but no Erdős–Kac law and no three-prime shell dispersion theorem.

The most important conceptual correction is that the present Apéry problem is not naturally a Gaussian Erdős–Kac problem. Since

\[
\lambda_X=\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac{|Z_p|}{p}
\]

is numerically of order \(1/\log X\), it tends to zero. The relevant analogy is a **Brun–Poisson/factorial-moment theorem in a rare-event shell**. The exact first missing input is the finite pair-Palm positive-excess estimate already isolated in `problems/3.2/hm3_result.tex`; a per-anchor conditional equidistribution theorem is a useful stronger sufficient statement, not the logically weakest one.

## 1. Three normalization corrections

### 1.1 The repository uses ordered factorial moments

Put

\[
M=X^2,
\qquad
K_X(m)=\sum_{X<p\le2X}\mathbf 1_{\{m\bmod p\in Z_p\}},
\qquad
\lambda_X=\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac{|Z_p|}{p}.
\]

The repository's moment is

\[
F_r(X)=\sum_{m<M}(K_X(m))_r,
\]

where

\[
(t)_r=t(t-1)\cdots(t-r+1).
\]

Thus the normalized target is

\[
\frac1M\sum_{m<M}(K_X(m))_r
\ll X^{o(1)}\lambda_X^r.
\tag{1.1}
\]

A statement such as “\(\omega_r(a_n)\le C_r\)” is meaningful here only after averaging and normalizing. For the total number of prime divisors of most classical sequences, the mean itself grows, so an unnormalized moment bounded by a fixed \(C_r\) cannot be expected.

### 1.2 A dyadic shell of random integers does not have mean one

For a uniformly random integer, the expected number of prime divisors in \((X,2X]\) is

\[
\sum_{X<p\le2X}\frac1p
=\log\log(2X)-\log\log X+o(1/\log X)
=\frac{\log 2}{\log X}+O\!\left(\frac1{(\log X)^2}\right).
\tag{1.2}
\]

This tends to zero. In particular, if integers are sampled from \([1,M]\) with \(M=X^2\), then two distinct primes greater than \(X\) cannot both divide the same sampled integer, because their product exceeds \(M\). The shell count is therefore essentially Bernoulli, or equivalently Poisson only in the degenerate small-mean sense.

A nonzero constant Poisson mean arises instead from a **logarithmic-power window**. For example,

\[
\sum_{N^a<p\le N^b}\frac1p\longrightarrow \log(b/a)
\qquad (0<a<b).
\]

This correction matters: the Apéry shell is a rare-event model, not the usual Gaussian regime with mean tending to infinity.

### 1.3 The proved `(HM)_2` is a CRT occupancy bound, not an asymptotic independence theorem

For distinct shell primes \(p,q\), one has \(pq>M\). Each selected residue pair has at most one representative below \(M\), so

\[
|\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q|
\le |Z_p||Z_q|.
\]

Summation gives the repository's bound

\[
F_2(X)\le4M\lambda_X^2.
\]

This is enough for `(HM)_2`, but it does not assert

\[
|\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q|
\sim M\frac{|Z_p|}{p}\frac{|Z_q|}{q}
\]

for individual pairs or even on average. At the pair level, the upper bound has the correct order because \(pq\asymp M\). At the triple level, \(pqr\asymp X^3\), and the same root-tuple bound loses a factor \(X\). That is the first genuine obstruction.

## 2. The general sieve identity behind every relevant Erdős–Kac theorem

Let \((a_n)\) be any integer sequence, let \(\mathcal P\) be a finite set of primes, and define

\[
\omega_{\mathcal P}(a_n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathbf1_{p\mid a_n}.
\]

For squarefree \(d\), write

\[
A_d(N)=\#\{n\le N:d\mid a_n\}.
\]

Then the factorial-moment identity is exact:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}(\omega_{\mathcal P}(a_n))_r
=
\sum_{\substack{(p_1,\ldots,p_r)\in\mathcal P^r\\
                  p_i\text{ pairwise distinct}}}
A_{p_1\cdots p_r}(N).}
\tag{2.1}
\]

Suppose one has a local-density formula

\[
A_d(N)=N g(d)+r_d,
\tag{2.2}
\]

where \(g\) is multiplicative on the relevant squarefree moduli. Inserting (2.2) into (2.1) gives

\[
\sum_{n\le N}(\omega_{\mathcal P}(a_n))_r
=
N\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_r}^{\!*}
   \prod_{i=1}^r g(p_i)
+
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_r}^{\!*}r_{p_1\cdots p_r}.
\tag{2.3}
\]

The first term is the independent Bernoulli factorial moment. The entire arithmetic problem is the aggregate remainder in the second term.

This is the architecture of the Granville–Soundararajan sieve proof of Erdős–Kac. Their abstract setup assumes a multiset \(\mathcal A\) for which

\[
|\mathcal A_d|=\frac{h(d)}d\,|\mathcal A|+r_d
\]

and controls sums of \(|r_d|\) over products of the primes entering the moment. In the classical proof one truncates to sufficiently small primes—roughly so that a product of \(r\) selected primes remains within the available level of distribution. Large prime factors can then be shown to have only a limited effect on the normalized Gaussian statistic.

That escape is unavailable in the present shell. All selected primes are already of size \(X=M^{1/2}\). The second moment sits at the critical product size \(pq\asymp M\), while the third moment immediately asks about moduli

\[
pqr\asymp X^3=M^{3/2}>M.
\]

For Apéry zeros, (2.2) becomes

\[
A_d(M)=\#\{m<M:m\bmod p\in Z_p\text{ for every }p\mid d\},
\qquad
 g(d)=\prod_{p\mid d}\frac{|Z_p|}{p}.
\]

The elementary CRT remainder satisfies only

\[
|r_d|\le\prod_{p\mid d}|Z_p|.
\tag{2.4}
\]

For triples, summing (2.4) is of order \(e_3((|Z_p|)_p)\), whereas the expected main term is

\[
M e_3\!\left(\left(\frac{|Z_p|}{p}\right)_p\right)
\asymp X^{-1}e_3((|Z_p|)_p).
\]

Thus the generic CRT estimate loses exactly a factor \(X\). This is the same gap exhibited constructively by `prop:hm3-anchored-star`.

## 3. What is known for the candidate sequences

### Summary table

| sequence | strongest directly relevant unconditional result located | full Erdős–Kac or critical shell moments? |
|---|---|---|
| Fibonacci \(F_n\) | almost all \(n\) satisfy \(\omega(F_n)\ge(\log n)^{\log2+o(1)}\); classification of \(\omega(F_n)\le2\) | no such theorem located |
| Mersenne \(2^n-1\) | almost all \(n\) satisfy \(\omega(2^n-1)>2^{(1-\varepsilon)\log\log n}-3\); classification of at most three factors | no such theorem located |
| \(n!+1\) | lower bounds for the largest prime factor, e.g. \(\limsup P(n!+1)/n\ge2.5\) | no such theorem located |
| partition function \(p(n)\) | extensive congruence/divisibility theory, but no termwise \(\omega(p(n))\) Erdős–Kac law located | no |
| \(\binom{2n}{n}\) | every prime \(n<p\le2n\) divides it exactly once | deterministic anti-example |
| Catalan \(C_n\) | same shell, except a possible cancellation at \(p=n+1\) | deterministic anti-example |
| Apéry \(b_n\) | density-one lower bounds for \(\omega(b_n)\) and the largest prime factor | no Erdős–Kac/HM3 theorem |

The negative entries mean that a targeted literature search through August 2026 did not locate such a theorem; they are not claims that no result could exist under a different terminology.

### 3.1 Fibonacci numbers

Bugeaud, Luca, Mignotte and Siksek proved

\[
\omega(F_n)\ge (\log n)^{\log 2+o(1)}
\]

for almost all \(n\), and described the cases with at most two distinct prime factors. Their proof is structural: primitive divisors imply approximately

\[
\omega(F_n)\ge\tau(n)-4,
\]

and the normal order of \(\tau(n)\) gives the result. This is strong information, but it is one-sided and driven by the divisor lattice of the **index**. It is not a Gaussian law for the prime factors of \(F_n\).

For a shell problem there is an exact alternative formulation. Let \(z_F(p)\) be the rank of apparition of \(p\) in the Fibonacci sequence. Apart from the standard exceptional conventions,

\[
p\mid F_m\iff z_F(p)\mid m.
\]

Hence

\[
\sum_{m\le N}
\left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathbf1_{p\mid F_m}\right)_r
=
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_r}^{\!*}
\left\lfloor
\frac{N}{\operatorname{lcm}(z_F(p_1),\ldots,z_F(p_r))}
\right\rfloor.
\tag{3.1}
\]

A shell factorial-moment theorem therefore becomes an average theorem about least common multiples of ranks of apparition. The primitive-divisor theorem does not supply that average, and I did not locate a theorem at the critical range analogous to (1.1).

### 3.2 Mersenne numbers

Cambraia, Knapp, Lemos, Moriya and Rodrigues prove that for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[
\omega(2^n-1)>2^{(1-\varepsilon)\log\log n}-3
\]

for almost all positive integers \(n\), and classify Mersenne numbers with at most three distinct prime factors. Again, this is an almost-all lower bound obtained from the divisibility/primitive-divisor structure, not an Erdős–Kac law.

For odd \(p\),

\[
p\mid 2^m-1\iff \operatorname{ord}_p(2)\mid m,
\]

so the exact analogue of (3.1) is

\[
\sum_{m\le N}
\left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathbf1_{p\mid 2^m-1}\right)_r
=
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_r}^{\!*}
\left\lfloor
\frac{N}{\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{ord}_{p_1}(2),\ldots,
                              \operatorname{ord}_{p_r}(2))}
\right\rfloor.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Thus even this particularly rigid recurrence requires joint order/lcm statistics. Known primitive-divisor and large-order results do not by themselves give the needed high factorial moments for a dyadic shell.

### 3.3 Shifted factorials \(n!+1\)

Luca and Shparlinski proved

\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{P(n!+1)}n\ge2.5,
\]

improving earlier results of Erdős and Stewart. This concerns the largest prime factor. It does not give a normal order, variance, or factorial moments for \(\omega(n!+1)\).

There is also an immediate local obstruction to importing the random-integer model: every prime \(p\le n\) is excluded, since

\[
n!+1\equiv1\pmod p.
\]

All prime factors lie above \(n\), so the usual accumulation of many small nearly independent divisibility events is absent.

### 3.4 The partition function

There is a large literature on congruences and divisibility of \(p(n)\), but the targeted search did not locate an unconditional Erdős–Kac theorem for

\[
\omega(p(n))
\quad\text{or}\quad
\omega_{(X,2X]}(p(n))
\]

over \(n\le N\). Congruences such as Ramanujan-type progressions give special residue classes; they do not amount to the squarefree-modulus joint distribution required by (2.3).

### 3.5 Central binomial coefficients and Catalan numbers

These are explicit counterexamples to a universal “specific sequences should look random” heuristic.

For every prime \(n<p\le2n\),

\[
v_p\binom{2n}{n}
=\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{p}\right\rfloor
 -2\left\lfloor\frac n p\right\rfloor
=1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\omega_{(n,2n]}\binom{2n}{n}
=\pi(2n)-\pi(n).}
\tag{3.3}
\]

For the Catalan number

\[
C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n},
\]

only the prime \(p=n+1\), when it is prime, can be cancelled from that shell. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\omega_{(n,2n]}(C_n)
=\pi(2n)-\pi(n)-\mathbf1_{\{n+1\text{ prime}\}}.}
\tag{3.4}
\]

The shell count is deterministic of order \(n/\log n\), not Poisson.

### 3.6 Apéry numbers: the closest direct theorem

Luca and Shparlinski prove that for a density-one set of indices,

\[
\omega(b_n)>c_0\log\log\log n
\]

and

\[
P(b_n)>c_0(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2}.
\]

Their method applies more generally to certain order-two recurrences with polynomial coefficients and Lucas-type congruences. This is the closest direct literature to the present sequence, but it is still a density-one lower-bound theorem. It does not estimate the joint conditions

\[
p_1\mid b_m,\ldots,p_r\mid b_m
\]

for shell primes, and it does not imply `(HM)_3`.

Two related results should not be overread:

- Luca's `Prime divisors of binary holonomic sequences` proves a lower bound for the number of distinct primes occurring in the product of the numerators and denominators of many sequence terms. It is an aggregate-support result, not a termwise distribution theorem.
- Luca–Shparlinski's `Arithmetic functions with linear recurrence sequences` does obtain all moments for certain arithmetic functions associated with linear recurrences and finite-field orders. Its title does not mean that it proves an Erdős–Kac theorem for \(\omega(u_n)\) of an arbitrary recurrence term.

## 4. Genuine unconditional Erdős–Kac theorems for structured sequences

The answer to the broad existence question is nevertheless **yes**. The following are representative unconditional results that go beyond a Chebyshev bound.

### 4.1 Shifted primes

For fixed nonzero \(a\), Kevin Ford proves a Kubilius-type model for the prime factors of \(p+a\), with \(p\) prime. The result includes a total-variation estimate and shows that prime factors lying in disjoint sets behave like independent Poisson random variables. This is one of the closest established analogues to the requested restricted-prime statement.

Dixit and Murty prove a localized Erdős–Kac theorem for

\[
\omega_y(p+a)=\#\{\ell<y:\ell\mid p+a\}
\]

under a specified slow-growth condition on \(y\). These results use the deep distribution theory of primes in progressions; they are not consequences of the formal identity (2.1) alone.

### 4.2 Beatty sequences

Banks and Shparlinski proved a single-sequence Erdős–Kac law for

\[
\omega(\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor),
\qquad \alpha>0\text{ irrational}.
\]

Their 2007 paper also records mean-value and parity results. Fredy Yip's 2026 preprint explicitly builds on their Gaussian convergence and proves multivariate and quantitative versions, including a universal one-dimensional Kolmogorov-distance bound. This is a genuine explicit non-polynomial sequence with a full Erdős–Kac law.

The enabling input is uniform distribution of the Beatty sequence in residue classes, obtained by Fourier/Diophantine methods. In other words, the theorem works because (2.2) is available uniformly enough over the relevant squarefree moduli.

### 4.3 Polynomial values and algebraic varieties

Yu-Ru Liu axiomatized Erdős–Kac in a general arithmetic-semigroup setting and applied it to number fields, function fields, and geometrically irreducible varieties over finite fields. Maosheng Xiong proved Erdős–Kac theorems for polynomials in several variables on certain varieties arising from translations and affine linear transformations.

El-Baz, Loughran and Sofos prove multivariate normal laws for the numbers of primes dividing coordinates, or more general divisors, of integral points on varieties. Their general sequence theorem is notable because it does not require a positive exponent of level of distribution in the usual form. It still requires sufficiently accurate local-density and correlation information; it does not make that information automatic.

### 4.4 Automorphic constructions

Murty, Murty and Pujahari prove an “all-purpose Erdős–Kac theorem” and apply it to the number of prime factors of sums of Fourier coefficients of Hecke eigenforms. This is another genuine arithmetic sequence application, but its hypotheses are verified using the algebraic and Galois structure of the automorphic coefficients.

### What these examples have in common

They all establish, in one form or another, that the sample family is well distributed in the simultaneous congruence conditions generated by many small primes. That is the substantive theorem. Once this is known, the probabilistic conclusion follows by moments, characteristic functions, Stein–Chen methods, or a Kubilius model.

A recurrence relation by itself does not supply such distribution. Indeed, a recurrence often creates extra divisibility through ranks of apparition, index divisibility, or fixed congruence classes.

## 5. Restricted prime sets and the exact criterion for factorial moments

Let \(\mathcal P_X\) be any prime set. Equation (2.1) immediately gives the exact criterion

\[
\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}
(\omega_{\mathcal P_X}(a_n))_r
=
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_r}^{\!*}
\frac{A_{p_1\cdots p_r}(N)}N.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Thus a sufficient estimate is

\[
A_{p_1\cdots p_r}(N)
=N\prod_{i=1}^r g(p_i)+R(p_1,\ldots,p_r)
\]

with

\[
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_r}^{\!*}
|R(p_1,\ldots,p_r)|
\ll N\left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}g(p)\right)^r.
\tag{5.2}
\]

For an asymptotic independent-Poisson law one asks for an error smaller than the main term. For the one-sided HM bound, (5.2) at the same order suffices.

For random integers, \(g(p)=1/p\). For shifted primes, the relevant local density is roughly \(1/(p-1)\), and Ford's theorem supplies a much stronger joint model. For Fibonacci or Mersenne values, the relevant local density over the **index** is respectively

\[
\frac1{z_F(p)}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\frac1{\operatorname{ord}_p(2)},
\]

with joint density governed by the lcm in (3.1) or (3.2). For Apéry values, it is

\[
\frac{|Z_p|}{p},
\]

with joint conditions represented by CRT cylinders in the index variable.

These are distinct arithmetic models. One cannot transfer the random-integer theorem simply because all of them count prime divisors.

## 6. Which techniques genuinely apply to Apéry shell moments?

### 6.1 Brun's pure sieve and the method of factorial moments

This is the correct probabilistic language. To prove a Poisson law with parameter \(\lambda_X\), or only the HM upper bounds, compute (5.1). The task is then exactly a squarefree-modulus incidence theorem for products of shell primes.

For Apéry, this method is not a heuristic—it is already the repository's exact expansion. Its limitation is equally exact: the elementary CRT remainder is summable for pairs at the desired scale but not for triples.

### 6.2 Granville–Soundararajan/Turán–Kubilius moment machinery

This machinery converts local-density estimates into centered Gaussian moments. Applied formally here, it identifies the same missing quantity: the aggregate remainder over moduli formed by up to three shell primes. It does not provide that remainder estimate.

Moreover, the usual Gaussian proof truncates to small primes and discards the large-prime tail. Here the shell itself consists of primes of size \(M^{1/2}\), so there is no smaller-prime surrogate for the target statistic.

### 6.3 Classical and larger sieves

A large sieve can control average Fourier energy or the size of a set occupying few residue classes. The anchored-star construction in `prop:hm3-anchored-star` shows that the currently known local features—small \(|Z_p|\), reflection, interval sparsity, natural second moment, and bounded row codegrees—are compatible with a common row hit by almost every active prime. Therefore no generic large-sieve theorem depending only on those data can prove HM3.

A useful large-sieve input would have to be specifically sensitive to the Apéry phases or to cross-prime algebraic incompatibility, not merely to the cardinalities of \(Z_p\).

### 6.4 Affine sieve and expansion

The affine sieve of Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak proves squarefree-modulus equidistribution for values of polynomials along suitable group orbits. Expansion of congruence graphs supplies the mixing needed by a Brun sieve. Bourgain–Kontorovich's “beyond expansion” work combines bilinear forms and the dispersion method to improve levels of distribution in thin-orbit problems.

This is a plausible **framework**, but only if the Apéry incidence problem can be realized as an orbit or dynamical system with:

1. strong approximation modulo squarefree products,
2. expansion or another uniform mixing theorem, and
3. a polynomial condition encoding \(b_m\equiv0\pmod p\).

The presently available Picard–Fuchs/monodromy description is not such a squarefree-modulus orbit theorem for the index interval \([0,X^2)\).

### 6.5 Large sieve for Frobenius and geometric monodromy

Kowalski's large sieve for Frobenius controls the distribution of Frobenius conjugacy classes in algebraic families over a common finite-field base and exploits large product monodromy. Katz–Deligne machinery can then give independence across auxiliary representations.

That theory is highly relevant conceptually, but the current Apéry problem is different in two ways:

- the primes \(p,q,r\) are different characteristics, and
- the same short integer interval \(0\le m<X^2\) is embedded into three different finite fields.

Primewise monodromy may predict the distribution or typical size of \(Z_p\) as \(p\) varies. It does not by itself control whether selected roots for different primes have CRT representative below \(X^2\). The latter is an Archimedean short-box condition.

### 6.6 Deligne bounds for complete sums

Complete trace-function estimates can control sums over all \(m\bmod p\). They do not automatically control the mixed-characteristic incomplete correlation

\[
\sum_{m<X^2}
\mathbf1_{m\bmod p\in Z_p}
\mathbf1_{m\bmod q\in Z_q}
\mathbf1_{m\bmod r\in Z_r}.
\]

After completion modulo \(pqr\), the local sums factor and the interval cutoff becomes the difficult Fourier kernel. Since \(|Z_p|\) is small, the trivial local bound on the zero-set Fourier transform is often already stronger than a generic \(O(\sqrt p)\) bound. The difficulty is cross-prime phase alignment, not a single local complete sum.

### 6.7 Stein–Chen and dependency-graph Poisson approximation

These tools can turn estimates for joint occurrence probabilities into a Poisson approximation. They cannot create those estimates. Q8867 exhibits this sharply: an artificial independent lift has a controlled random variance, but transferring it to the deterministic interval is exactly the missing discrepancy theorem.

## 7. Comparison with Linnik's dispersion method

The analogy is real but must be stated precisely.

Linnik's dispersion method bounds a variance after expanding a square. In prime-distribution applications, Vaughan/Heath-Brown identities make the von Mangoldt weight bilinear, and the resulting off-diagonal terms are controlled by exponential-sum, Kloosterman, spectral, or Deligne estimates. Bombieri–Friedlander–Iwaniec used this circle of ideas to obtain distribution of primes to moduli beyond the Bombieri–Vinogradov range in important weighted or fixed-residue settings. It is not a general theorem that primes are uniformly distributed to every modulus beyond level \(1/2\).

There is a direct Apéry analogue at the **pair-anchor** level. Define

\[
J_{p,q}=|\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q|,
\]

\[
L_{p,q}(m)=
\sum_{\ell\notin\{p,q\}}\mathbf1_{\Omega_\ell}(m),
\]

and, using exact finite marginals,

\[
\rho_\ell=\frac{|\Omega_\ell|}{M},
\qquad
\mu_{p,q}=\sum_{\ell\notin\{p,q\}}\rho_\ell.
\]

Then the ordered third factorial moment is exactly

\[
F_3(X)
=
\sum_{p\ne q}
\sum_{m\in\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q}L_{p,q}(m).
\tag{7.1}
\]

A Linnik/Barban–Davenport–Halberstam-style sufficient theorem would be

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal D_3(X):=
\sum_{p\ne q}
\sum_{m\in\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q}
\bigl(L_{p,q}(m)-\mu_{p,q}\bigr)^2
\ll X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^3.}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Indeed, for large \(X\) one has \(\mu_{p,q}\le1/2\). Since \(L_{p,q}(m)\) is a nonnegative integer,

\[
L_{p,q}(m)\le4\bigl(L_{p,q}(m)-\mu_{p,q}\bigr)^2
\qquad\text{whenever }L_{p,q}(m)\ge1.
\]

Terms with \(L_{p,q}(m)=0\) do not contribute to (7.1). Hence

\[
F_3(X)\le4\mathcal D_3(X),
\]

so (7.2) proves `(HM)_3`.

This is a clean direct analogue of dispersion: condition on a two-prime fiber, center the remaining-prime load, and bound its aggregate variance.

What is missing is the analogue of the prime-specific analytic structure. For \(\Lambda(n)\), convolution identities produce Type I/II bilinear sums and Kloosterman-type cancellation. No comparable factorization is currently known for

\[
\ell\mapsto\mathbf1_{\{b_{m\bmod\ell}\equiv0\pmod\ell\}}
\]

on an actual pair CRT locus. The invalid bilinear floor-factorization noted in the project does not supply it.

Thus dispersion is the right **shape** of a sufficient theorem, not an already available black box.

## 8. The weakest Apéry-specific input that proves `(HM)_3`

The repository has already isolated the exact answer.

With

\[
T_{p,q}=
\sum_{m\in\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q}L_{p,q}(m),
\]

define the finite pair-Palm positive excess

\[
\boxed{
\mathscr P_3^{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
=
\sum_{p\ne q}
\bigl(T_{p,q}-J_{p,q}\mu_{p,q}\bigr)_+.}
\tag{8.1}
\]

`thm:hm3-palm-characterization` proves the exact sandwich

\[
\mathscr P_3^{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
\le F_3(X)
\le
\mathscr P_3^{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
+5(1+2/X)M\lambda_X^3.
\tag{8.2}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
(\mathrm{HM})_3
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathscr P_3^{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
\ll X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^3.}
\tag{8.3}
\]

This is weaker than each of the following natural sufficient inputs:

1. a uniform conditional estimate for every pair \((p,q)\);
2. the quadratic dispersion estimate (7.2);
3. full three-prime asymptotic independence with square-root error;
4. total-variation approximation by an independent Bernoulli/Poisson model.

A conditional anti-concentration theorem of the form

\[
T_{p,q}
\le J_{p,q}\mu_{p,q}+E_{p,q},
\qquad
\sum_{p\ne q}(E_{p,q})_+
\ll X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^3,
\tag{8.4}
\]

would prove (8.3). But (8.4) is best understood as a **summable pair-fiber extension theorem**. Calling it merely “monodromy” or “Erdős–Kac” does not identify a known result that supplies it.

The anchored-star proposition proves that primewise facts are insufficient. Its artificial zero sets satisfy reflection, endpoint avoidance, interval sparsity, natural second-moment bounds, and bounded row codegrees, yet all active columns share one row and the third moment is too large by a factor of order \(X\). Therefore the new theorem must exclude coherent alignment of different characteristics on actual pair fibers.

### Known frameworks that could, in principle, deliver (8.4)

A proof would need one of the following genuinely new Apéry-specific structures:

- **squarefree-level expansion:** an orbit model mixing uniformly modulo \(pqr\);
- **product monodromy plus short-box equidistribution:** not merely independent Frobenius classes, but control of the initial interval inside the CRT product;
- **bilinear dispersion for reciprocal CRT phases:** a factorization that converts the pair-conditioned extension count into exponential sums with cancellation;
- **an arithmetic incompatibility theorem:** a result showing that many zero congruences cannot coherently point to the same small integer except on a sufficiently small exceptional set.

At present, no standard theorem in the literature turns the Picard–Fuchs monodromy of the Apéry family directly into any of these statements.

## 9. Direct answers to parts (a)–(e)

### (a) Are there Erdős–Kac theorems for specific sequences?

Yes, unconditionally, for several highly structured but sufficiently equidistributed sequences: shifted primes, Beatty sequences, polynomial values on suitable varieties, integral points, and some automorphic constructions.

For the named recurrence candidates Fibonacci and Mersenne, the strongest directly relevant results located are almost-all lower bounds and classifications of terms with few prime factors, not Gaussian laws. For \(n!+1\) the nearby literature concerns large prime factors. No termwise theorem was located for the partition function. Central binomial and Catalan numbers are deterministic anti-examples in their natural dyadic shell.

### Are fixed factorial-moment bounds known?

For a family satisfying uniform squarefree-modulus distribution, yes: fixed truncated factorial moments follow from (2.3). For the total \(\omega(a_n)\), a fixed constant bound is usually the wrong normalization because the mean grows. For the critical shell counts of the recurrence examples above, I did not locate the analogue of (1.1).

### (b) What is known for restricted primes?

For random integers, the dyadic-shell mean is \((\log2)/\log X+o(1/\log X)\), not one. For shifted primes, Ford's total-variation Kubilius model is a strong restricted-prime theorem, and localized Erdős–Kac results are known. General restricted-prime theorems reduce to uniform estimates for \(A_d(N)\) with \(d\) supported on the chosen prime set.

No corresponding critical-shell theorem was located for Fibonacci, Mersenne, or Apéry numbers.

### (c) Which multiplicative-number-theory tools apply?

The directly applicable tools are Brun factorial moments, abstract sieve moment theorems, dispersion/second-moment methods, and—if an orbit realization exists—affine-sieve expansion. Frobenius large sieves and Deligne estimates may provide ingredients, but primewise monodromy does not itself control the short CRT landing condition.

The exact arithmetic input remains an aggregate squarefree-modulus or pair-fiber correlation estimate.

### (d) Is there a direct Linnik-dispersion analogue?

Yes at the level of formulation: (7.2) is a precise pair-anchored variance theorem that would imply HM3. What is absent is the analytic decomposition that makes Linnik/BFI dispersion effective for primes. The analogy identifies the right target but does not close it.

### (e) What is the weakest Apéry-specific input?

The exact weakest named residual in the current project is (8.3), the aggregate finite pair-Palm positive excess. Uniform conditional anti-concentration and the L² dispersion bound are stronger sufficient forms. No existing generic Erdős–Kac, affine-sieve, or Frobenius-large-sieve theorem presently supplies (8.3) from the known Apéry local data.

## 10. Recommended terminology and research route

For this project, I would not advertise `(HM)_3` as “an Erdős–Kac theorem for Apéry numbers” without qualification. That phrase suggests a Gaussian limit with a diverging mean and can obscure the actual obstruction.

The source-faithful description is:

> **A sparse Brun–Poisson factorial-moment theorem for Apéry zero cylinders in the critical shell \(p\asymp M^{1/2}\), whose first missing case is a pair-anchored three-prime dispersion estimate.**

The next mathematically meaningful target is one of these two equivalent/implicational forms:

```text
Exact weakest residual:
  sum_{p != q} (T_pq - J_pq * mu_pq)_+
      << X^(2+o(1)) * lambda_X^3.

Stronger Linnik-style target:
  sum_{p != q} sum_{m in Omega_p intersect Omega_q}
      (L_pq(m) - mu_pq)^2
      << X^(2+o(1)) * lambda_X^3.
```

A proof should be sought through an Apéry-specific cross-prime mechanism. More information about the marginal distribution of \(|Z_p|\), reflection symmetry, or individual Frobenius traces cannot alone rule out the anchored-star alignment.
