# Verdict

CMRD is close in spirit to Drappeau's dispersion problems, but Drappeau's theorem does not apply as a black box. The missing input is not the individual Kloosterman bound; it is a **semiprime-modulus, factored-modulus dispersion theorem** preserving the arithmetic relation between the two prime factors.

The correct reformulation is:

$$
S=\sum_{p,\ell\sim X}|B_{p\ell}|^2,
\qquad
B_{p\ell}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal Z_{p\ell}}e_{p\ell}(Az^{-1}).
$$

After expansion,

$$
S=\sum_{p,\ell}\sum_{z_1,z_2}e_{p\ell}(A(z_1^{-1}-z_2^{-1})).
$$

The diagonal gives the expected $L^6$ contribution. The problem is controlling the off-diagonal where the reciprocal phases from different semiprimes correlate.

# (A) Can Drappeau's method be transferred?

Only partially.

Drappeau's framework has three ingredients:

1. completion to Kloosterman sums;
2. averaging over many moduli;
3. Kuznetsov/spectral cancellation in the modulus variable.

CMRD has the first ingredient in a disguised form, but the other two are different.

## 1. Semiprime restriction

Drappeau averages over

$$
q\le Q,
$$

where the modulus variable is dense. CMRD averages over

$$
q=p\ell,
$$

with $p,\ell\sim X$.

This is a thin set of moduli. Spectral methods usually need either:

- a smooth weight over all moduli;
- or a trace formula adapted to the arithmetic family.

The semiprime family has additional structure, but loses density.

## 2. Reciprocal sum versus Kloosterman sum

The classical Kloosterman sum is

$$
S(m,n;q)=\sum_{x\bmod q}^*e_q(mx+n x^{-1}).
$$

CMRD has essentially

$$
\sum_z e_{p\ell}(Az^{-1}).
$$

This is the degenerate case with one additive coefficient missing. It is actually simpler locally, but harder globally because the variables $p,\ell$ occur inside the modulus factorization.

## 3. The factored modulus is the main issue

The useful structure is not just averaging over $p\ell$; it is the CRT factorization:

$$
e_{p\ell}(Az^{-1})
=e_p(A\ell^*z^{-1})e_\ell(Ap^*z^{-1}).
$$

The inverses $\ell^*,p^*$ couple the two primes. This coupling is exactly what a generic Kuznetsov average over $q$ does not see.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Drappeau gives the correct philosophy, but not the required theorem directly.}
}
$$

# (B) CRT decomposition

The CRT identity is valid:

$$
e_{p\ell}(Az^{-1})
=e_p(A\ell^{-1}z^{-1})e_\ell(Ap^{-1}z^{-1}).
$$

However, this does not split the bilinear problem.

If

$$
z=qr,
$$

then the local factor is

$$
e_p(A\ell^*(qr)^{-1}).
$$

The variables $q,r$ remain coupled through

$$
(qr)^{-1}=q^{-1}r^{-1}.
$$

One can rewrite the inner sum as a multiplicative convolution:

$$
\sum_{q,r}e_p(A\ell^*q^{-1}r^{-1}),
$$

but the two prime sums are not independent.

The correct local object is a multiplicative Fourier transform:

$$
\widehat{1_Q}(\chi)\widehat{1_R}(\chi^{-1}),
$$

and one must average the resulting character sums simultaneously over $p$ and $\ell$.

# (C) Does Cauchy-Schwarz over p solve it?

It produces the right kind of expression, but not automatically the right bound.

After Cauchy-Schwarz one obtains terms like

$$
\sum_p\sum_{\ell_1,\ell_2}
B_{p\ell_1}\overline{B_{p\ell_2}}.
$$

CRT expansion gives phases of the form

$$
e_p(A(\ell_1^*z_1^{-1}-\ell_2^*z_2^{-1})).
$$

Summing over $p$ indeed gives cancellation unless

$$
\ell_1^*z_1^{-1}\equiv \ell_2^*z_2^{-1}\pmod p.
$$

But this congruence is itself a moving target because $p$ appears in $\ell_i^*$.

The completion argument reduces the problem to:

1. Weil-type bounds for non-degenerate prime-modulus sums;
2. counting exceptional coincidence solutions.

The second part is the hard one.

A typical coincidence equation becomes

$$
\ell_1 z_2\equiv \ell_2 z_1\pmod p.
$$

Since all variables are about size $\sqrt X$, the product sizes are about $X$, and this is close to the square-root barrier. One needs a genuine dispersion estimate, not just Weil.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Cauchy-Schwarz exposes the correct obstruction but does not remove it.}
}
$$

# (D) Related literature

There are related ingredients, but not an off-the-shelf semiprime theorem matching CMRD.

## Fouvry--Kowalski--Michel

FKM developed powerful bilinear estimates for trace functions and Kloosterman-type sums. Their results are closest conceptually because they handle:

- families of finite-field trace functions;
- bilinear sums;
- monodromy-driven cancellation.

However, the CMRD modulus is varying in the defining primes $p,\ell$, and the reciprocal phase contains the factorization data. This is outside the standard fixed-modulus trace-function setup.

## Bettin--Chandee

Bettin--Chandee type estimates handle bilinear forms with Kloosterman fractions and shifted convolution structures. They are relevant to reciprocal phases, but their averaging variables are different from the semiprime CRT family here.

## Kowalski--Michel--Sawin

KMS and related work on algebraic trace functions give strong cancellation for families with controlled conductor. The obstacle again is not the local conductor; it is constructing a bounded-conductor family whose parameter space is the semiprime factorization $(p,\ell)$.

The missing theorem is best described as:

> A semiprime modulus dispersion theorem for reciprocal trace functions with two moving defining characteristics.

# (E) What exactly appears after CRT + Cauchy?

Yes, the reduction is essentially:

$$
\text{prime-modulus exponential sums}
+
\text{coincidence counting}.
$$

More explicitly, after opening the square one gets diagonal terms

$$
z_1=z_2,
$$

and off-diagonal terms controlled by sums like

$$
\sum_p e_p(A(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)).
$$

If $\alpha_1\neq\alpha_2$, Weil gives square-root cancellation. If

$$
\alpha_1=\alpha_2\pmod p,
$$

then one obtains an incidence problem.

The required ARK estimate is therefore equivalent to showing that the incidence locus is sparse.

# The missing theorem in CMRD language

The desired statement is not really a Kuznetsov theorem. It is closer to a large-sieve theorem for a two-parameter family:

$$
\sum_{p,\ell\sim X}
\left|
\sum_z
e_{p\ell}(Az^{-1})
\right|^2
\ll X^{o(1)}L^6.
$$

A successful proof likely needs:

1. CRT factorization;
2. multiplicative Fourier expansion in $q,r$;
3. large sieve over characters modulo the prime factors;
4. a bound for exceptional coincidence varieties.

The coincidence variety is the true analogue of the diagonal in the large sieve.

# Final assessment

The answer to (E) is yes: CRT + Cauchy transforms CMRD into individual prime-modulus exponential sums plus a coincidence-counting problem.

The answer to (C) is also yes in spirit: Cauchy exposes the right structure.

But the final counting problem is exactly the hard part. Existing Drappeau/FKM/Bettin-Chandee/KMS techniques provide pieces:

- Weil bounds control generic phases;
- spectral theory handles dense modulus averages;
- trace-function theory handles bounded-conductor families.

CMRD requires their combination in a new setting:

$$
\boxed{
\text{semiprime CRT dispersion for reciprocal sums.}
}
$$

Proving this ARK theorem would be a genuine advance and would provide the missing off-diagonal input for the CMRD route to P3.2.
