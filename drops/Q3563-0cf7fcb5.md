# P3.2 — self-improving rigidity: can one large \(K_6(n_0)\) force nearby large values?

## Executive verdict

**No, not from the currently banked local/one-prime information.** In fact the recurrence points in the opposite direction: a hit at row \(n_0\) is *anti-persistent* under \(n\mapsto n_0\pm1\). The same prime cannot survive one step, and the reflection symmetry sends its companion zero to a row depending injectively on that prime, so the reflected hits disperse rather than coalesce.

There is an even sharper obstruction. One can make a q=6-specific incidence model in which

\[
K_6(n_0)\asymp \frac{n_0}{42\log n_0},
\]

while

\[
K_6(n)\le 1\qquad(n\ne n_0),
\]

and every pair of distinct rows has codegree at most \(1\). The model also has reflection symmetry, \(|Z_p|\le2\), no consecutive zeros, endpoint avoidance after deleting \(O(1)\) primes, and the same reflection gap certificate used in the campaign. It is not the actual Apéry sequence, but it proves a precise logical point: **isolation plus excellent codegree is fully compatible with a linear-size rainbow pileup at one row.**

Therefore the desired contradiction cannot be obtained from “large row + nearby codegree repulsion” alone.

What can kill an isolated spike is one of three genuinely stronger mechanisms:

1. a **global carrier** of height \(e^{o(n)}\) divisible by every hit prime;
2. a **cross-characteristic factorial-energy bound**: one isolated row of load \(M\) already contributes \((M)_2\) to the q=6 pair energy, so a sufficiently strong global pair-energy estimate forbids it without any propagation;
3. a true **replication/coalescence identity**: many of the \(M\) witnesses at \(n_0\) must be transformed into packets of witnesses producing more than \(O((\log N)^2)\) *other bad rows*.

The third mechanism is much stronger than ordinary recurrence propagation. I do not know a holonomic-zero theorem in the literature that gives it. Standard Skolem–Mahler–Lech/holonomic-zero results either concern one fixed characteristic or allow a sparse exceptional set; positive-characteristic zero sets can even have Frobenius/automatic sparse structure. None says that one isolated exceptional row must generate nearby exceptional rows.

There is also an important source-audit caveat: connector-visible current `main` is commit `47fa0e653f52c4a71e9a8c26b31ca9f66f6bbe86`, and `problems/3.2/hm3_result.tex` explicitly says the written proof of the older `lem:codegree` had a quotient-wrap gap. The conclusion below does **not** depend on that gap: the same file proves an anchored-star countermodel with *uniform \(O(1)\) row codegree*, which is stronger than what is needed for the no-go. So even granting the advertised bound

\[
W(m,m')\le 6h+O(h^2/\log X),\qquad h=|m-m'|,
\]

does not create replication.

## 1. A one-step shift destroys every inherited q=6 hit

Let

\[
S_6(n):=\{p:n/7<p\le n/6,\ p\mid b_{n-6p}\},
\qquad K_6(n)=|S_6(n)|.
\]

Fix \(p\in S_6(n_0)\), and write

\[
j=n_0-6p.
\]

For a genuine hit, \(j\) is automatically an interior residue. Indeed

\[
b_0=1,
\]

and the standard endpoint congruence gives

\[
b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p,
\]

so a hit has

\[
1\le j\le p-2.
\]

The Apéry recurrence is

\[
(j+1)^3b_{j+1}=P(j)b_j-j^3b_{j-1}.
\]

If \(p\mid b_j\), then modulo \(p\)

\[
(j+1)^3b_{j+1}\equiv-j^3b_{j-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

Because \(j,j+1\) are units modulo \(p\), if also \(p\mid b_{j+1}\), then \(p\mid b_{j-1}\). Iterating backwards would force \(p\mid b_0=1\), impossible. Hence

\[
\boxed{p\mid b_j\Longrightarrow p\nmid b_{j+1}.}
\tag{2}
\]

The same argument in the other direction gives

\[
\boxed{p\mid b_j\Longrightarrow p\nmid b_{j-1}.}
\tag{3}
\]

Thus, whenever the same prime remains in both geometric windows,

\[
S_6(n_0)\cap S_6(n_0+1)=\varnothing,
\qquad
S_6(n_0)\cap S_6(n_0-1)=\varnothing.
\tag{4}
\]

So the user's diagnosis is correct, and it is actually stronger than “generically nonzero”: for genuine interior hits, the inherited prime is **provably absent** one step later.

The mod-\(p^2\) first-jet formula

\[
b_j\equiv p\,b_6(\eta-6c_j)\pmod{p^2}
\]

does not change this one-step conclusion. It controls the singular first jet at the hit; the zero/nonzero question at \(j\pm1\) is already decided modulo \(p\) by the nonsingular recurrence and gap-1 impossibility.

### Critical distinction

Equation (4) says that the **same witnesses** disappear. It does *not* say

\[
K_6(n_0\pm1)\text{ is small}.
\]

A neighboring row could in principle have many completely different prime witnesses. No one-prime recurrence relation couples those new characteristics to the old ones.

So “codegree is small” means **low overlap of witness sets**, not “low degree of every neighboring row.”

## 2. What a codegree bound actually gives: packing, not replication

Suppose we have rows

\[
n_1,\ldots,n_t
\]

in a short interval around size \(X\), and write

\[
S_i=S_6(n_i),\qquad |S_i|\ge M.
\]

Assume a pairwise codegree estimate

\[
|S_i\cap S_j|\le B
\qquad(i\ne j).
\tag{5}
\]

Let \(U\) be the union of all candidate primes occurring in the windows of these rows, and put \(L=|U|\). If \(d_p\) is the number of sets \(S_i\) containing \(p\), then

\[
\sum_{p\in U}d_p\ge tM
\]

and

\[
\sum_{p\in U}d_p^2
=
\sum_i|S_i|+2\sum_{i<j}|S_i\cap S_j|
\le tM+t(t-1)B
\]

if we take the threshold case \(|S_i|=M\); with larger degrees the same argument can be written using the actual sum. Cauchy gives the clean packing inequality

\[
\boxed{
L\ge
\frac{(tM)^2}{tM+t(t-1)B}.}
\tag{6}
\]

When \(B=o(M/t)\), this becomes

\[
L\ge(1-o(1))tM.
\tag{7}
\]

For q=6 at scale \(X\), a single prime window has only

\[
\pi(X/6)-\pi(X/7)
\sim \frac{X}{42\log X}
\tag{8}
\]

candidate primes. A short union of nearby windows has the same order. Thus if

\[
M\ge \varepsilon X/\log X,
\]

and \(B=o(M)\), (6) merely says that the number of simultaneously large rows in that short neighborhood is \(O_\varepsilon(1)\).

That is **repulsion**, not contradiction.

At distance one the situation is even cleaner: \(B=0\) exactly by (4). Two consecutive large rows would have to use disjoint prime sets. This is perfectly combinatorially possible whenever their two loads fit inside the available prime pool. For small fixed \(\varepsilon\), there is plenty of room.

If one grants the campaign estimate

\[
B(h)\le6h+O(h^2/\log X),
\]

then for \(h=o(\sqrt X)\),

\[
B(h)=o(X/\log X).
\]

Consequently a threshold \(M\asymp\varepsilon X/\log X\) makes bad rows in an \(o(\sqrt X)\)-neighborhood essentially disjoint in prime labels. Again the conclusion is: **only constantly many such rows can coexist locally.** This strengthens isolation; it does not eliminate an isolated row.

This is entirely compatible with a theorem saying there are only \(O_\varepsilon((\log N)^2)\) bad rows up to \(N\).

## 3. Exact q=6 star obstruction: one huge isolated row with codegree \(\le1\)

There is a particularly transparent model adapted exactly to this question.

Fix a large row \(n_0\), and let

\[
\mathcal P_0=\{p\text{ prime}:n_0/7<p\le n_0/6\}.
\]

For each \(p\in\mathcal P_0\), put

\[
j_p=n_0-6p.
\tag{9}
\]

Thus \(0\le j_p<p\). Delete the at most \(O(1)\) primes for which an endpoint or the reflection fixed point causes a degeneracy, and define the fake zero set

\[
\boxed{
Z_p^*=\{j_p,p-1-j_p\}.}
\tag{10}
\]

For all other primes set \(Z_p^*=\varnothing\).

Then every active prime hits the same center row:

\[
6p+j_p=n_0.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
K_6^*(n_0)=|\mathcal P_0|-O(1)
\sim\frac{n_0}{42\log n_0}.}
\tag{11}
\]

Now look at the reflected zero

\[
r_p=p-1-j_p.
\]

Its q=6 row is

\[
\begin{aligned}
n_p^*
&=6p+r_p\\
&=6p+p-1-j_p\\
&=13p-1-n_0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

The map

\[
p\longmapsto13p-1-n_0
\]

is injective. Hence the reflected witnesses go to **different rows**. Since each active \(p\) has only the two prescribed residues (10), one obtains

\[
\boxed{K_6^*(n)\le1\quad\text{for every }n\ne n_0.}
\tag{13}
\]

Moreover, any two distinct rows share at most one prime:

\[
\boxed{|S_6^*(m)\cap S_6^*(n)|\le1\qquad(m\ne n).}
\tag{14}
\]

This model has:

- \(|Z_p^*|\le2\), far stronger than \(p^{2/3}\);
- exact reflection symmetry;
- no consecutive pair (the two reflected residues have even difference because their sum is \(p-1\));
- endpoint avoidance after deleting finitely many boundary primes;
- an isolated row of maximal natural order \(\asymp n_0/\log n_0\);
- uniform row codegree at most \(1\).

The current campaign source `problems/3.2/hm3_result.tex` proves the same logical phenomenon in a more general incidence normalization: its “anchored rainbow star with bounded codegrees” has zero sets of size \(0\) or \(2\), reflection symmetry, no consecutive elements, strong interval/fiber/energy bounds, natural second moment, and uniform row codegree \(\le7\), while one row has \(\asymp X/\log X\) labels. It also proves that a reflected two-point orbit automatically satisfies the necessary gap-polynomial reflection certificate. See:

- `problems/3.2/hm3_result.tex`, Proposition `hm3-anchored-star` and the following “Scope of the obstruction” remark, current connector-visible main commit `47fa0e653f52c4a71e9a8c26b31ca9f66f6bbe86`.

The q=6 construction (9)–(14) is the same obstruction with the slope built in explicitly.

### Scope

This is **not** a counterfeit Apéry sequence. It does not say the actual \(Z_p\) can be chosen arbitrarily. It proves exactly what is needed here:

> Any attempted contradiction using only reflection, local gap exclusion, per-prime cardinality/energy bounds, and small row codegrees cannot rule out one isolated rainbow star.

Something must constrain **which reflection orbit is selected across different characteristics**.

## 4. Reflection is a propagation law — but it disperses maximally

For a genuine Apéry hit

\[
n_0=6p+j,\qquad p\mid b_j,
\]

reflection gives

\[
p\mid b_{p-1-j}.
\]

Thus the same prime gives a second q=6 hit at

\[
\boxed{n'=13p-1-n_0.}
\tag{15}
\]

As \(p\) runs through \((n_0/7,n_0/6]\), these child rows lie roughly in

\[
\left[\frac67n_0,\frac76n_0\right],
\]

but they are all distinct because (15) is injective in \(p\).

So a row carrying \(M\) primes produces \(M\) additional *incidences*, but in the worst possible shape for a density-theorem contradiction:

\[
M\text{ parent hits}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
M\text{ different child rows, one hit each}.
\]

No child row is forced to be exceptional.

This is an important diagnostic for any proposed self-improvement theorem: **counting inherited incidences is useless unless they coalesce into high-load rows.**

## 5. A general coalescence criterion explains why the obvious symmetries fail

Suppose a one-prime zero transformation has an affine form

\[
j' = A p+Bj+C
\tag{16}
\]

and starts from a q=6 hit

\[
j=n_0-6p.
\]

The q=6 row associated to \(j'\) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
n'
&=6p+j'\\
&=(A+6-6B)p+B n_0+C.
\end{aligned}
\tag{17}
\]

For **all parent primes to coalesce into one common child row**, the coefficient of \(p\) must vanish:

\[
\boxed{A+6-6B=0.}
\tag{18}
\]

Now inspect the natural transformations.

### Identity / Lucas reuse

\[
j'=j
\]

has \((A,B,C)=(0,1,0)\), so (18) holds — but the child row is just

\[
n'=n_0.
\]

No new row is produced.

If one reuses the same low digit in another quotient \(q'\), then

\[
n'=q'p+j=n_0+(q'-6)p,
\]

which disperses unless \(q'=6\), the original row.

### Reflection

\[
j'=p-1-j
\]

has \((A,B,C)=(1,-1,-1)\). Equation (17) gives

\[
n'=13p-1-n_0,
\]

so the coefficient of \(p\) is \(13\), maximally nonzero. Reflection disperses.

### Fixed shift

A hypothetical forced zero

\[
j'=j+h
\]

would have \((A,B,C)=(0,1,h)\) and therefore

\[
n'=n_0+h,
\]

which is exactly the desired replication: every one of the \(M\) primes would survive at a common nearby row.

But the Apéry recurrence says precisely that no such universal shift exists even for \(h=1\); gap-1 is impossible, and fixed positive gaps are governed by nontrivial gap polynomials rather than forced identities.

This algebraic criterion captures the obstruction succinctly:

> To obtain self-improving row replication from a one-prime symmetry, one needs a zero transformation satisfying the slope-cancellation equation (18). The known nontrivial symmetry — reflection — fails it.

The campaign's digit-reuse propagation result fits the same picture. It creates valid occurrences at other scales/quotients, but the rows depend on \(p\), so the inherited logarithmic weight spreads out rather than forming another q=6 spike.

## 6. Can “isolated but large” itself be contradictory?

Not from the presently available **upper bounds**.

A contradiction from isolation would require some conservation law saying that a large number of prime labels used at \(n_0\) must reappear with substantial multiplicity nearby. But the current local information has the opposite form:

- \(|Z_p|\) is bounded **above**, not below;
- codegree is bounded **above**;
- gap counts are bounded **above**;
- the exceptional-set theorem bounds the number of high rows **above**.

All these inequalities are perfectly happy with a star: one center of enormous degree and many leaves of degree one.

There is no “capacity exhaustion” contradiction. A prime being used once at \(n_0\) does not oblige it to be used again nearby. Even if reflection supplies its second zero, (15) puts that zero at its own private leaf.

### What extra analytic input would change this?

A genuine **lower regularity** statement could. For example, if one could prove that every short row interval must contain approximately a prescribed amount of q=6 incidence mass, then a large center together with codegree repulsion might force compensating structure elsewhere. But no such lower equidistribution theorem is banked, and per-prime upper cardinality gives no route to it.

Likewise, a sharp local variance or factorial-energy theorem could rule out a star, but that is new cross-characteristic information, not a consequence of isolation.

## 7. The cleanest way to kill an isolated spike is pair energy, not replication

If

\[
K_6(n_0)=M,
\]

then that one row alone contributes

\[
\boxed{(M)_2=M(M-1)}
\tag{19}
\]

to the second factorial moment

\[
\mathcal E_6(X)
:=
\sum_{X<n\le2X}(K_6(n))_2.
\tag{20}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\mathcal E_6(X)
=
\sum_{p\ne q}
\#\{j\in Z_p,k\in Z_q:
6p+j=6q+k\in(X,2X]\}.
\tag{21}
\]

So if one could prove

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal E_6(X)
=o\!\left(\frac{X^2}{\log^2X}\right),}
\tag{22}
\]

then no row can have

\[
K_6(n)\ge\varepsilon X/\log X
\]

for any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), because its single contribution (19) would already violate (22).

This is exactly why an isolated spike is not invisible to a sufficiently strong factorial moment. It is invisible only to the **currently proved** almost-all estimates whose scale still permits one spike.

Thus there are two fundamentally different “pair” statements:

1. **row codegree:** how many prime labels two different rows share;
2. **within-row factorial energy:** how many ordered pairs of distinct prime labels coexist on one row.

A star has tiny quantity (1) and huge quantity (2).

The codegree theorem controls the wrong axis for eliminating a one-row spike. The q=6 pair-energy target controls the right axis.

## 8. What would a genuine replication theorem have to look like?

Let

\[
\mathcal B_\varepsilon(N)
=
\{n\le N:K_6(n)>\varepsilon n/\log n\}.
\]

Suppose the campaign gives

\[
|\mathcal B_\varepsilon(N)|\ll_\varepsilon(\log N)^2.
\tag{23}
\]

To contradict (23) from a single \(n_0\in\mathcal B_\varepsilon\), a useful rigidity theorem must produce **more than logarithmically many high rows**, not merely many individual inherited hits.

A sufficient statement would be something like:

> If \(K_6(n_0)\ge\varepsilon n_0/\log n_0\), then there exist at least \(n_0^\delta\) distinct rows \(m\in[c n_0,C n_0]\) with
> \[
> K_6(m)\ge\varepsilon' m/\log m,
> \]
> for constants \(\delta,\varepsilon',c,C>0\).

Even the much weaker conclusion

\[
\#\{m\asymp n_0:K_6(m)>\varepsilon' m/\log m\}
>C_\varepsilon(\log n_0)^2
\]

would suffice.

But note the combinatorial burden. Starting with \(M\) parent hits, a map that sends each hit to one child hit gives only \(M\) rows of degree one. To create bad child rows one needs **packets**: many parent witnesses must coalesce onto the same child row.

The most plausible abstract mechanism would be pair-based:

\[
(p_i,j_i),(p_k,j_k)
\longmapsto
\text{a child row carrying many derived witnesses}.
\]

A parent row has

\[
\binom M2
\]

prime pairs, which is enormous when \(M\asymp X/\log X\). If an Apéry-specific identity could map these pairs into only moderately many child parameters, with controlled fiber multiplicity and with each child parameter certifying many genuine q=6 hits, then a replication theorem might emerge.

But that would be a **cross-characteristic algebraic identity**. The ordinary second-order recurrence acts separately modulo each \(p_i\); it gives no relation between the states modulo \(p_i\) and \(p_k\). Any successful pair-to-child law would therefore be very close in spirit to the “carrier” route: it must encode many different characteristics in one characteristic-zero object or one common algebraic relation.

So paths (a) and (b) are not completely disjoint. A strong replication theorem would likely contain a hidden carrier/eliminant.

## 9. Why the present density theorem cannot be self-improved just from isolation

The logical shape is important.

An almost-all theorem such as (23) is consistent with

\[
\mathcal B_\varepsilon\cap[2^k,2^{k+1})
=\{n_k\}
\]

for one carefully placed bad row in every dyadic block. Then

\[
|\mathcal B_\varepsilon(N)|\asymp\log N,
\]

comfortably below \(O((\log N)^2)\).

Making the bad rows more isolated only makes this configuration **more compatible** with a codegree argument whose gain comes from pairs of bad rows.

This is the same generic phenomenon as in graph theory: a codegree theorem can forbid dense clusters of high-degree vertices while permitting one high-degree star center. To eliminate the center, one needs a degree bound, an energy bound that counts pairs of incident edges at the center, or a global structural constraint.

The current `hm3_result.tex` obstruction is exactly of this form. Its concluding scope remark says that even uniform \(O(1)\) row codegree does not imply the desired G2 power saving; the missing arithmetic must constrain, **across distinct primes**, which structurally permitted reflection orbit becomes the zero fiber.

## 11. Known zero-pattern rigidity for recurrences does not supply the missing clustering

I do not know any general theorem for holonomic/P-recursive zero sets that says

\[
\text{one exceptional zero event}\Longrightarrow
\text{many nearby exceptional zero events}.
\]

The classical results point the other way.

### 11.1 Skolem–Mahler–Lech

For a **constant-coefficient** linear recurrence over characteristic zero, the zero set is a finite union of full arithmetic progressions plus a finite set. This is very rigid for the infinite part, but the finite exceptional part is exactly the problem for an “isolated survivor”: the theorem does not force an isolated zero to replicate.

More importantly, our q=6 bad-row condition is not the zero set of one fixed constant-coefficient recurrence. It mixes different primes and different low indices \(j=n-6p\).

### 11.2 Nonconstant-coefficient / holonomic analogues

Work of Bézivin and Methfessel on recurrences with nonconstant coefficients gives, under broad hypotheses, a finite union of arithmetic progressions together with a **density-zero exceptional set**. That is qualitatively analogous to the present wall: density zero does not mean empty, and it permits isolated exceptional indices.

A convenient reference is:

- C. Methfessel, *On the zeros of recurrence sequences with non-constant coefficients*, Archiv der Mathematik **74** (2000), 201–206, DOI `10.1007/s000130050431`.

Bell–Burris–Yeats prove a full Skolem–Mahler–Lech conclusion for a reciprocal polynomial-recurrence class where the recurrence can be run backwards with a nonzero constant trailing coefficient:

- J. P. Bell, S. N. Burris, K. Yeats, *On the set of zero coefficients of a function satisfying a linear differential equation*, Math. Proc. Cambridge Philos. Soc. **153** (2012), 235–247, DOI `10.1017/S0305004112000114`.

That theorem is not an off-the-shelf theorem for the present mod-\(p\), varying-characteristic q=6 incidence problem; and even where a Skolem–Mahler–Lech conclusion applies to one fixed sequence, it does not couple zeros belonging to different primes.

### 11.3 Positive characteristic is less locally rigid

Harm Derksen's positive-characteristic analogue of Skolem–Mahler–Lech describes zero sets of linear recurrence sequences using finite automata:

- H. Derksen, *A Skolem–Mahler–Lech theorem in positive characteristic and finite automata*, Invent. Math. **168** (2007), 175–224, DOI `10.1007/s00222-006-0031-0`.

Positive characteristic admits sparse Frobenius-type zero patterns that are impossible in the characteristic-zero SML theorem. Automatic/Frobenius structure is global digital structure, not a nearby-clustering statement.

Again the application here would be indirect at best: for each prime \(p\) we have a different characteristic and a different finite-field recurrence problem. There is no single automaton uniform in \(p\) whose zero set is the set of bad rows \(n\).

### 11.4 p-adic analytic zero isolation

Strassmann-type arguments and p-adic interpolation can show that a **single p-adic analytic function** cannot have too many zeros in one disk unless extra degeneracy occurs. They are powerful same-characteristic rigidity tools.

But a large \(K_6(n_0)\) gives one zero in each of many different residue characteristics. There is no p-adic accumulation in one field. To use p-adic analyticity across these witnesses one would first need a common characteristic-zero interpolation/carrier — again returning to path (a).

### Bottom line from the literature

The known recurrence-zero rigidity mechanisms organize many zeros of **one fixed recurrence/field**. They do not turn a rainbow event consisting of one zero in each of many different characteristics into nearby high-load rows.

## 12. The one rigidity mechanism that *would* matter: cross-characteristic coalescence

The missing statement is not ordinary “zero propagation.” It is something like:

\[
\boxed{
\text{many }p\text{ satisfying }p\mid b_{n_0-6p}
\Longrightarrow
\text{many algebraically linked }(p,q)\text{ pairs}.}
\]

Then one would need the pair relation to have a second interpretation that creates either

- a small characteristic-zero divisor/carrier, or
- many new rows of large q=6 load.

Notice that the parent row already supplies the linear relation

\[
(n_0-6p)-(n_0-6q)=6(q-p).
\tag{24}
\]

Thus every pair of parent hits is a cross-characteristic zero-pair on one fixed slope. This is exactly the object measured by the localized q=6 pair energy. The issue is not producing pair relations — a large row produces \(M(M-1)\) of them automatically. The issue is proving that the actual Apéry zero sets cannot support so many such relations at one common intercept.

That is the cleanest remaining analytic rigidity question.

A theorem bounding those cross-characteristic slope-6 pairs gives a direct contradiction to the isolated star. A theorem merely bounding overlaps between *different rows* does not.

## 13. Answer to the question as posed

### Can \(K_6(n_0)>\varepsilon n_0/\log n_0\) force \(K_6(n_0\pm1)\) large?

**No known mechanism does this, and the same-prime mechanism proves the opposite.** Every inherited prime disappears at \(n_0\pm1\). Any large neighboring value would have to be rebuilt from essentially new prime labels.

### Can a large \(K_6(n_0)\) force many nearby large values?

Not from reflection, Lucas reuse, the local recurrence, cardinality bounds, gap-polynomial constraints, or codegree bounds. Reflection sends the \(M\) hits injectively to \(M\) different child rows; fixed-quotient Lucas reuse gives no new common row; codegree only says different high rows would use different prime labels.

### Does small codegree imply nearby \(K_6\) is small?

**No.** It implies only that nearby large rows, if they exist, have mostly disjoint witness sets. The packing inequality (6) can bound how many such rows coexist in a short interval, typically by an \(O_\varepsilon(1)\) number, but it does not rule out one.

### Can isolated-but-large itself be contradicted?

Only after adding a new ingredient that sees the star center. The clean options are:

1. **carrier height** — compress its \(M\) prime witnesses into one short integer/algebraic object;
2. **localized q=6 factorial energy** — the center contributes \((M)_2\) by itself;
3. **cross-characteristic replication/coalescence** — convert the \(M\) witnesses or their \(M^2\) pairs into more than \(O((\log N)^2)\) other high rows.

The current local rigidity results do not provide (3).

## 14. Sharp research target if one insists on the self-improving lane

I would formulate the missing theorem in a way that makes the required coalescence explicit rather than asking for neighbor propagation:

Without a statement of this type, the anchored-star obstruction survives.

is already exactly a **rainbow-star instability theorem in averaged form**: it says no single star of linear prime degree can exist. It is therefore substantially more aligned with the remaining obstruction than trying to prove \(K_6(n_0\pm1)\) is large.

## Final conclusion

The self-improving-neighbor idea does not survive the exact recurrence audit.

A q=6 hit at \(n_0\):

\[
\text{dies at }n_0\pm1,
\]

and its only unconditional nontrivial zero symmetry gives

\[
n_0\longmapsto13p-1-n_0,
\]

which depends injectively on \(p\). Thus the natural dynamics **decohere** the parent pileup rather than replicate it.

Small codegree reinforces this decoherence. It can prove that large rows cannot cluster densely, but that is precisely why it cannot eliminate one isolated large row. The campaign's own anchored-star model formally demonstrates this, even under uniform constant codegree.

So there is no contradiction from “isolated but large” using the current rigidity package. To cross the all-index wall, one still needs an ingredient that sees many different characteristics simultaneously:

\[
\boxed{\text{short carrier}\quad\text{or}\quad
\text{cross-characteristic pair-energy/coalescence}.}
\]

A holonomic zero-pattern theorem forcing nearby exceptional rows is not presently available, and the standard recurrence-zero literature does not have the needed shape.
