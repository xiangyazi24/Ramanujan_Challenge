# Apéry hypergeometric interpolation: exact inhomogeneous recurrence and the connection-scalar obstruction

## Executive verdict

Let

\[
P(x)=34x^3+51x^2+27x+5,
\qquad
(\mathcal Ly)(x)=(x+1)^3y(x+1)-P(x)y(x)+x^3y(x-1),
\]

and

\[
B(x)={}_4F_3\!\left(
\begin{matrix}-x,-x,x+1,x+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};1\right)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T_k(x),
\]

where

\[
T_k(x)=\frac{(-x)_k^2(x+1)_k^2}{(k!)^4}.
\]

The proposed identity is **exactly true** for every complex \(x\):

\[
\boxed{
(\mathcal LB)(x)
=8(2x+1)\left(\frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi}\right)^2.
}
\tag{A}
\]

An explicit Gosper certificate is

\[
R_k(x)=
\frac{4(2x+1)k^4\bigl(2k^2-3k-4x(x+1)\bigr)}
{(k-x-1)^2(k+x)^2},
\qquad k\ge1,
\tag{B}
\]

with \(R_0T_0:=0\). The apparent poles cancel against the last factors of the Pochhammer symbols. Equivalently, set

\[
G_0(x)=0,
\]

and, for \(k\ge1\),

\[
G_k(x)=4(2x+1)\bigl(2k^2-3k-4x(x+1)\bigr)T_{k-1}(x).
\tag{C}
\]

Then the termwise creative-telescoping identity is

\[
\boxed{
(x+1)^3T_k(x+1)-P(x)T_k(x)+x^3T_k(x-1)
=G_{k+1}(x)-G_k(x).
}
\tag{D}
\]

Summing (D) and evaluating the nonzero boundary at \(k=\infty\) proves (A).

However, (A) does **not by itself force**

\[
c=(\log\phi)''(0)=-\frac{2\pi^2}{3}.
\]

The reason is structural, not merely technical: \(B\) is an inhomogeneous interpolation of the integer Apéry solution, whereas \(\phi\) is the dominant connection coefficient of the homogeneous shifted recurrence used in the limiting-cell construction. They cannot be identified without a separate connection theorem. Indeed,

\[
B(0)=1,\qquad B'(0)=0,\qquad
(\log B)''(0)=B''(0)=\frac{\pi^2}{3},
\tag{E}
\]

which has the wrong sign and magnitude. Even multiplication by an exponential gauge \(e^{az}\) does not alter this second logarithmic derivative.

The sharpest direct missing lemma is the gauge-invariant two-jet statement

\[
\boxed{
\phi(z)\phi(-z)
=1-\frac{2\pi^2}{3}z^2+O(z^4).
}
\tag{F}
\]

A stronger, very natural version is

\[
\boxed{
\phi(z)\phi(-z)
=\left(\frac{\sin \pi z}{\pi z}\right)^4
}
\tag{G}
\]

near \(z=0\), or equivalently

\[
\phi(z)=e^{az}\left(\frac{\sin \pi z}{\pi z}\right)^2
\]

for some exponential gauge constant \(a\). Either (F) or (G) gives
\(c=-2\pi^2/3\) immediately, but neither follows from (A) alone.

Below I also derive an exact equivalent scalar obstruction in the current repository normalization of the shifted solution \(F_n(z)\). Under the expected derivative-level dominant asymptotic for \(B\), the desired value of \(c\) is equivalent to one explicit positive Apéry series identity:

\[
\boxed{
16\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(2j+1)b_j}
{(n+1)^3b_nb_{n+1}}
=\frac{\pi^2}{3}.
}
\tag{H}
\]

No numerical fitting is used anywhere in the proof or reduction.

## 1. Convergence and normalization of the hypergeometric series

For fixed \(k\), \(T_k(x)\) is a polynomial in \(x\). For large \(k\), write

\[
T_k(x)
=
\left(
\frac{\Gamma(k-x)\Gamma(k+x+1)}
{\Gamma(k+1)^2\Gamma(-x)\Gamma(x+1)}
\right)^2.
\tag{1.1}
\]

Uniform gamma-ratio asymptotics on compact subsets of the \(x\)-plane give

\[
T_k(x)=O_K(k^{-2})
\]

for \(x\) in any fixed compact set \(K\). Thus the series for \(B\), and the corresponding series after replacing \(x\) by \(x\pm1\), converge normally on compact sets. In particular \(B\) is entire and termwise application of \(\mathcal L\) is justified.

For a nonnegative integer \(n\), the factor \((-n)_k\) makes the series terminate at \(k=n\), and

\[
T_k(n)
=\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
B(n)=b_n.
\tag{1.2}
\]

Also the parameters are merely exchanged under \(x\mapsto-x-1\), so

\[
B(-x-1)=B(x).
\tag{1.3}
\]

Equation (1.2) explains why the inhomogeneous right side in (A) is invisible on the integer lattice: it vanishes whenever \(x\in\mathbb Z\).

## 2. The explicit Gosper certificate

The two shift ratios in the external variable are

\[
\frac{T_k(x+1)}{T_k(x)}
=\left(\frac{x+k+1}{x-k+1}\right)^2,
\tag{2.1}
\]

and

\[
\frac{T_k(x-1)}{T_k(x)}
=\left(\frac{x-k}{x+k}\right)^2.
\tag{2.2}
\]

The ratio in the summation index is

\[
\frac{T_k(x)}{T_{k-1}(x)}
=\frac{(k-x-1)^2(k+x)^2}{k^4},
\qquad k\ge1.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Define

\[
C_k(x)=4(2x+1)\bigl(2k^2-3k-4x(x+1)\bigr).
\tag{2.4}
\]

Then \(G_k=C_kT_{k-1}\) for \(k\ge1\). Dividing (D) by \(T_k\), the desired identity becomes

\[
\begin{aligned}
&(x+1)^3\left(\frac{x+k+1}{x-k+1}\right)^2
-P(x)
+x^3\left(\frac{x-k}{x+k}\right)^2\\
&\qquad =
C_{k+1}(x)
-C_k(x)\frac{k^4}{(k-x-1)^2(k+x)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5}
\]

This is an elementary rational identity. After clearing

\[
(k-x-1)^2(k+x)^2,
\]

both sides are the same polynomial in \(k,x\). A compact machine-readable verification line is

```text
Together[
 (x+1)^3 ((x+k+1)/(x-k+1))^2
 -(34 x^3+51 x^2+27 x+5)
 +x^3 ((x-k)/(x+k))^2
 -C[k+1]
 +C[k] k^4/((k-x-1)^2 (k+x)^2)
] = 0,

C[j] = 4 (2x+1) (2j^2-3j-4x(x+1)).
```

The rational certificate (B) is the same statement because

\[
R_k(x)T_k(x)=C_k(x)T_{k-1}(x)=G_k(x).
\tag{2.6}
\]

Thus all apparent certificate poles are removable. This also proves (D) at the exceptional values where one of the displayed shift ratios has a zero denominator: clear the denominators first, or use the entire form (C).

For \(k=0\), one has \(G_0=0\) and

\[
G_1=-4(2x+1)^3,
\]

while

\[
(x+1)^3-P(x)+x^3=-4(2x+1)^3,
\]

so the initial term is included without exception.

## 3. The boundary at infinity

Let

\[
B_N(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}T_k(x).
\]

Summing (D) from \(k=0\) to \(N\) gives the exact finite identity

\[
(\mathcal LB_N)(x)=G_{N+1}(x).
\tag{3.1}
\]

From (1.1) and the gamma-ratio asymptotic,

\[
N^2T_N(x)
\longrightarrow
\frac{1}{\Gamma(-x)^2\Gamma(x+1)^2}
\tag{3.2}
\]

locally uniformly in \(x\). Euler reflection gives

\[
\Gamma(-x)\Gamma(x+1)
=-\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi x},
\]

and hence

\[
N^2T_N(x)
\longrightarrow
\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi}\right)^2.
\tag{3.3}
\]

On the other hand,

\[
C_{N+1}(x)
=8(2x+1)N^2+O_x(N).
\tag{3.4}
\]

Therefore

\[
G_{N+1}(x)
=C_{N+1}(x)T_N(x)
\longrightarrow
8(2x+1)\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi}\right)^2.
\tag{3.5}
\]

Letting \(N\to\infty\) in (3.1), using normal convergence, proves (A):

\[
\boxed{
(x+1)^3B(x+1)-P(x)B(x)+x^3B(x-1)
=8(2x+1)\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi}\right)^2.
}
\]

The nonzero right side is precisely the boundary term of the Gosper telescoping. For integer \(x=n\ge0\), the hypergeometric series terminates before that boundary is reached and the sine factor vanishes, recovering the ordinary homogeneous Apéry recurrence for \(b_n\).

## 4. Why \(B\) is not the limiting connection function \(\phi\)

The quickest normalization check is at the origin. For \(k\ge1\),

\[
(-x)_k=-x\,(k-1)!+O(x^2),
\qquad
(x+1)_k=k!+O(x),
\]

so

\[
T_k(x)=\frac{x^2}{k^2}+O_k(x^3).
\]

Termwise differentiation, justified by normal convergence, yields

\[
B(x)=1+\zeta(2)x^2+O(x^3).
\tag{4.1}
\]

Consequently

\[
B'(0)=0,
\qquad
B''(0)=2\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{3},
\tag{4.2}
\]

and

\[
(\log B)''(0)=\frac{\pi^2}{3}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Thus the assignment \(\phi=B\) would give \(+\pi^2/3\), not \(-2\pi^2/3\). The same is true for \(\phi=e^{az}B\), because an exponential gauge changes only the first logarithmic derivative.

There is no contradiction with the conjectured value of \(c\): the objects are different.

- \(B(x)\) is an entire interpolation satisfying the inhomogeneous equation (A).
- \(\phi(z)\) is the dominant connection coefficient for the homogeneous shifted recurrence.
- Agreement at integer points, \(B(n)=b_n\), does not identify their off-lattice jets.

The inhomogeneous forcing begins at second order at every integer, because

\[
\left(\frac{\sin\pi(n+z)}{\pi}\right)^2
=z^2+O(z^4).
\]

This explains why the zeroth and first jets can agree with a homogeneous shifted solution while the second jet acquires a genuine correction.

## 5. Exact jet comparison with the repository shifted solution

W_n=\partial_zF_n(0),

### 5.1 The first jet agrees exactly with \(B'(n)\)

Put

\[
Y_n(z)=B(n+z).
\]

Identity (A) becomes

\[
\begin{aligned}
&(n+z+1)^3Y_{n+1}(z)-P(n+z)Y_n(z)+(n+z)^3Y_{n-1}(z)\\
&\qquad =8(2n+2z+1)\left(\frac{\sin\pi z}{\pi}\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.7}
\]

The right side and its first derivative vanish at \(z=0\). Hence \(B'(n)\) satisfies exactly the same differentiated recurrence as \(W_n\).

The initial values also agree:

\[
B'(0)=0=W_0.
\]

At \(x=1\), all terms with \(k\ge2\) have a double zero because of \((-x)_k^2\); only \(k=1\) contributes to the first derivative, and

\[
B'(1)=\left.\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(x^2(x+1)^2\bigr)\right|_{x=1}=12.
\]

Also

\[
W_1=\left.\frac{d}{dz}\frac{P(z)}{(z+1)^3}\right|_{z=0}=12.
\]

Uniqueness for the second-order recurrence gives the exact identity

\[
\boxed{W_n=B'(n)\quad(n\ge0).}
\tag{5.8}
\]

\[
E_n=B''(n)-V_n.
\tag{5.9}

\[
8(2n+2z+1)\left(\frac{\sin\pi z}{\pi}\right)^2

The initial values are explicit. Equation (4.2) gives

\[
E_0=\frac{\pi^2}{3}.
\tag{5.11}

For \(B''(1)\), the \(k=1\) term contributes \(26\). For \(k\ge2\),

## 6. Exact variation-of-constants formula

Let

\[
S_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(2j+1)b_j.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Define the mixed Casoratian

\[
Q_{n+1}=U_{n+1}b_n-U_nb_{n+1}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Subtracting the homogeneous recurrence for \(b_n\) from (5.16) gives

\[
(n+1)^3Q_{n+1}
=n^3Q_n+16(2n+1)b_n.
\tag{6.3}
\]

Since \(Q_1=16\), induction yields

\[
\boxed{
Q_{n+1}=\frac{16S_n}{(n+1)^3}.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

Consequently

\[
\frac{U_{n+1}}{b_{n+1}}-rac{U_n}{b_n}
=rac{16S_n}{(n+1)^3b_nb_{n+1}}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

The right side is positive and exponentially summable because
\(b_n\asymp(17+12\sqrt2)^n n^{-3/2}\). Thus

\[
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{U_n}{b_n}
=16\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{S_n}{(n+1)^3b_nb_{n+1}}.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

This formula is exact and unconditional.

## 7. The exact scalar stopping point

From (5.6), (5.8), and (5.9),

\[
\begin{aligned}
c
&=(\log\phi)''(0)\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}
\left(
\frac{V_n}{b_n}-\left(\frac{W_n}{b_n}\right)^2
\right)\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}
\left(
\frac{B''(n)}{B(n)}-\left(\frac{B'(n)}{B(n)}\right)^2
\right)
-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{E_n}{b_n}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Therefore, provided the displayed limits exist,

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
c
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log B)''(n)
-\frac{\pi^2}{3}\\
&\quad
-16\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(2j+1)b_j}
{(n+1)^3b_nb_{n+1}}.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

This is the exact implication supplied by the inhomogeneous identity.

A derivative-level dominant asymptotic of the form

\[
B(x)=K\lambda^x x^{-3/2}\bigl(1+O(x^{-1})\bigr),
\qquad
\lambda=17+12\sqrt2,
\tag{7.3}
\]

uniform with two \(x\)-derivatives in a sector containing the positive real axis, would imply

\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log B)''(n)=0.
\tag{7.4}
\]

Under (7.4), equation (7.2) shows that

\[
c=-\frac{2\pi^2}{3}
\]

is **equivalent** to the positive series evaluation

\[
16\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(2j+1)b_j}
{(n+1)^3b_nb_{n+1}}
=\frac{\pi^2}{3}.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Thus the hypergeometric telescoping has reduced the desired connection scalar to two precise analytic inputs:

1. **[B-DOM-JET]** Prove the two-derivative dominant asymptotic (7.3), or at least (7.4).
2. **[APERY-PI2-CONNECTION]** Prove the exact series identity (7.5).

Neither input is a consequence of the finite telescoping algebra alone. The second is the genuinely scalar connection evaluation.

An equivalent, cleaner statement directly in the limiting-cell language is **[PHI-PRODUCT-2JET]**, equation (F). This is the weakest lemma needed merely to determine \(c\), and it is invariant under the unavoidable exponential gauge of \(\phi\).
