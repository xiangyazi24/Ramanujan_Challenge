# One-zero integer detectors for Apéry numbers: radical minimality, a transport obstruction, and geometric object mismatch

## Verdict

The proposed implication from Q8746 is **not valid as stated**.  A small detector attached separately to each index \(r\) does not bound a moving-modulus row, because different hit primes use different indices \(r=nmod p\).  In fact there are artificial integer sequences for which every index has a detector with

\[
\log |G_r|=O(\log r)=r^{o(1)},
\]

while one row has \(\asymp n/\log n\) primes \(p>\sqrt n\) satisfying \(p\mid u_{nmod p}\).  Thus properties (1) and (2) alone cannot prove the all-\(n\) Apéry bound.

For a fixed Apéry index, there is also an exact minimality theorem.  Put

\[
R_r^+:=\prod_{\substack{p>r\\p\mid B_r}}p.
\]

This is the weakest radical relevant to the moving-modulus problem, since every hit has \(r=nmod p<p\).  Every nonzero integer detector satisfying

\[
p>r,\quad p\mid B_r\Longrightarrow p\mid G_r
\]

must satisfy

\[
\boxed{R_r^+\mid G_r.}
\]

Hence resultants, norms, discriminants, Padé determinants, or constant-term constructions cannot have height below the large-prime radical they are supposed to detect.  Constructing arbitrary \(G_r\) of height \(\exp(r^{o(1)})\) is exactly the unproved assertion

\[
\log R_r^+=r^{o(1)};
\]

it is not supplied by a formal elimination construction.  Even that assertion would still not prove the all-\(n\) theorem without an additional **row-collapsing or congruence-transport property**.

The correct strengthened target is a **congruence-preserving zero detector**.  One concrete, very strong version would be a fixed nonzero polynomial \(F\in\mathbb Z[x]\) such that

\[
\boxed{
 p>r,\ p\mid B_r\Longrightarrow p\mid F(r)
}
\tag{CPZD-polynomial}
\]

outside finitely many primes.  Then \(n\equiv r\pmod p\) gives \(p\mid F(n)\), so all row hits divide the *single* integer \(F(n)\), yielding \(O_F(1)\) hits.  No such polynomial, fixed norm, or equivalent fixed divisor is currently known.

The seven proposed routes divide into two exact classes:

1. **They return \(B_r\), its radical, or forced radical content.**  This includes direct coefficient resultants and recurrence-only elimination.
2. **They detect a different locus.**  K3 discriminants detect bad reduction; Hecke polynomials detect a special moving central coefficient; Hasse-polynomial roots are evaluation roots rather than coefficient zeros; point products detect a vanishing factor rather than additive cancellation.

Accordingly, this report gives a scoped no-go and a strictly narrower residual, not a construction of the requested \(G_r\).

## 1. The first correction: pointwise detectors do not imply a pointwise moving-modulus bound

Let an arbitrary integer sequence \(U_r\) have nonzero detectors \(G_r\) satisfying

\[
p\mid U_r\Longrightarrow p\mid G_r.
\tag{1.1}
\]

The inference suggested in the question would replace the moving family \(G_{nmod p}\) by one integer.  That replacement is invalid: the index in \(G_{nmod p}\) depends on the prime being counted.

The correct elementary inequality is only

\[
\#\{p>\sqrt n:p\mid U_{nmod p}\}
\le
\sum_{0\le r<n/2}\omega_{>\sqrt n}(G_r),
\tag{1.2}
\]

where \(\omega_{>y}(m)\) counts distinct prime divisors of \(m\) exceeding \(y\).  Since

\[
\omega_{>\sqrt n}(G_r)\le {2\log^+|G_r|\over\log n},
\]

one gets at best

\[
\#\{p>\sqrt n:p\mid U_{nmod p}\}
\le {2\over\log n}
\sum_{r<n/2}\log^+|G_r|.
\tag{1.3}
\]

A pointwise estimate \(\log|G_r|=r^{o(1)}\) gives only

\[
{n^{1+o(1)}\over\log n},
\]

not \(o(n/\log n)\).

### Proposition 1.1 — exact moving-index counterexample

There are positive integer sequences \(U_r,G_r\) such that

\[
p\mid U_r\Longrightarrow p\mid G_r
\quad\text{for every prime }p,
\]

and

\[
\log G_r=O(\log r),
\]

but for infinitely many \(n\),

\[
\#\{p>\sqrt n:p\mid U_{nmod p}\}
\sim {n\over12\log n}.
\tag{1.4}
\]

#### Proof

Choose integers \(N_1<N_2<\cdots\) with \(N_{j+1}>4N_j\).  For every prime

\[
{2N_j\over3}<p\le {3N_j\over4},
\]

put

\[
r=N_j-p.
\]

Then

\[
{N_j\over4}\le r<{N_j\over3}.
\]

The intervals \([N_j/4,N_j/3)\) are disjoint.  Define

\[
U_r=G_r=p
\]

at each of these marked indices, and \(U_r=G_r=1\) elsewhere.  Thus (1.1) holds, and at a marked index \(p\asymp r\), so \(\log G_r=O(\log r)\).

Take \(n=N_j\).  Every selected prime exceeds \(n/2\), hence

\[
nmod p=n-p=r,
\]

and therefore \(p\mid U_{nmod p}\).  The prime number theorem gives

\[
\pi(3n/4)-\pi(2n/3)
\sim\left({3\over4}-{2\over3}\right){n\over\log n}
={n\over12\log n}.
\]

This proves (1.4).  \(\square\)

This is not an Apéry counterexample.  It is a counterexample to the *logical implication from the two proposed detector axioms*.  Any Apéry proof based on \(G_r\) needs an additional property coupling the detectors at congruent indices.

### Terminology warning

The condition

\[
\log|G_r|=r^{o(1)}
\]

is much stronger than ordinary subexponential growth.  It means

\[
G_r=\exp(r^{o(1)}),
\]

whereas ordinary subexponential growth is only \(\log|G_r|=o(r)\).  Proposition 1.1 shows that even the still stronger bound \(\log|G_r|=O(\log r)\) does not repair the moving-index problem.

## 2. Exact minimality of a one-index detector

For \(Y\ge0\), define

\[
R_r(Y)=\prod_{\substack{p>Y\\p\mid B_r}}p.
\]

### Proposition 2.1 — radical minimality

For a nonzero integer \(G_r\), the following are equivalent:

1. every prime \(p>Y\) dividing \(B_r\) divides \(G_r\);
2. \(R_r(Y)\mid G_r\).

Consequently, \(R_r(Y)\) is the smallest positive detector, and every detector has the exact height lower bound

\[
\boxed{
\log|G_r|\ge
\sum_{\substack{p>Y\\p\mid B_r}}\log p.
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

#### Proof

The primes occurring in \(R_r(Y)\) are distinct.  Condition 1 says each one divides \(G_r\); unique factorization therefore gives their product as a divisor.  The converse is immediate.  \(\square\)

For the all-\(n\) application the optimal cutoff is \(Y=r\), because \(r=nmod p<p\).  Hence

\[
R_r^+=R_r(r)
\]

is the exact minimal one-zero detector relevant to the campaign.

### Consequence 2.2

The existence of arbitrary detectors satisfying

\[
\log|G_r|=r^{o(1)}
\]

is equivalent to

\[
oxed{\log R_r^+=r^{o(1)}.}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Thus a resultant or norm cannot *prove* small height merely by changing the presentation: if it has the required divisibility property, Proposition 2.1 forces it to contain \(R_r^+\).

The known general arithmetic theorem of Luca and Shparlinski gives only density-one lower bounds

\[
\omega(B_r)>c\log\log\log r,
\qquad
P(B_r)>c(\log r\log\log r)^{1/2},
\]

which neither prove nor refute (2.2).  In particular, no presently banked theorem shows that the large-prime radical has linear logarithmic height, even though that is the natural heuristic.

Most importantly, (2.2) alone still does not imply the row bound, by Proposition 1.1.

## 3. What would actually suffice: a row carrier or congruence transport

### Theorem 3.1 — exact row-carrier criterion

Suppose that for every \(n\) there is a nonzero integer \(H_n\) such that, outside finitely many primes,

\[
p>\sqrt n,\quad p\mid B_{nmod p}
\Longrightarrow p\mid H_n,
\tag{3.1}
\]

and

\[
\log|H_n|=o(n).
\tag{3.2}
\]

Then

\[
\#\{p>\sqrt n:p\mid B_{nmod p}\}
=o(n/\log n).
\]

#### Proof

Let \(S_n\) be the set of hit primes.  Since the primes are distinct and all divide \(H_n\),

\[
(\sqrt n)^{|S_n|}
<\prod_{p\in S_n}p
\le |H_n|.
\]

Therefore

\[
|S_n|\le {2\log|H_n|\over\log n}=o(n/\log n).
\quad\square
\]

This is the form that the all-\(n\) problem really needs: all moving hits must collapse into one small row-dependent integer.

### Definition 3.2 — congruence-preserving zero detector

A sequence of nonzero integers \(G_m\) is a **CPZD** if, outside finitely many primes,

\[
p>r,\ p\mid B_r\Longrightarrow p\mid G_r,
\tag{3.3}
\]

and

\[
m\equiv r\pmod p,\quad p\mid G_r
\Longrightarrow p\mid G_m.
\tag{3.4}
\]

If additionally \(\log|G_m|=o(m)\), then Theorem 3.1 applies with \(H_n=G_n\).

The missing ingredient in Q8746 is precisely (3.4).

### Corollary 3.3 — a fixed-polynomial detector would be decisive

Assume there are \(F\in\mathbb Z[x]\setminus\{0\}\) and \(P_0\) such that

\[
p>\max(r,P_0),\quad p\mid B_r
\Longrightarrow p\mid F(r).
\tag{3.5}
\]

Then, for every hit \(p>\sqrt n\) with \(r=nmod p\),

\[
F(n)\equiv F(r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Except at the finitely many integer roots of \(F\), all hit primes divide \(F(n)\).  Hence

\[
\#\{p>\sqrt n:p\mid B_{nmod p}\}
\le {2\log|F(n)|\over\log n}
=2\deg F+o(1).
\tag{3.6}
\]

Equivalently, a sufficient fixed-divisor theorem is

\[
\boxed{
R_r^+\mid F(r)\qquad(r\ge0).
}
\tag{3.7}
\]

This is a legitimate and extremely strong research target.  It is not known, and none of the K3, Hasse-square, recurrence, or Padé identities currently gives it.

A norm of a **fixed** algebraic function is of the same type.  If \(K/\mathbb Q\) is fixed and \(\alpha(t)\in K(t)\), clearing a fixed denominator turns

\[
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha(r))
\]

into the value of a fixed polynomial (or a quotient of two fixed polynomials) at \(r\).  Thus the norm route can close the problem only by proving a statement equivalent to (3.5).

## 4. Resultants: what they detect and the exact saturation theorem

There is a preliminary indexing correction.  The Apéry sequence satisfies a **second-order P-recursive recurrence** (three adjacent terms), while its generating function is annihilated by a **third-order differential operator**.  The recurrence state has dimension two.

### 4.1 The interpolation resultant is tautological

For a fixed prime \(p\), one can interpolate a polynomial

\[
P_p(X)\in\mathbb F_p[X],\qquad \deg P_p\le p-1,
\]

with

\[
P_p(d)=B_d\quad(0\le d<p).
\]

Then

\[
\operatorname{Res}_X(P_p(X),X-r)=P_p(r)=B_r\pmod p.
\]

This is only a rewriting of the original value.  The polynomial \(P_p\) itself depends on \(p\), has potentially degree \(p-1\), and contains the whole characteristic-\(p\) row.

A higher resultant \(\operatorname{Res}(P_p,Q_r)\) detects a common *evaluation root* of two polynomials.  The desired condition is the vanishing of one prescribed coefficient/value \(B_r\).  If \(Q_r=X-r\), the resultant is again \(B_r\); if \(Q_r\) does not isolate \(r\), its vanishing is a different condition.

Proposition 2.1 supplies a universal height audit: any integer lift \(D_r\) of any resultant that truly satisfies

\[
p>r,\ p\mid B_r\Longrightarrow p\mid D_r
\]

must have \(R_r^+\mid D_r\).  No determinant presentation evades this lower bound.

### 4.2 Exact recurrence-elimination saturation

Put

\[
F_m=(m!)^3B_m.
\]

Then

\[
F_{m+1}=P(m)F_m-m^6F_{m-1},
\qquad
P(m)=34m^3+51m^2+27m+5.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Let \(R_m(X,Y)\in\mathbb Z[X,Y]\) be the universal solution:

\[
R_0=X,\qquad R_1=Y,\qquad
R_{m+1}=P(m)R_m-m^6R_{m-1}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Every \(R_m\) is an integral homogeneous linear form in \(X,Y\).

### Proposition 4.1 — one-index packet saturation

Fix \(r\), and let \(p_1,\ldots,p_s>r\) be distinct primes.  In \(\mathbb Z[X,Y]\), put

\[
I_i=(p_i,R_r(X,Y)).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^s I_i\right)\cap\mathbb Z
=\left(\prod_{i=1}^s p_i\right).
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Moreover,

\[
\boxed{
\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^s I_i:\left(\prod_i p_i\right)^\infty\right)
=\mathbb Z[X,Y].
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

#### Proof

For \(i\ne j\), Bézout gives integers \(a,b\) with \(ap_i+bp_j=1\), so \(I_i+I_j\) is the unit ideal.  Therefore

\[
\bigcap_i I_i=\prod_i I_i.
\]

Evaluate at \(X=Y=0\).  Since \(R_r(0,0)=0\), every product generator containing an \(R_r\) vanishes; the only surviving scalar generator is \(\prod_i p_i\).  Thus every constant in the product ideal is divisible by \(\prod_i p_i\), and that product itself belongs to the ideal.  This proves (4.3).  Since \(\prod_i p_i\) belongs to the intersection, saturation by it gives the whole ring, proving (4.4).  \(\square\)

Because \(p_i>r\), the factorial \((r!)^3\) is a \(p_i\)-unit, so \(p_i\mid F_r\) is equivalent to \(p_i\mid B_r\).  Proposition 4.1 says that recurrence transfer, polynomial Bézout identities, Sylvester determinants, and subresultants have exactly the forced prime product as scalar content.  Once that product is divided out, no primitive scalar remains.

This does not rule out an identity special to the distinguished binomial-sum solution.  It rules out obtaining the desired compression from the recurrence module alone.

## 5. K3 surfaces and norms: the coefficient/parameter mismatch

The generating function

\[
\mathcal B(t)=\sum_{m\ge0}B_mt^m
\]

is annihilated by the Picard--Fuchs operator

\[
\mathcal L=
\theta^3
-t(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+t^2(\theta+1)^3,
\qquad \theta=t{d\over dt}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Beukers and Peters realize this differential equation through a one-parameter family of K3 surfaces.  This does **not** identify the coefficient index \(r\) with the geometric fibre parameter \(t=r\).

There are three distinct objects:

1. a fixed K3 fibre \(X_t\), whose full \(H^2\) has rank \(22\) and gives an \(\ell\)-adic representation after a fibre is chosen;
2. the rank-three period local system governed by (5.1);
3. the Taylor coefficient \(B_r=[t^r]\mathcal B(t)\).

The statement

\[
B_r\bmod p=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_p\mid H^2(X_r))mod p
\]

is not an accepted identity and conflates the third object with the first.  A family gives an \(\ell\)-adic sheaf over the parameter line, not one fixed \(\mathrm{GL}_{22}\) representation simultaneously encoding all coefficient indices.

### 5.1 Exact coefficient/evaluation separation

For each prime \(p\), set

\[
A_p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}B_jt^j.
\]

For \(1\le j\le p-2\), multiplicative orthogonality gives the exact finite-Mellin identity

\[
\boxed{
B_j=-\sum_{t\in\mathbb F_p^\times}A_p(t)t^{-j}.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Thus

\[
Z_p=\{j:[t^j]A_p(t)=0\}
\]

is a coefficient/Mellin-zero set.  It is not the evaluation-root set of \(A_p\), its square root, or a discriminant polynomial.

The separation is already visible at \(p=7\):

\[
A_7(t)=1+5t+3t^2+3t^3+3t^4+5t^5+t^6
=(t-1)^2(t^2+1)^2\pmod7.
\]

It has evaluation roots, but none of its coefficients vanish; hence \(Z_7=\varnothing\).

The proved factorization of the truncated Apéry series has the form

\[
A_p(t)=\bigl(t^2-34t+1\bigr)^{\varepsilon_p}Q_p(t)^2,
\qquad \deg Q_p=\Theta(p).
\tag{5.3}
\]

The project’s exact triangular-freedom proposition shows that square factorization plus reciprocity permits arbitrary zero patterns in the first half of the coefficient vector (with their reflected copies).  Therefore (5.3) alone cannot produce a coefficient detector.

### 5.2 What a bounded-degree norm would really assert

Let \(K/\mathbb Q\) be fixed and \(\alpha(t)\in K(t)\).  After clearing a fixed denominator,

\[
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha(t))={F(t)\over D(t)}
\]

with fixed \(F,D\in\mathbb Z[t]\).  For primes away from the fixed denominator and discriminant, the condition

\[
p\mid N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha(r))
\]

means that the specialization of a fixed algebraic divisor meets \(rmod p\).  To make it a detector one must prove

\[
Z_p\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb F_p:F(x)=0\}
\tag{5.4}
\]

for every large \(p\).  This is exactly the fixed-polynomial CPZD theorem (3.5), not a consequence of the existence of the K3 family.

No natural fixed \(K,\alpha\) satisfying (5.4) is known.  The K3/Hasse structures presently control fibrewise evaluation data; (5.2) shows that the target is a Mellin coefficient cancellation.

## 6. Discriminant and conductor: exact degree and an explicit counterexample

Converting (5.1) to ordinary derivatives, the coefficient of \(d^3/dt^3\) is

\[
t^3(1-34t+t^2).
\]

Therefore the **reduced finite singular support** of the Picard--Fuchs equation is

\[
D_{\mathrm{PF}}(t)=t(t^2-34t+1),
\tag{6.1}
\]

of degree \(3\); the nonzero quadratic factor has degree \(2\).  A discriminant of a particular K3 model or elliptic fibration may contain powers of these factors and is model-dependent, so there is no single model-independent multiplicity or degree beyond this reduced support.

Its value has excellent height:

\[
\log|D_{\mathrm{PF}}(r)|=O(\log r).
\]

But it detects the wrong event.  Already

\[
B_2=73,
\]

whereas

\[
D_{\mathrm{PF}}(2)=2(4-68+1)=-126,
\]

so

\[
73\nmid D_{\mathrm{PF}}(2).
\]

Likewise

\[
B_3=1445=5\cdot17^2,
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{PF}}(3)=3(9-102+1)=-276,
\]

and \(17\nmid276\).

A discriminant or conductor records primes of bad reduction or ramification.  The congruence \(B_r\equiv0\pmod p\) is normally a cancellation at a prime of good reduction.  These are categorically different loci.

## 7. Hecke polynomials: only a special moving diagonal

There is a genuine modular-form congruence at the centre:

\[
B_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p(f)\pmod p,
\qquad
f=\eta(2z)^4\eta(4z)^4.
\tag{7.1}
\]

For this weight-four form the local Hecke polynomial is, up to the usual normalization,

\[
X^2-a_p(f)X+p^3.
\]

This does not construct \(G_r\):

1. the relation (7.1) concerns the single moving index \(r=(p-1)/2\); for a fixed \(r\), it addresses at most the prime \(p=2r+1\);
2. the polynomial itself depends on the same prime \(p\) that is being detected;
3. arbitrary prime divisors of a fixed \(B_r\) do not appear as Hecke coefficients at that index;
4. even the density-zero problem for primes \(p\mid a_p(f)\) is the nonordinary-prime problem for a non-CM weight-four form and is not settled by ordinary Sato--Tate.

Thus the Hecke route supplies an interesting special subfamily, not a universal one-zero integer detector.

## 8. Padé approximants and the Casoratian give anti-divisibility, not a detector

Let \(a_m\) be the primitive second Apéry solution normalized so that

\[
a_mB_{m-1}-a_{m-1}B_m={6\over m^3}.
\tag{8.1}
\]

For \(p>m\), all denominators introduced by the usual Apéry normalization are \(p\)-units.  If \(p>\max(m,3)\) and \(p\mid B_m\), then (8.1) gives

\[
a_mB_{m-1}\equiv {6\over m^3}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{8.2}
\]

Hence both \(a_m\) and \(B_{m-1}\) are \(p\)-units.  This proves that a large prime dividing \(B_m\) does *not* divide the adjacent state or the companion coordinate.  It does not force the prime into a smaller integer.

The suggested ratio difference simplifies exactly to

\[
B_{m-1}\left({a_m\over a_{m-1}}-{B_m\over B_{m-1}}\right)
={a_mB_{m-1}-a_{m-1}B_m\over a_{m-1}}
={6\over m^3a_{m-1}}.
\tag{8.3}
\]

It is a rational number and, at a hit prime, (8.1) provides a nonzero \(p\)-adic unit relation rather than divisibility by \(p\).  Clearing denominators restores the large Apéry/companion coordinates and no low-height radical carrier appears.

This is also consistent with the recurrence-elimination saturation in Proposition 4.1: the Wronskian certifies state independence, but cannot compress the prime divisors of one coordinate.

## 9. Constant-term and Straub-type Laurent-polynomial formulas

An exact constant-term representation used in the project is

\[
B_m=\operatorname{CT}(\Lambda^m),
\]

where

\[
\Lambda(x,y,z)=
{(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)\bigl((1+y)(1+z)+xyz\bigr)\over xyz}.
\tag{9.1}
\]

Such representations explain Lucas and Dwork congruences and are genuinely useful.  They do not turn an additive zero into a product divisor.

When the exponent range is smaller than \(p-1\), finite-field orthogonality expresses the constant term, up to a fixed sign, as a sum

\[
B_m\equiv
\sum_{(x,y,z)\in(\mathbb F_p^\times)^3}\Lambda(x,y,z)^m
\pmod p.
\tag{9.2}
\]

The condition \(B_m\equiv0\) is cancellation of this **sum**.  A product

\[
\prod_{(x,y,z)}\Phi_m(x,y,z)
\]

vanishes when one factor vanishes; that is an entirely different incidence condition.  There is no implication from additive cancellation to a zero factor.

Lifting (9.2) to characteristic zero while preserving its additive meaning simply returns the constant term \(B_m\), whose logarithmic height is linear in \(m\).  Letting the product range depend on \(p\) produces a \(p\)-dependent object rather than one integer \(G_m\).  Thus the Laurent-polynomial formula gives strong local congruences, but not the proposed global norm.

## 10. Varying number fields and Chebotarev do not compress a finite divisor set

For each fixed \(r\), the set

\[
\{p:p\mid B_r\}
\]

is finite.  In contrast, a nonempty Frobenius conjugacy condition in a fixed finite Galois extension defines an infinite Chebotarev set (indeed one of positive density).  Therefore the prime divisors of \(B_r\) cannot be *exactly*, up to finitely many exceptions, the primes in a nonempty splitting class of one fixed extension.

There are three distinct notions:

1. **Splitting.**  If every divisor prime merely splits in \(K_r\), those primes do not divide \(\operatorname{disc}(K_r)\) or a fixed norm; infinitely many unrelated primes split as well.  Splitting gives no detector integer.
2. **Ramification.**  If every divisor prime is forced to ramify, then
   \[
   R_r^+\mid\operatorname{rad}(\operatorname{disc}K_r),
   \]
   so Proposition 2.1 reappears with no height saving.
3. **Norm divisibility.**  If every divisor prime divides \(N_{K_r/\mathbb Q}(\alpha_r)\), then again
   \[
   R_r^+\mid N_{K_r/\mathbb Q}(\alpha_r).
   \]

A bounded field degree does not control discriminant or norm height.  For example, an arbitrary squarefree integer \(R\) can be encoded in the quadratic field \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{\pm R})\); the degree is \(2\), while the discriminant has size comparable to \(R\).  Bounded degree therefore does not compress an arbitrary set of ramified primes.

Finally, a congruence such as

\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Frob}_p)\equiv0\pmod p
\]

is not a classical fixed finite-quotient Chebotarev condition: the modulus in the trace condition changes with \(p\).  Even for a fixed modular form this is the nonordinary-prime problem, not an immediate Chebotarev theorem.

## 11. Direct answers to the seven proposed constructions

### 11.1 Resultant

A resultant with \(X-r\) is \(B_r\) itself.  A higher resultant either detects a different common-root locus or, if it genuinely detects every large divisor of \(B_r\), is divisible by \(R_r^+\).  Recurrence-only scalar elimination is exactly saturated by the forced prime product (Proposition 4.1).

### 11.2 K3 norm

The proposed trace interpretation identifies the coefficient index with a fibre parameter and is not valid.  A norm of a fixed algebraic function reduces to a fixed polynomial divisor and would require the new theorem (3.5).  No natural \(K,\alpha\) with that property is known.

### 11.3 Discriminant/conductor

The reduced Picard--Fuchs singular factor is \(t(t^2-34t+1)\), of degree \(3\), but \(B_2=73\) and \(73\nmid D_{\mathrm{PF}}(2)\).  Bad reduction is not coefficient/trace cancellation at good reduction.

### 11.4 Hecke polynomial

It controls the special moving centre \(r=(p-1)/2\), not all prime divisors of fixed \(B_r\).  The Hecke polynomial also varies with \(p\).

### 11.5 Padé approximant

The Casoratian makes a hit prime a unit on the companion and previous Apéry coordinates.  It yields an inverse congruence, not a small divisible integer.

### 11.6 Straub/constant-term polynomial

The zero is an additive constant-term or finite-field moment cancellation.  Products and norms detect a vanishing factor.  Preserving the additive sum over the integers returns \(B_r\).

### 11.7 Bounded-degree field \(K_r\)

Exact splitting cannot equal a finite divisor set; splitting does not imply divisibility of a discriminant or norm; ramification or norm encoding contains \(R_r^+\) and therefore has at least its height.  Bounded degree alone gives no compression.

## 12. Terminal theorem and the narrow surviving residual

### Status

```text
EXACT_LOGICAL_NO_GO_AND_RADICAL_REDUCTION
```

### Proved in this report

1. The two proposed one-index detector axioms do **not** imply the all-\(n\) moving-modulus estimate; Proposition 1.1 remains false even with \(\log G_r=O(\log r)\).
2. The minimal detector at one index is exactly \(R_r^+\); every resultant, norm, or other true detector contains it.
3. Recurrence-only elimination has scalar ideal exactly equal to the forced prime product and becomes the unit ideal after saturation.
4. The K3/Hasse evaluation locus, discriminant locus, Hecke-centre locus, Padé Wronskian, and constant-term product locus are all different from the required coefficient-zero radical.
5. A fixed-polynomial or fixed-norm detector would close the problem because it supplies the missing congruence transport, but proving its divisibility property is a new theorem.

### Not proved

- There is no counterexample to the bare asymptotic
  \[
  \log R_r^+=r^{o(1)};
  \]
  the minimal radical always exists, and its true growth is not known.
- No congruence-preserving detector, fixed polynomial \(F\), or subexponential row carrier \(H_n\) has been constructed.

### Named residual

```text
CRUX_ID: CONGRUENCE_PRESERVING_ZERO_DETECTOR
```

The concrete residual is:

> Construct nonzero integers \(G_m\) with \(\log|G_m|=o(m)\) such that, outside finitely many primes,
> \[
> p>r,\ p\mid B_r\Longrightarrow p\mid G_r,
> \]
> and
> \[
> m\equiv r\pmod p,\ p\mid G_r\Longrightarrow p\mid G_m.
> \]

The simplest stronger version is the fixed-polynomial divisibility theorem

\[
\boxed{
\exists F\in\mathbb Z[x]\setminus\{0\}:
\quad R_r^+\mid F(r)\quad\text{for every }r.
}
\]

That is the precise point at which a norm, resultant, or geometric divisor would become useful.  Without congruence transport, a one-index detector—even one of polynomial size—does not address the all-\(n\) moving-modulus obstruction.
