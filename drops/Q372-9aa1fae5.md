# Q372 — Is the Apéry zero-set Fourier transform a trace function?

## Executive verdict

There are two different questions hidden in “is \(S_p\) a trace function?” They have very different answers.

1. **Literal trace-function status is easy.** For every \(p\),
   
   \[
   S_p(u)=\sum_{r\in Z_p}e_p(ru)
   \]
   
   is exactly a sum of \(z_p=|Z_p|\) rank-one Artin–Schreier trace functions. Equivalently, it is the trace function of their direct sum. Thus, if one could prove \(z_p=O(1)\), then \(S_p\) would be a sum of \(O(1)\) bounded-conductor trace functions, independently of any Apéry geometry.

2. **The useful Fouvry–Kowalski–Michel statement is not proved.** The natural direct sum has conductor/rank \(O(z_p)\), is generally not geometrically isotypic, and its summands are exceptional linear additive-character sheaves. More importantly, bounded conductor at each individual prime does not control how the frequencies \(r\in Z_p\) align across different primes. That cross-prime alignment is exactly the pair-dispersion problem.

3. **The polynomial-degree discussion is a red herring.** The raw polynomial \(1-f_p^{p-1}\) can be reduced modulo \(X^p-X\) to degree at most \(p-1\), but no nontrivial indicator \(1_{Z_p}\) has a bounded-degree polynomial representative. In fact its reduced degree is at least
   
   \[
   \max(z_p,p-z_p).
   \]
   
   Nevertheless, high interpolation degree does not rule out a low-conductor sheaf. Polynomial degree and sheaf conductor are different complexity measures.

4. **The proposed \(p\)-curvature identification is not correct.** The displayed operator with \(T\) is a difference operator, not the Apéry differential operator. Its natural \(2\times2\) transfer-cycle determinant telescopes to \(1\), not to a polynomial vanishing on \(Z_p\). The actual generating-function differential operator has order \(3\), hence a \(3\times3\) differential \(p\)-curvature. Neither \(p\)-curvature determinant records the zeros of one chosen coefficient sequence.

5. **There is a numerical normalization contradiction.** The current repository analysis in `drops/Q359-4a66b8d3.md` records, through \(p\le2{,}000{,}000\), mean \(z_p=1.001222\ldots\) and maximum \(z_p=12\). Therefore Parseval gives total Fourier energy about \(p\), not \(0.91p^2\). The stated \(0.91p^2\) would force \(z_p\approx0.91p\), which is a different data set or a normalization error.

6. **The proposed fourth-moment substitute is automatic and useless:**
   
   \[
   \sum_u|S_p(u)|^4\le p^2z_p^2
   \]
   
   holds with constant \(1\) for every subset \(Z_p\subseteq\mathbf F_p\). A meaningful one-prime estimate would remove the zero frequency and save a factor \(p\), but even that would not prevent cross-prime anchoring.

7. **The displayed bilinear estimate is incorrect by a full factor \(pq\).** Since
   
   \[
   \sum_{h=1}^{pq-1}|D_N(h/pq)|\ll pq\log(2N),
   \]
   
   the assumption \(|S_p(u)|\le C\sqrt p\) gives, after absolute values,
   
   \[
   |\varepsilon_{pq}^{\mathrm{mixed}}|
   \ll C^2\sqrt{pq}\log(2N),
   \]
   
   not \(C^2\log(pq)/\sqrt{pq}\). For \(p,q\asymp N\), this is \(O(N\log N)\), no better than the trivial interval-length bound after truncation.

8. **Square-root bounds for the individual \(S_p\) do not imply ALPDD.** In fact, take abstract singleton sets
   
   \[
   Z_p=\{a\bmod p\}
   \]
   
   for one common \(a\le N\). Then \(|S_p(u)|=1\) for every \(u\), each \(S_p\) is a rank-one bounded-conductor trace function, and all one-prime Fourier moments are optimal. Yet every pair \((p,q)\) has the common representative \(a\), so \(arepsilon_{pq}=1-N/(pq)\asymp1\). The pair sum has the full anchored-star size \(\asymp N^2/\log^2N\).

Thus the decisive conclusion is:

> **The missing input is not a Deligne bound for each \(S_p\). It is a two-modulus, short-interval dispersion theorem that retains the cross-prime phases of the roots, together with the three Lucas channels not covered by suffix/suffix CRT.**

## 0. First fix the Fourier normalization

Put

\[
e_p(x):=e^{2\pi i x/p},
\qquad
S_p(u):=\sum_{r\in Z_p}e_p(ur),
\qquad
z_p:=|Z_p|.
\]

The exact identities are

\[
S_p(0)=z_p
\tag{0.1}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{u\bmod p}|S_p(u)|^2=pz_p.
}
\tag{0.2}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|^2=pz_p-z_p^2.
}
\tag{0.3}
\]

The project state matters here. `drops/Q359-4a66b8d3.md` records from the large scan that

\[
\operatorname{mean}_p z_p=1.001222\ldots,
\qquad
\max_{p\le2{,}000{,}000}z_p=12.
\tag{0.4}
\]

Thus, for the actual zero sets being used in P3.2, the typical total energy in (0.2) is approximately \(p\), and the average value of \(|S_p(u)|^2\) is approximately \(1\). Moreover,

\[
|S_p(u)|\le z_p
\tag{0.5}
\]

is then much stronger than \(3.5\sqrt p\) for all moderately large \(p\). The numerical bound

\[
|S_p(u)|\le3.5\sqrt p
\]

is therefore not evidence for Deligne-type cancellation in the sparse regime; it is mostly a consequence of the trivial cardinality bound.

### If the intended data really had density \(0.91\)

Suppose, contrary to (0.4), that

\[
z_p=\delta p+o(p),
\qquad \delta=0.91.
\]

Then (0.2) is indeed approximately \(0.91p^2\), but the zero frequency alone contributes

\[
|S_p(0)|^2=z_p^2\sim0.8281p^2.
\]

Hence the nonzero-frequency energy is only

\[
pz_p-z_p^2
\sim\delta(1-\delta)p^2
=0.0819p^2.
\]

The average over \(u\ne0\) is therefore approximately \(0.0819p\), not \(0.91p\). A bound

\[
\max_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|^2\le12.25p
\]

would have peak-to-nonzero-average ratio at most

\[
\frac{12.25}{0.0819}\approx149.6,
\]

not \(13.5\). Thus the numerical paragraph in the question cannot simultaneously describe the stated \(Z_p\), the stated Parseval identity, and the nonzero-frequency maximum.

The first computational task should therefore be to print, for each \(p\), the three quantities

```text
z_p,
S_p(0)^2 = z_p^2,
sum_{u != 0} |S_p(u)|^2 = p z_p - z_p^2,
```

and verify (0.2) exactly before interpreting the peak statistic.

## 1. Literal trace-function realization of \(S_p\)

Fix a nontrivial additive character

\[
\psi_p:\mathbf F_p\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbf Q}_\ell^{\times}
\]

whose chosen complex realization is \(e_p\). For each \(r\in\mathbf F_p\), the function

\[
u\longmapsto e_p(ru)
\]

is the trace function of the rank-one Artin–Schreier sheaf

\[
\mathcal L_{\psi_p(rX)}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
S_p(u)
=t_{\mathcal G_p}(u),
\qquad
\mathcal G_p:=\bigoplus_{r\in Z_p}\mathcal L_{\psi_p(rX)}.
}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Each nonconstant linear Artin–Schreier summand has bounded conductor—at most \(3\) in the normalization used by Fouvry–Kowalski–Michel—independently of \(p\) and \(r\). The direct sum has

\[
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal G_p)=z_p,
\qquad
\operatorname{cond}(\mathcal G_p)\ll z_p+1.
\tag{1.2}
\]

This immediately answers part of Question 1:

- in the broad sense of “trace function of an ℓ-adic sheaf,” **yes**;
- as a single FKM *geometrically isotypic* trace weight, generally **not by this construction**, because the summands for distinct \(r\) are geometrically nonisomorphic;
- as a sum of \(z_p\) rank-one FKM trace weights, **yes exactly**;
- if \(z_p=O(1)\) uniformly, it is a sum of \(O(1)\) uniformly bounded-conductor trace weights.

There is a further warning: these rank-one linear additive-character sheaves are precisely of the “exceptional” type in FKM terminology. Their classification is easy, but a general nonexceptional trace-function theorem does not manufacture cancellation between their frequencies.

### The indicator itself

The function

\[
x\longmapsto1_{Z_p}(x)
\]

is the trace function of the direct sum of punctual sheaves supported at the points \(r\in Z_p\), if one allows general constructible complexes. Its finite Fourier transform is \(S_p\), up to the conventional sign, shift, and normalization.

For analytic purposes, however, (1.1) is cleaner: it already realizes \(S_p\) as a lisse direct sum on \(\mathbf A^1\).

### Sparse set versus dense complement

For \(u\ne0\),

\[
\sum_{r\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(ur)=0,
\]

so

\[
S_{Z_p}(u)=-S_{\mathbf F_p\setminus Z_p}(u).
\tag{1.3}
\]

Thus the direct additive-character description uses at most

\[
\min(z_p,p-z_p)
\]

summands at nonzero frequencies. This can help when either the set or its complement is uniformly small. It does not help for a genuinely positive-density set whose complement also has positive density.

### What about \(f_p\) itself?

The interpolation function

\[
f_p:\mathbf F_p\longrightarrow\mathbf F_p,
\qquad f_p(n)=b_n\pmod p,
\]

is \(\mathbf F_p\)-valued, not canonically complex-valued, so it is not itself an FKM trace weight without applying an additive or multiplicative character.

For example,

\[
x\longmapsto e_p(vf_p(x))
\]

is an Artin–Schreier trace function. If the reduced interpolation polynomial has degree \(d_p<p\), the standard pullback construction has conductor \(O(d_p)\), usually \(d_p+2\). Since \(d_p\) may be of order \(p\), this construction is not uniformly bounded-conductor.

The additive-character identity

\[
1_{f_p(x)=0}
=\frac1p\sum_{v\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(vf_p(x))
\tag{1.4}
\]

uses \(p\) trace functions and therefore also has growing complexity. It is much less efficient than the punctual/direct-root description when \(z_p\) is small.

## 2. There is no bounded-degree indicator polynomial

Every function \(\mathbf F_p\to\mathbf F_p\) has a unique polynomial representative of degree at most \(p-1\), modulo \(X^p-X\). Thus the raw expression

\[
1-f_p(X)^{p-1}
\]

may have formal degree \((p-1)^2\), but as a polynomial function it reduces modulo \(X^p-X\) to degree at most \(p-1\).

That reduction does **not** produce a bounded-degree formula. In fact there is an elementary lower bound.

Let \(P_p(X)\) be the unique reduced polynomial of degree at most \(p-1\) satisfying

\[
P_p(x)=1_{Z_p}(x)
\qquad(x\in\mathbf F_p),
\]

and assume \(0<z_p<p\). Since \(P_p\) vanishes at every point outside \(Z_p\),

\[
\deg P_p\ge p-z_p.
\]

Since \(P_p-1\) vanishes at every point of \(Z_p\),

\[
\deg(P_p-1)=\deg P_p\ge z_p.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\deg P_p\ge\max(z_p,p-z_p).
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

Consequences:

- if \(z_p\asymp p\) with density bounded away from \(0\) and \(1\), then \(\deg P_p\asymp p\);
- if \(z_p=O(1)\), then \(\deg P_p\ge p-O(1)\).

The second case is instructive. A singleton indicator has interpolation degree \(p-1\), yet its Fourier transform is one rank-one Artin–Schreier trace function. Therefore:

> **High interpolation degree is not evidence of high sheaf conductor.**

Conversely, a low-degree polynomial presentation would imply bounded algebraic complexity, but it is far from necessary. The correct question is whether \(Z_p\) has a bounded-complexity geometric description, not whether its \(0/1\) interpolation polynomial has bounded degree.

## 3. The recurrence operator and the two notions of \(p\)-curvature

The operator displayed in the question,

\[
(n+1)^3T-(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)+n^3T^{-1},
\]

is a **difference operator in the index \(n\)**. It is not the Picard–Fuchs differential operator of the Apéry generating function.

### 3.1 The \(2\times2\) difference system

Write

\[
P(n):=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)
\]

and

\[
v_n:=\binom{b_n}{b_{n-1}}.
\]

Away from \(n=-1\), the recurrence becomes

\[
v_{n+1}=A(n)v_n,
\]

where

\[
A(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{P(n)}{(n+1)^3}&-\dfrac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\\[6pt]
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det A(n)=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

If one forms the natural characteristic-\(p\) shift-cycle product as a rational matrix,

\[
M_p(n):=A(n+p-1)\cdots A(n+1)A(n),
\]

then

\[
\det M_p(n)
=\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{(n+j)^3}{(n+j+1)^3}
=\frac{n^3}{(n+p)^3}
=1
\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_p(n).
\tag{3.3}
\]

There are singular-orbit issues when one evaluates at \(n\in\mathbf F_p\), since every \(p\)-cycle meets the pole at \(-1\). But as a rational identity, (3.3) already rules out

\[
Z_p=\{n:\det M_p(n)=0\}.
\]

After clearing denominators, any zeros introduced into a determinant come from the singularity-clearing factors, not from the condition \(b_n=0\).

More fundamentally, \(b_n=0\) is a matrix-**coefficient** condition depending on the chosen initial vector. For \(1\le n<p\), schematically,

\[
b_n=e_1^TA(n-1)\cdots A(1)v_1.
\tag{3.4}
\]

The determinant or conjugacy class of a \(p\)-curvature/cycle matrix is an invariant of the operator. It does not remember which one-dimensional coordinate of which chosen solution vanishes.

Even replacing \(\det M_p\) by \(\det(M_p-I)\) would only detect the occurrence of eigenvalue \(1\); it would not detect \(b_n=0\).

### 3.2 The actual Apéry differential operator

Let

\[
B(x):=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nx^n,
\qquad
\theta:=x\frac{d}{dx}.
\]

The generating function satisfies the order-\(3\) Picard–Fuchs equation

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L B=0,
\qquad
\mathcal L
=\theta^3
-x(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+x^2(\theta+1)^3.
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

Thus the differential connection naturally has rank \(3\), and its differential \(p\)-curvature is represented by a \(3\times3\) matrix. There may also be a rank-\(2\) modular system whose symmetric square is related to (3.5), but that does not turn the displayed index recurrence into a \(2\times2\) differential \(p\)-curvature statement.

Differential \(p\)-curvature controls the reduction of the differential module, horizontal sections, and Frobenius phenomena in the **generating variable \(x\)**. The set

\[
Z_p=\{n:[x^n]B(x)\equiv0\pmod p\}
\]

is a condition in the **coefficient index \(n\)**. No general theorem identifies these two kinds of zero loci.

A truncated solution or Hasse polynomial

\[
A_p(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}b_nx^n
\]

may indeed have a geometric relation to the Picard–Fuchs system. But roots of \(A_p(x)\) in the evaluation variable and zero coefficients \(b_n\) are dual-space objects. The latter are not recovered from \(\det\Psi_p(x)\) without a new, explicit coefficient-extraction theorem.

Therefore the statement

\[
Z_p=\{n:\det\Psi_p(n)=0\}\quad\text{“roughly”}
\]

should not be used. It is not a harmless approximation; it confuses the operator invariant, the chosen solution, and the evaluation/coefficient variables.

## 4. What the fixed Apéry geometry does—and does not—give uniformly in \(p\)

There is genuine fixed geometry behind the Apéry numbers. For example, they are diagonal coefficients of the fixed rational function

\[
\frac{1}
{(1-x_1-x_2)(1-x_3-x_4)-x_1x_2x_3x_4}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

The Picard–Fuchs operator (3.5) is also defined over the integers. These facts can yield bounded-rank geometric objects in an **evaluation parameter**, with a fixed finite set of bad primes and fixed singularity data.

They do not automatically yield a sheaf on \(\mathbf A^1_{\mathbf F_p}\) whose trace at the point \(n\) is \(1_{b_n=0}\). Passing from a fixed period or diagonal to

1. its \(n\)-th Taylor coefficient,
2. reduction modulo the varying characteristic \(p\), and
3. the nonlinear predicate “this coefficient is zero,”

is a \(p\)-dependent operation.

The automatic-sequence machinery for diagonals of rational functions proves that, for each \(p\), the sequence \(b_n\bmod p\) is generated by a finite automaton and satisfies Lucas-type structure. But the automaton and its transition data may depend on \(p\); this is not a theorem that the state count or an associated sheaf conductor is \(O(1)\) as \(p\to\infty\).

### What “uniform family” is actually needed?

For many single-modulus FKM estimates, one does **not** need a compatible system over all primes. FKM explicitly allow a different sheaf for each \(p\), provided the conductors are bounded uniformly and the relevant geometric exceptional cases are excluded.

For the present cross-prime problem, however, uniform conductor is only the first requirement. One additionally needs to rule out coherent phase alignment as \(p\) varies. A robust geometric route would require something like

\[
1_{Z_p}(n)-\frac{z_p}{p}
=\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j,p}\,t_{\mathcal F_{j,p}}(\phi_j(n))
\tag{4.2}
\]

with all of the following uniform:

- \(J=O(1)\);
- ranks, singularities, and Swan conductors of \(\mathcal F_{j,p}\);
- degrees of \(\phi_j\);
- coefficient sizes \(c_{j,p}\);
- geometric nonexceptionality and nonisomorphism conditions sufficient for cross-correlation estimates;
- compatibility with the short interval \(n\le N\) when the product modulus is \(pq\asymp N^2\).

Nothing in the recurrence or in the bare existence of \(p\)-curvature supplies (4.2).

There is also an important simplification: if \(z_p=O(1)\), then \(S_p\) already has the representation (1.1). In that regime the open problem is not finding a sheaf. It is proving that the roots indexing those simple Artin–Schreier sheaves do not form an anchored configuration across many primes.

## 5. Exact fourth moments and why the proposed substitute is vacuous

The second moment is Parseval, (0.2). The fourth moment has the exact additive-energy interpretation

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{u\bmod p}|S_p(u)|^4
=pE^+(Z_p),
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

where

\[
E^+(Z_p)
:=\#\{(a,b,c,d)\in Z_p^4:a+b=c+d\pmod p\}.
\]

Removing the zero mode gives

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|^4
=pE^+(Z_p)-z_p^4.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

The proposed bound

\[
\sum_u|S_p(u)|^4\le Cp^2z_p^2
\tag{5.3}
\]

holds with \(C=1\) for **every** set \(Z_p\). Indeed,

\[
\sum_u|S_p(u)|^4
\le \bigl(\max_u|S_p(u)|^2\bigr)
\sum_u|S_p(u)|^2
\le z_p^2\cdot pz_p
=pz_p^3
\le p^2z_p^2.
\tag{5.4}
\]

So (5.3) contains no arithmetic information.

A genuinely random-scale one-prime target would be, for example,

\[
\sum_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|^4\ll pz_p^2,
\tag{5.5}
\]

or equivalently

\[
E^+(Z_p)=\frac{z_p^4}{p}+O(z_p^2).
\tag{5.6}
\]

For a positive-density set, (5.5) saves a factor \(p\) over (5.3). It says that the nonzero Fourier coefficients have random-scale fourth moment.

But even (5.5) is not sufficient for pair dispersion. A singleton has

\[
|S_p(u)|=1,
\qquad
\sum_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|^4=p-1,
\]

which is optimal at the scale (5.5), yet singleton roots can be perfectly aligned across all primes. One-prime additive energy sees the internal geometry of \(Z_p\); it does not see the CRT geometry of \(Z_p\times Z_q\) across different characteristics.

## 6. Separate the mixed correlation from the zero-frequency axes

For the suffix event, put

\[
I_p(n):=1_{Z_p}(n\bmod p),
\qquad
a_p:=\frac{z_p}{p},
\qquad
g_p(n):=I_p(n)-a_p.
\]

Then

\[
R_{pq}:=\sum_{n\le N}I_p(n)I_q(n)
\]

satisfies the exact decomposition

\[
\boxed{
R_{pq}-Na_pa_q
=\sum_{n\le N}g_p(n)g_q(n)
+a_p\sum_{n\le N}g_q(n)
+a_q\sum_{n\le N}g_p(n).
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

The first term is the genuinely mixed covariance. The last two are one-prime boundary discrepancies. In the Fourier formula:

- the mixed term corresponds to frequencies \(h\) with \(p\nmid h\) and \(q\nmid h\);
- the term \(a_p\sum g_q\) corresponds to \(p\mid h\), so that \(S_p(0)=z_p\);
- the term \(a_q\sum g_p\) corresponds to \(q\mid h\).

Thus a bound assumed only for \(u\ne0\) cannot simply be inserted for every \(h=1,\ldots,pq-1\).

This issue can be removed by centering and treating (6.1) term by term. It does not repair the main factor error below.

## 7. The Dirichlet-kernel calculation: the missing factor \(pq\)

Let

\[
m:=pq,
\qquad
D_N(\alpha):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(n\alpha).
\]

Up to harmless signs or complex conjugates depending on Fourier convention,

\[
\varepsilon_{pq}
=\frac1m\sum_{h=1}^{m-1}
S_p(h\bar q)
S_q(h\bar p)
D_N(h/m).
\tag{7.1}
\]

Because \(p,q>\sqrt N\), one has \(N<m\). The standard bound

\[
|D_N(h/m)|\le
\min\left(N,\frac{1}{2\|h/m\|}\right)
\tag{7.2}
\]

implies

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{h=1}^{m-1}|D_N(h/m)|\ll m\log(2N).
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Indeed, for \(1\le h\le m/2\), split the sum at \(h\asymp m/N\): the first \(m/N\) terms contribute \(O(m)\), and the rest contribute \(O(m\log N)\).

### 7.1 Mixed frequencies

Assume

\[
|S_p(u)|\le C\sqrt p,
\qquad
|S_q(v)|\le C\sqrt q
\]

for nonzero \(u,v\). On the mixed frequencies \(p\nmid h\), \(q\nmid h\),

\[
|S_p(h\bar q)S_q(h\bar p)|\le C^2\sqrt{pq}=C^2\sqrt m.
\]

Therefore absolute-value completion gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
|\varepsilon_{pq}^{\mathrm{mixed}}|
&\le
\frac{C^2\sqrt m}{m}
\sum_{h=1}^{m-1}|D_N(h/m)|\\
&\ll
\frac{C^2\sqrt m}{m}\cdot m\log(2N)\\
&=\boxed{\,O\!\left(C^2\sqrt m\log(2N)\right).\,}
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Since \(m=pq\), this is

\[
\boxed{
|\varepsilon_{pq}^{\mathrm{mixed}}|
\ll C^2\sqrt{pq}\log(2N),
}
\tag{7.5}
\]

not

\[
C^2\frac{\log(pq)}{\sqrt{pq}}.
\]

The proposed calculation correctly estimated the Dirichlet-kernel \(L^1\)-norm as roughly \(pq\log(pq)\), but then divided by \(pq\) twice. The actual Fourier prefactor after applying \(|S_pS_q|\le C^2\sqrt{pq}\) is

\[
\frac{\sqrt{pq}}{pq}=rac1{\sqrt{pq}},
\]

and multiplying by \(pq\log(pq)\) yields

\[
\sqrt{pq}\log(pq).
\]

For \(p,q\asymp N\),

\[
\sqrt{pq}\asymp N,
\]

so (7.5) is \(O(N\log N)\). Since trivially \(|\varepsilon_{pq}|\ll N\), completion gives no useful saving in that top block.

This is the classical square-root barrier in its cleanest form:

- modulus \(m=pq\asymp N^2\);
- interval length \(N\asymp\sqrt m\);
- complete Fourier square-root cancellation yields an incomplete-sum bound of size \(\sqrt m\log m\asymp N\log N\).

To beat the interval length, one needs Burgess-type input, Type-II/Type-III averaging, spectral structure, or cancellation while averaging over \(p,q\). A pointwise Deligne bound for each complete local Fourier transform does not suffice.

### 7.2 Axis frequencies

For \(h=pk\), \(1\le k<q\), the \(p\)-frequency is zero. The corresponding contribution is bounded by

\[
\frac{z_p}{pq}
\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}|S_q(k)|\,|D_N(k/q)|.
\]

Using \(|S_q(k)|\le C\sqrt q\) and

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}|D_N(k/q)|\ll q\log(2q),
\]

one obtains

\[
\boxed{
|\varepsilon_{pq}^{(p\text{-axis})}|
\ll C\,\frac{z_p\sqrt q}{p}\log(2q).
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

Similarly,

\[
\boxed{
|\varepsilon_{pq}^{(q\text{-axis})}|
\ll C\,\frac{z_q\sqrt p}{q}\log(2p).
}
\tag{7.7}
\]

These terms are small when \(z_p,z_q=O(1)\) and \(p,q\asymp N\), but large in a dense-zero regime. In either case they must be separated; the nonzero-frequency hypothesis does not cover them.

### 7.3 What Parseval alone gives

The CRT map \(h\bmod pq\mapsto(h\bar q\bmod p,h\bar p\bmod q)\) is a bijection, so

\[
\sum_{h\bmod pq}
|S_p(h\bar q)S_q(h\bar p)|^2
=(pz_p)(qz_q)
=pqz_pz_q.
\tag{7.8}
\]

Also, because \(N<pq\),

\[
\sum_{h\bmod pq}|D_N(h/pq)|^2=pqN.
\tag{7.9}
\]

Cauchy–Schwarz therefore gives a bound at the scale

\[
\boxed{
|\varepsilon_{pq}|\ll\sqrt{Nz_pz_q}
}
\tag{7.10}
\]

up to the harmless handling of the removed zero term. For \(z_p,z_q=O(1)\), this is \(O(\sqrt N)\), still far too large to sum over roughly \(N^2/\log^2N\) prime pairs. In the sparse regime, the direct combinatorial bound below is better.

### 7.4 The sparse combinatorial bound

Since \(pq>N\), each CRT residue class modulo \(pq\) has at most one representative in \([1,N]\). There are \(z_pz_q\) pairs of roots, hence

\[
R_{pq}\le z_pz_q.
\tag{7.11}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
|\varepsilon_{pq}|\le
R_{pq}+\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}
<2z_pz_q.
}
\tag{7.12}
\]

If \(z_p,z_q=O(1)\), this is \(O(1)\), much stronger than (7.10) or (7.5). But summing \(O(1)\) over all pairs gives the forbidden anchored-star scale. The unresolved question is not the size of one Fourier coefficient; it is how often one of the \(z_pz_q\) CRT representatives lands in the short initial interval \([1,N]\).

---

## 8. A decisive counterexample: optimal one-prime Fourier behavior, maximal pair failure

The insufficiency of the square-root hypothesis is not merely a weakness of the preceding proof. It is logically false that one-prime Fourier magnitude controls cross-prime dispersion.

Fix an integer \(a\in[1,N]\), and for every prime \(p>\sqrt N\) define the abstract root set

\[
Z_p:=\{a\bmod p\}.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Then \(z_p=1\) and

\[
S_p(u)=e_p(au).
\tag{8.2}
\]

Consequently, for every \(u\),

\[
|S_p(u)|=1.
\tag{8.3}
\]

This is far stronger than \(C\sqrt p\). Moreover:

- \(S_p\) is the trace function of the single rank-one sheaf \(\mathcal L_{\psi_p(aX)}\);
- its conductor is uniformly bounded;
- it is even obtained from the same fixed integer frequency \(a\) in every characteristic;
- Parseval is optimal: \(\sum_u|S_p(u)|^2=p\);
- the fourth moment is optimal: \(\sum_u|S_p(u)|^4=p\).

Yet for every pair \(p<q\), the CRT class determined by the two roots contains \(a\), and \(a\le N\). Since \(pq>N\), it is the unique representative in the interval. Thus

\[
R_{pq}=1
\]

and

\[
\varepsilon_{pq}=1-\frac{N}{pq}=1-o(1)
\qquad(p,q\asymp N).
\tag{8.4}
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_{p<q}|\varepsilon_{pq}|
\asymp
\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right)^2.
\tag{8.5}
\]

This example is not claimed to be the Apéry family. It isolates the logical issue: all one-prime trace/conductor/\(L^2\)/\(L^4\)/pointwise Fourier conditions can hold perfectly while the roots are anchored at one integer across primes.

For the Apéry problem, an anchored integer \(a\) means that many primes divide the same \(b_a\). Excluding precisely that phenomenon is the all-\(n\) content of P3.2. It cannot be deduced from the magnitudes \(|S_p(u)|\), because the magnitudes erase the phases that encode the common anchor.

---

## 9. Does the hypothetical bound, if true, suffice?

Yes—the final summation arithmetic in the question is essentially correct **conditional on** the claimed per-pair estimate.

For primes in a dyadic block \(p,q\asymp N\), there are

\[
\asymp\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}
\]

pairs. If one somehow proved

\[
|\varepsilon_{pq}|\ll\frac{\log N}{N},
\]

then

\[
\sum_{p<q}|\varepsilon_{pq}|
\ll
\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\cdot\frac{\log N}{N}
\ll\frac{N}{\log N}.
\tag{9.1}
\]

Across the full window, the same estimate follows from

\[
\sum_{p\le N}\frac1{\sqrt p}\asymp\frac{\sqrt N}{\log N}.
\]

Such an aggregate bound would be more than adequate for a weak ALPDD target and would also be \(O(N)\) at the second-moment scale.

But (9.1) is irrelevant unless the per-pair estimate is established. It does not follow from \(|S_p(u)|\ll\sqrt p\); the correct completion bound is (7.5).

There is also an integrality warning in the sparse regime. If \(z_p=z_q=1\) and \(p,q\asymp N\), then

\[
R_{pq}\in\{0,1\},
\qquad
\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}\asymp\frac1N.
\]

Whenever a CRT representative lands in \([1,N]\), one has

\[
|\varepsilon_{pq}|=1+O(1/N),
\]

not \(O(\log N/N)\). Thus a uniform tiny per-pair estimate would force essentially every pair to be empty. The realistic theorem must allow exceptional hitting pairs and control their **aggregate number**, rather than make every pair individually pseudorandom.

---

## 10. The weaker substitute that actually matches P3.2

For the suffix/suffix channel, let

\[
[r,s]_{p,q}\in[0,pq)
\]

be the CRT representative satisfying

\[
[r,s]_{p,q}\equiv r\pmod p,
\qquad
[r,s]_{p,q}\equiv s\pmod q.
\]

Since \(pq>N\), one has the exact identity

\[
R_{pq}
=\sum_{r\in Z_p}\sum_{s\in Z_q}
1_{[r,s]_{p,q}\in[1,N]}.
\tag{10.1}
\]

The natural aggregate target is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}
\sum_{r\in Z_p}\sum_{s\in Z_q}
1_{[r,s]_{p,q}\le N}
=O(N),
}
\tag{10.2}
\]

or, after subtracting the main term, bounded aggregate positive covariance:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}
\left(
R_{pq}-\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}
\right)_+
=O(N).
}
\tag{10.3}
\]

These formulations retain the cross-prime phases/root positions that \(|S_p(u)|\) discards.

An analytic substitute would be a genuine two-modulus large-sieve or dispersion estimate for the centered periodic functions \(g_p\), at interval length \(N\approx\sqrt{pq}\), for example an estimate strong enough to control

\[
\sum_{p<q}
\left(
\sum_{n\le N}g_p(n)g_q(n)
\right)_+.
\tag{10.4}
\]

A sheaf-theoretic proof of (10.4) would need cross-prime geometric independence and a short-interval mechanism beyond ordinary complete-sum Deligne bounds. Merely recognizing each \(S_p\) as a trace function does not provide either ingredient.

### The other three Lucas channels remain

For the full P3.2 indicator and \(p>\sqrt N\), write

\[
n=a_pp+r_p,
\qquad 0\le a_p,r_p<p.
\]

The Apéry Lucas congruence gives

\[
p\mid b_n
\iff
a_p\in Z_p\quad\text{or}\quad r_p\in Z_p.
\]

Thus the two-prime intersection splits into four channels:

1. suffix/suffix;
2. suffix/leading;
3. leading/suffix;
4. leading/leading.

The sum \(S_p(u)\) in the question directly addresses only the suffix variable. Even a complete solution of (10.2) would not control low roots \(a\in Z_p\) that generate intervals of length about \(p\). This is the same four-channel warning already identified in `drops/Q359-4a66b8d3.md` and `drops/Q375-563302f3.md`.

In the top-half model \(p>n/2\), the leading digit is \(1\), and for \(p\ge7\) it is not a zero because \(b_1=5\). Only there is suffix/suffix the whole event.

## 11. Direct answers to the five questions

### 1. Is \(f_p\) or \(1_{Z_p}\) a trace function? Is there a lower-degree expression?

- \(f_p\) is \(\mathbf F_p\)-valued, so not itself an FKM complex trace weight without composition with a character.
- \(e_p(vf_p(x))\) is an Artin–Schreier trace function, but the natural conductor grows with the interpolation degree, generally \(O(p)\).
- \(1_{Z_p}\) is a trace function of a punctual constructible complex in the broad sense.
- \(S_p\) is exactly a sum of \(z_p\) rank-one Artin–Schreier trace functions, equation (1.1).
- If \(z_p=O(1)\), this is already a bounded-length, bounded-conductor decomposition.
- There is no bounded-degree exact indicator polynomial: its reduced degree is at least \(\max(z_p,p-z_p)\).
- High polynomial degree does not exclude a more economical sheaf realization.

### 2. Does Apéry \(p\)-curvature give a complexity-\(O(1)\) sheaf cutting out \(Z_p\)?

Not from the stated argument.

- The displayed \(T\)-operator is a difference operator.
- Its natural \(2\times2\) cycle determinant telescopes to \(1\).
- The actual differential operator has order \(3\).
- Operator \(p\)-curvature does not encode zeros of one selected solution coefficient.
- The claimed identity \(Z_p=\{n:\det\Psi_p(n)=0\}\) is therefore false or, at minimum, requires an entirely new theorem with a different \(\Psi_p\).

### 3. Does the differential equation give a uniformly bounded-conductor family as \(p\) varies?

It gives fixed geometric complexity in the generating/evaluation variable, but no known uniformly bounded-conductor realization of the coefficient-zero indicator in the index variable.

For many FKM applications a compatible system across all \(p\) is not strictly necessary; a uniform conductor bound for separately chosen sheaves can suffice. For this pair-dispersion problem, however, one also needs cross-prime nonalignment. The direct Artin–Schreier decomposition is highly exceptional and does not provide it.

### 4. What weaker substitute exists if there is no useful trace family?

- If \(z_p=O(1)\), \(S_p\) is already a sum of \(O(1)\) trace functions.
- The proposed fourth moment \(\ll p^2z_p^2\) is trivial for every set.
- A meaningful one-prime fourth moment is \(\sum_{u\ne0}|S_p(u)|^4\ll pz_p^2\), but even this does not control cross-prime anchoring.
- The correct substitute is an aggregate CRT-incidence or centered cross-prime large-sieve estimate such as (10.2)–(10.4).

### 5. Is the bilinear estimate correct, and does square-root cancellation alone suffice for ALPDD?

- The final implication from a hypothetical \(O(\log N/N)\) per-pair bound is correct.
- The derivation of that per-pair bound is not correct.
- Absolute-value completion gives \(O(\sqrt{pq}\log N)\), not \(O(\log N/\sqrt{pq})\).
- The original formula also contains zero-frequency axes not covered by a nonzero-\(u\) hypothesis.
- Even the much stronger bound \(|S_p(u)|\le1\) for every \(p,u\) can coexist with maximal anchored-star pair discrepancy.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Square-root cancellation of each }S_p\text{ alone does not suffice for ALPDD.}
}
\]

---

## 12. Recommended next theorem

The most focused next step is not to seek a generic statement that \(S_p\) is a trace function—it already is one in the elementary sense. Instead prove one of the following Apéry-specific assertions:

1. **Suffix CRT anti-anchor:** equation (10.2), perhaps first dyadically and with a small exceptional set of pairs.
2. **Cross-prime phase large sieve:** a bound for the Gram matrix of the centered functions \(g_p\) on \([1,N]\), strong at length \(N\approx\sqrt{pq}\).
3. **Structural root transport:** an explicit formula showing that roots \(r\in Z_p\) are reductions of points in finitely many algebraic families with enough nonexceptional monodromy to prevent the singleton-anchor model.
4. **Direct all-channel incidence theorem:** control the four Lucas channels simultaneously, avoiding a suffix-only detour.

A theorem merely stating

\[
|S_p(u)|\ll\sqrt p
\]

is neither the minimum ingredient nor, by itself, a sufficient ingredient.
