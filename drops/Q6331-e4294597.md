# Executive verdict

Let

\[
F(x)=\sum_{n\ge 0}b_nx^n,\qquad
\tau(x)=F(x)^{1/2}=\sum_{n\ge0}\tau_nx^n,
\qquad
\sigma(x)=\left(\frac{F(x)}{1-34x+x^2}\right)^{1/2}
 =\sum_{n\ge0}\sigma_nx^n.
\]

The conclusions are as follows.

1. **Neither coefficient sequence is hypergeometric.** In fact, the displayed second-order recurrences have **no nonzero hypergeometric-term solution at all** over \(\overline{\mathbf Q}\): there is no solution with rational consecutive quotient. Consequently neither \(\tau_n\) nor \(\sigma_n\) can be
   \[
   c\lambda^n\frac{(A)_n(B)_n}{(C)_n n!},
   \]
   and neither can be a fixed linear combination of two such terms.

2. **The direct differential equations are genuine Heun equations.** They have four regular singular points
   \[
   0,\quad 17-12\sqrt2,\quad17+12\sqrt2,\quad\infty.
   \]
   However, they are modular Heun equations: after an algebraic change from the Fricke Hauptmodul \(x\) to a canonical \(\Gamma_0(6)\) Hauptmodul \(T\), and an elementary gauge, both are expressed through Maier's canonical level-six weight-one form \(h_6(T)\). That form is itself a rational pullback and algebraic gauge of
   \[
   {}_2F_1\!\left(\frac1{12},\frac5{12};1;z\right).
   \]
   Thus **the functions are Gauss-pullback modular functions, but their \(x\)-coefficients are not Gauss hypergeometric terms**. There is no contradiction between these statements.

3. There is an explicit finite nested-binomial formula for every \(\tau_n\) and \(\sigma_n\), obtained from Maier's coefficient formula for \(h_6\) followed by Lagrange inversion for
   \[
   x=\frac{T}{(T+8)(T+9)}.
   \]

4. For each prime \(p>3\), the quarter coefficient has an exact reduction to a finite polynomial coefficient, equivalently to a finite weighted power sum over \(\mathbf F_p\). This is a precise finite-field reduction of the empirical law.

5. **The proposed direct Greene-\({}_2F_1\)/Jacobi-sum proof is not presently valid.** There are two independent obstructions:
   - the quarter coefficient is a **Cartier/coefficient extraction after a nonlinear pullback**, not a value of the Gauss function at a field element;
   - the universal Gauss parameters \(1/12,5/12\) require order-twelve characters, which do not exist over \(\mathbf F_p\) in either vanishing class \(p\equiv5,23\pmod{24}\).

   One can move to \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\), where order-twelve characters exist, but then a Frobenius-descent and Cartier identity is required. That identity is the sharp remaining gap.

6. The numerical law can be stated compactly, on the two branch classes where the quarter position is integral, as
   \[
   \boxed{\text{relevant quarter coefficient vanishes}
   \iff \left(\frac{-3}{p}\right)=-1.}
   \]
   This means \(p\equiv5\pmod{24}\) for the \(\tau\)-branch and \(p\equiv23\pmod{24}\) for the \(\sigma\)-branch. This is still an empirical theorem target here, not a proof. In particular, the union of the two classes is **not** the set of primes represented by the single quadratic form \(2X^2+3Y^2\): for example, \(23\) has no such representation. Any proof that treats the two branches as one ordinary discriminant-\(-24\) representation class is therefore incorrect.

The strongest honest endpoint is an exact modular/finite-field reduction plus a sharply isolated Cartier/Jacobi-sum gap.

# 1. Differential equations derived directly from the recurrences

Write \(\theta=x\,d/dx\), and put

\[
q(x):=1-34x+x^2.
\]

## 1.1 The \(\tau\)-equation

The coefficient recurrence is

\[
4(n+2)^2\tau_{n+2}
-2(68n^2+170n+107)\tau_{n+1}
+(2n+1)^2\tau_n=0.
\tag{1.1}
\]

Multiplying by \(x^{n+2}\), summing, and using the initial values gives

\[
\boxed{
\left[\theta^2
-x\left(34\theta^2+17\theta+\frac52\right)
+x^2\left(\theta+\frac12\right)^2\right]\tau=0.
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\left[q(x)\theta^2+x(x-17)\theta+\frac{x(x-10)}4\right]\tau=0.
}
\tag{1.3}
\]

In ordinary derivative form,

\[
\boxed{
xq(x)\tau''+(1-51x+2x^2)\tau'
+\frac{x-10}{4}\tau=0.}
\tag{1.4}
\]

## 1.2 The \(\sigma\)-equation

Likewise,

\[
4(n+2)^2\sigma_{n+2}
-2(68n^2+238n+209)\sigma_{n+1}
+(2n+3)^2\sigma_n=0
\tag{1.5}
\]

gives

\[
\boxed{
\left[\theta^2
-x\left(34\theta^2+51\theta+\frac{39}{2}\right)
+x^2\left(\theta+\frac32\right)^2\right]\sigma=0,
}
\tag{1.6}
\]

or

\[
\boxed{
\left[q(x)\theta^2+3x(x-17)\theta
+\frac{x(9x-78)}4\right]\sigma=0,
}
\tag{1.7}
\]

and hence

\[
\boxed{
xq(x)\sigma''+(1-85x+4x^2)\sigma'
+\frac{9x-78}{4}\sigma=0.}
\tag{1.8}
\]

Direct substitution verifies the exact gauge relation

\[
\boxed{\sigma(x)=q(x)^{-1/2}\tau(x),}
\tag{1.9}
\]

with the branch normalized to equal \(1\) at \(x=0\).

## 1.3 Relation with the Apéry third-order equation

The symmetric square of (1.2) is the Apéry operator

\[
\boxed{
\left[
\theta^3
-x(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+x^2(\theta+1)^3
\right]F=0.
}
\tag{1.10}
\]

The normalized analytic solution is exactly \(F=\tau^2\). Thus the rank-three Apéry equation is genuinely the symmetric square of this rank-two Heun equation.

# 2. The equations in direct Heun form

Set

\[
\rho:=17+12\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^4,
\qquad z=\rho x,
\qquad a=\rho^2.
\]

Then the roots of \(q\) map to \(z=1,a\).

I use the Heun convention

\[
y''+\left(\frac\gamma z+\frac\delta{z-1}
+\frac\epsilon{z-a}\right)y'
+\frac{\alpha_H\beta_Hz-Q}{z(z-1)(z-a)}y=0,
\tag{2.1}
\]

with

\[
\alpha_H+\beta_H+1=\gamma+\delta+\epsilon,
\]

and \(\operatorname{Hl}(a,Q;\alpha_H,\beta_H,\gamma,\delta;z)\) denoting the solution equal to \(1\) at \(z=0\).

The equations above give

\[
\boxed{
\tau(x)=
\operatorname{Hl}\!\left(
\rho^2,\frac52\rho;
\frac12,\frac12,1,\frac12;
\rho x
\right).
}
\tag{2.2}
\]

For \(\sigma\),

\[
\boxed{
\sigma(x)=
\operatorname{Hl}\!\left(
\rho^2,\frac{39}{2}\rho;
\frac32,\frac32,1,\frac32;
\rho x
\right).
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

The local exponent data are

\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
&0&\rho^{-1}&\rho&\infty\\ \hline
\tau&(0,0)&(0,\tfrac12)&(0,\tfrac12)&(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\\
\sigma&(0,0)&(-\tfrac12,0)&(-\tfrac12,0)&(\tfrac32,\tfrac32).
\end{array}
\tag{2.4}
\]

The two finite nonzero singularities have nonintegral exponent difference. They are not apparent singularities removable by a rational gauge. Therefore the direct \(x\)-equations are genuinely four-singularity Heun equations, not Gauss equations in disguise under a Möbius change of variable.

They can nevertheless be nontrivial algebraic pullbacks of Gauss equations, and in fact they are. That modular pullback is described in Section 5.

# 3. No hypergeometric-term solution: a proof from the recurrence

A sequence \(u_n\) is a hypergeometric term if

\[
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\in\overline{\mathbf Q}(n)
\]

for all sufficiently large \(n\).

The requested Pochhammer expression is a special case, since

\[
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}
=\lambda\frac{(n+A)(n+B)}{(n+C)(n+1)}.
\]

I use the Gosper--Petkovšek normal form. For a second-order polynomial-coefficient recurrence

\[
p_2(n)u_{n+2}+p_1(n)u_{n+1}+p_0(n)u_n=0,
\tag{3.1}
\]

any nonzero hypergeometric solution has a quotient expressible as

\[
r(n):=\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}
=z\frac{a(n)}{b(n)}\frac{c(n+1)}{c(n)},
\tag{3.2}
\]

where \(c\) is a polynomial, \(a\) divides the trailing coefficient \(p_0(n)\), and \(b\) divides \(p_2(n-1)\), subject to the standard dispersion coprimality conditions. This is the structural step behind Petkovšek's algorithm `Hyper`.

Precise reference: Marko Petkovšek, “Hypergeometric solutions of linear recurrences with polynomial coefficients,” *Journal of Symbolic Computation* **14** (1992), 243--264, DOI
`10.1016/0747-7171(92)90038-6`.

## 3.1 Asymptotics forced by the \(\tau\)-recurrence

For (1.1),

\[
p_0(n)=(2n+1)^2,\qquad p_2(n-1)=4(n+1)^2.
\]

After constants are absorbed into \(z\), the normal form therefore forces

\[
a(n)=(n+\tfrac12)^e,\qquad b(n)=(n+1)^e,\qquad e\in\{0,1,2\},
\tag{3.3}
\]

because the quotient must have degree zero at infinity.

Let \(d=\deg c\). Then (3.2) has expansion

\[
r(n)=\lambda\left(1+\frac{d-e/2}{n}+O(n^{-2})\right).
\tag{3.4}
\]

On the other hand, substitute

\[
r(n)=\lambda\left(1+\frac\kappa n+O(n^{-2})\right)
\]

into the Riccati equation obtained from (1.1). The leading equation is

\[
\lambda^2-34\lambda+1=0.
\tag{3.5}
\]

The next coefficient is

\[
(\lambda^2-1)(\kappa+\tfrac32)=0.
\]

Since neither characteristic root is \(\pm1\),

\[
\boxed{\kappa=-\frac32.}
\tag{3.6}
\]

But the Petkovšek form gives

\[
d-e/2\ge -1.
\]

This contradicts (3.6). Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{the \(\tau\)-recurrence has no nonzero hypergeometric solution.}}
\tag{3.7}
\]

## 3.2 The \(\sigma\)-recurrence

For (1.5),

\[
p_0(n)=(2n+3)^2,\qquad p_2(n-1)=4(n+1)^2.
\]

The normal form now forces

\[
a(n)=(n+\tfrac32)^e,\qquad b(n)=(n+1)^e,\qquad e\in\{0,1,2\},
\tag{3.8}
\]

and hence

\[
r(n)=\lambda\left(1+\frac{d+e/2}{n}+O(n^{-2})\right),
\qquad d+e/2\ge0.
\tag{3.9}
\]

The recurrence itself gives the same characteristic equation (3.5), but the next coefficient is

\[
(\lambda^2-1)(\kappa+\tfrac12)=0,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{\kappa=-\frac12.}
\tag{3.10}
\]

This contradicts (3.9). Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{the \(\sigma\)-recurrence has no nonzero hypergeometric solution.}}
\tag{3.11}
\]

This is stronger than disproving one guessed parameter set: it rules out every rational consecutive quotient.

# 4. A fixed sum of two hypergeometric terms is also impossible

Suppose, for contradiction, that

\[
y_n=u_n+v_n
\tag{4.1}
\]

is either \(\tau_n\) or \(\sigma_n\), where \(u,v\) are hypergeometric terms.

Each is annihilated by a first-order Ore operator

\[
E-r_u(n),\qquad E-r_v(n),
\]

where \(E\) is the forward shift. Their least common left multiple has order at most two and annihilates \(u+v=y\).

The sequence \(y\) has no first-order annihilator, by Section 3. Its minimal annihilator therefore has order exactly two. The displayed recurrence is an order-two annihilator, so it is associate to the minimal operator. The least common left multiple of the two first-order operators also has order two and must be associate to that same minimal operator. It consequently annihilates \(u\) and \(v\), producing a nonzero hypergeometric solution of the displayed recurrence. This contradicts (3.7) or (3.11).

If \(u,v\) are linearly dependent, (4.1) is already a single hypergeometric term and was excluded above. A term that terminates is zero in the eventual sequence ring and cannot alter the conclusion.

Hence:

\[
\boxed{
\tau_n\text{ and }\sigma_n\text{ are not fixed two-term combinations of hypergeometric terms.}
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This applies in particular to two terms of the requested Pochhammer type, with arbitrary algebraic parameters and bases.

# 5. The exact modular parametrization

The absence of coefficientwise hypergeometric terms does **not** mean that the functions lack a hypergeometric modular origin.

## 5.1 Canonical level-six data

Let

\[
T=T(\tau)
=72\frac{\eta(2\tau)\eta(6\tau)^5}
{\eta(\tau)^5\eta(3\tau)}
\tag{5.1}
\]

and

\[
h_6(T(\tau))
=\frac{\eta(\tau)^6\eta(6\tau)}
{\eta(2\tau)^3\eta(3\tau)^2}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Put

\[
D(T):=(T+8)(T+9)=T^2+17T+72.
\tag{5.3}
\]

The Fricke Hauptmodul used for the Apéry expansion is

\[
\boxed{
x=\frac{T}{D(T)}
=\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)\eta(6\tau)}
{\eta(2\tau)\eta(3\tau)}\right)^{12}.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

The local inverse at the cusp is

\[
\boxed{
T(x)=\frac{1-17x-\sqrt{1-34x+x^2}}{2x}
=72x+O(x^2).
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

The Apéry modular form is

\[
F(x(T))
=\frac{\eta(2\tau)^7\eta(3\tau)^7}
{\eta(\tau)^5\eta(6\tau)^5}
=\frac{D(T)}{72}h_6(T)^2.
\tag{5.6}
\]

It follows that

\[
\boxed{
\tau(x(T))=\sqrt{\frac{D(T)}{72}}\,h_6(T).
}
\tag{5.7}
\]

A direct algebraic calculation from \(x=T/D\) gives

\[
\boxed{
1-34x(T)+x(T)^2
=\frac{(72-T^2)^2}{D(T)^2}.
}
\tag{5.8}
\]

With the local square root equal to one at \(T=0\),

\[
\boxed{
\sigma(x(T))
=\frac{D(T)^{3/2}}
{\sqrt{72}(72-T^2)}h_6(T).
}
\tag{5.9}
\]

## 5.2 Canonical Heun form for \(h_6\)

Maier gives

\[
\boxed{
h_6(T)=
\operatorname{Hl}\!\left(
\frac98,\frac34;1,1,1,1;-\frac{T}{8}
\right).
}
\tag{5.10}
\]

Its differential equation is

\[
\boxed{
h_6''+\left(\frac1T+\frac1{T+8}+\frac1{T+9}\right)h_6'
+\frac{T+6}{T(T+8)(T+9)}h_6=0.
}
\tag{5.11}
\]

## 5.3 Exact Gauss pullback

Define

\[
\mathcal N(T):=(T+6)(T^3+18T^2+84T+24).
\tag{5.12}
\]

Then \(\mathcal N(0)=144\), and Maier's level-six formula is

\[
\boxed{
h_6(T)=
\left(\frac{\mathcal N(T)}{144}\right)^{-1/4}
{}_2F_1\!\left(
\frac1{12},\frac5{12};1;
\frac{1728T(T+8)^3(T+9)^2}{\mathcal N(T)^3}
\right).
}
\tag{5.13}
\]

Thus the most precise description is:

- in the original variable \(x\): a genuine Heun equation;
- after the algebraic map \(T=T(x)\) and a gauge: Maier's canonical level-six Heun solution;
- after a further rational modular map and gauge: the universal Gauss function \({}_2F_1(1/12,5/12;1;\cdot)\).

A primary reference is Robert S. Maier, “On Rationally Parametrized Modular Equations,” *Journal of the Ramanujan Mathematical Society* **24** (2009), 1--73, arXiv:math/0611041v4. The relevant items are Theorem 7.1, Tables 12--15, Theorem 7.4, and equations (7.3), (7.9)--(7.12):

`https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0611041`

For the Fricke-level-six Apéry parametrization itself, see also Cynthia Bortolotto and Lucas Oliveira, “Modular Forms and Numerical Explorations of Rational Approximations to \(\zeta(3)\),” arXiv:2605.00673v1 (1 May 2026), especially the level-six construction. This is a current preprint; I have not verified peer-reviewed publication status:

`https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00673`

# 6. An explicit finite coefficient formula

Maier's equation (7.3) gives a finite formula for the coefficients of \(h_6\). Write

\[
h_6(T)=\sum_{\ell\ge0}c_\ell T^\ell,
\qquad d_\ell:=72^\ell c_\ell.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
d_\ell=
\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}(-8)^k
\sum_{r=0}^{\ell-k}\binom{\ell-k}{r}^3.
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

This already expresses the canonical Heun coefficients as a finite binomial/Franel sum.

## 6.1 Coefficients in the \(T\)-coordinate

Define

\[
U(T):=\sqrt{\frac{D(T)}{72}}h_6(T)=\sum_{m\ge0}u_mT^m.
\]

Since

\[
\sqrt{\frac{D(T)}{72}}
=(1+T/8)^{1/2}(1+T/9)^{1/2},
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{
u_m=
\sum_{\ell+a+b=m}c_\ell
\binom{1/2}{a}8^{-a}
\binom{1/2}{b}9^{-b}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Similarly, write

\[
V(T):=\frac{D(T)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{72}(72-T^2)}h_6(T)
=\sum_{m\ge0}v_mT^m.
\]

Because

\[
V(T)=(1+T/8)^{3/2}(1+T/9)^{3/2}
(1-T^2/72)^{-1}h_6(T),
\]

we obtain

\[
\boxed{
v_m=
\sum_{\ell+a+b+2c=m}c_\ell
\binom{3/2}{a}8^{-a}
\binom{3/2}{b}9^{-b}72^{-c}.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

## 6.2 Lagrange inversion back to \(x\)

The equation \(x=T/D(T)\) is equivalent to

\[
T=xD(T).
\tag{6.4}
\]

For \(j\ge1\), Lagrange--Bürmann inversion yields

\[
[x^j]W(T(x))
=\frac1j[T^{j-1}]W'(T)D(T)^j.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Put

\[
C_{j,s}:=[T^s](T+8)^j(T+9)^j.
\]

Explicitly,

\[
\boxed{
C_{j,s}=\sum_{a+b=s}
\binom ja\binom jb\,8^{j-a}9^{j-b}.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

Applying (6.5) to \(U\) and \(V\) gives

\[
\boxed{
\tau_j=\frac1j\sum_{m=1}^{j}m\,u_m C_{j,j-m},
}
\tag{6.7}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\sigma_j=\frac1j\sum_{m=1}^{j}m\,v_m C_{j,j-m}.
}
\tag{6.8}
\]

Equations (6.1)--(6.3), (6.6)--(6.8) are a completely explicit finite nested-binomial formula for both coefficient sequences. No unproved special-function identification enters this formula.

As a normalization check,

\[
c_1=-\frac1{12},\qquad u_1=\frac5{144},
\qquad v_1=\frac{13}{48}.
\]

Since \(T(x)=72x+O(x^2)\), this gives

\[
\tau_1=72u_1=\frac52,
\qquad
\sigma_1=72v_1=\frac{39}{2},
\]

as required.

# 7. Exact finite-field reduction of the quarter coefficients

Let \(p>3\), and put

\[
J=\left\lfloor\frac{p-1}{4}\right\rfloor.
\]

Every denominator in (6.1)--(6.8) is a \(p\)-unit. Therefore all formulas reduce literally modulo \(p\).

Define \(\bar c_\ell,\bar u_m,\bar v_m\in\mathbf F_p\) by (6.1)--(6.3), and define \(\bar C_{J,s}\) by (6.6). Then

\[
\boxed{
\tau_J\equiv
J^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{J}m\bar u_m\bar C_{J,J-m}\pmod p,
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\sigma_J\equiv
J^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{J}m\bar v_m\bar C_{J,J-m}\pmod p.
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

This is already a precise truncated-hypergeometric/binomial identity. The empirical quarter law is exactly the assertion that these explicit sums have the following behavior:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
p\pmod{24}&\text{relevant sum}&\text{predicted value}\\ \hline
1&\tau_{(p-1)/4}&\ne0\\
5&\tau_{(p-1)/4}&=0\\
19&\sigma_{(p-3)/4}&\ne0\\
23&\sigma_{(p-3)/4}&=0.
\end{array}
\tag{7.3}
\]

For the first two rows,

\[
J=\frac{p-1}{4}\equiv-\frac14\pmod p,
\]

so

\[
\binom Ja\equiv\binom{-1/4}{a}\pmod p.
\tag{7.4}
\]

For the last two rows,

\[
J=\frac{p-3}{4}\equiv-\frac34\pmod p,
\]

and

\[
\binom Ja\equiv\binom{-3/4}{a}\pmod p.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Thus the outer Lagrange kernel itself has fixed quarter parameters modulo \(p\). The remaining inner coefficients still come from the level-six Heun/Gauss pullback.

## 7.1 A power-sum/Cartier form

Let

\[
U_{\le J}(T)=\sum_{m=0}^{J}u_mT^m,
\qquad
V_{\le J}(T)=\sum_{m=0}^{J}v_mT^m.
\]

Lagrange inversion gives

\[
J\tau_J=[T^{J-1}]U_{\le J}'(T)D(T)^J,
\tag{7.6}
\]

\[
J\sigma_J=[T^{J-1}]V_{\le J}'(T)D(T)^J.
\tag{7.7}
\]

The polynomials on the right have degree at most \(3J-1<p-1\). Multiplicative orthogonality in \(\mathbf F_p\) therefore yields the exact identities

\[
\boxed{
J\tau_J
=-\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}
 t^{-(J-1)}U_{\le J}'(t)D(t)^J,
}
\tag{7.8}
\]

\[
\boxed{
J\sigma_J
=-\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}
 t^{-(J-1)}V_{\le J}'(t)D(t)^J.
}
\tag{7.9}
\]

For \(p\equiv1\pmod4\), define the \(\mathbf F_p\)-valued quartic character

\[
\chi_4(t)=t^{(p-1)/4}.
\]

Then (7.8) becomes

\[
\boxed{
J\tau_J
=-\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p^\times}
 t\,\chi_4(t)^{-1}\chi_4(D(t))
 U_{\le J}'(t).
}
\tag{7.10}
\]

This is an exact quartic-character-weighted sum. But it is not a classical Jacobi sum: the factor \(U_{\le J}'(t)\) is a \(p\)-dependent truncation of a modular Heun solution.

For \(p\equiv3\pmod4\), no quartic character exists on \(\mathbf F_p^\times\), so the analogous \(\sigma\)-identity cannot even be put into this form over \(\mathbf F_p\) without extension of scalars.

# 8. Why a direct Greene/Jacobi-sum proof is invalid

## 8.1 Evaluation is not coefficient extraction

The modular formula (5.13) represents \(h_6(T)\) as a Gauss function **evaluated** at the rational function

\[
\Lambda(T)=
\frac{1728T(T+8)^3(T+9)^2}{\mathcal N(T)^3}.
\]

But \(\tau_J\) is

\[
[x^J]\left(\sqrt{D(T(x))/72}\,h_6(T(x))\right),
\]

not \(h_6(T_0)\) at any fixed \(T_0\). Formula (7.6) shows that it is a Cartier-type coefficient of a nonlinear pullback, mixing all coefficients through level \(J\).

A Greene finite hypergeometric value computes a finite-field period/Frobenius trace at a field parameter. It does not automatically compute a Taylor coefficient after Lagrange inversion. A theorem identifying these two operations is required and has not been established here.

## 8.2 The character denominators do not fit the vanishing primes

A naive Greene analogue of the universal Gauss function would use

\[
A=\chi_{12},\qquad B=\chi_{12}^{5},
\qquad C=\varepsilon,
\tag{8.1}
\]

where \(\chi_{12}\) is a multiplicative character of order twelve.

Such a character over \(\mathbf F_p\) exists only if

\[
12\mid p-1.
\]

But:

- if \(p\equiv5\pmod{24}\), then \(p-1\equiv4\pmod{24}\);
- if \(p\equiv23\pmod{24}\), then \(p-1\equiv22\pmod{24}\).

Thus **neither vanishing class has an order-twelve character over \(\mathbf F_p\)**.

Order-twelve characters do exist over \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\), since \(24\mid p^2-1\) for these primes. A possible finite-field object is therefore

\[
{}_2F_1\!\left(\chi_{12},\chi_{12}^{5};\varepsilon;\Lambda(T)\right)_{p^2},
\tag{8.2}
\]

with the quartic gauge supplied by \(\chi_{12}^3\). But a proof still needs:

1. a precise normalization compatible with (5.13);
2. Frobenius descent from \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\) to the relevant \(\mathbf F_p\)-coefficient;
3. a Cartier/coefficient-extraction identity linking (8.2) to (7.1) or (7.2);
4. evaluation or nonvanishing of the resulting descended Jacobi sum.

This is not a cosmetic issue. Frits Beukers explicitly identifies the denominator-divides-\(q-1\) restriction and develops extensions designed to handle it in “Fields of Definition of Finite Hypergeometric Functions,” *2017 MATRIX Annals*, Vol. 2 (2019), pp. 391--400, DOI
`10.1007/978-3-030-04161-8_26`, arXiv:1805.02863.

For Greene's normalization and Jacobi-sum definition, see John Greene, “Hypergeometric functions over finite fields,” *Transactions of the American Mathematical Society* **301** (1987), 77--101, DOI
`10.1090/S0002-9947-1987-0879564-8`.

## 8.3 Exact status of the Jacobi route

The direct implication

\[
\text{classical Gauss pullback}
\Longrightarrow
\text{quarter coefficient equals one Greene value}
\]

is false as a formal step. The missing bridge is not a routine transformation formula; it is a statement about the Cartier operator on a modular pullback.

A legitimate target is the following.

> **[GAP-CARTIER]** Construct a rank-two finite-field realization of the level-six equation for which \(\tau_J\) (respectively \(\sigma_J\)) is an explicit entry of the Cartier/Hasse matrix, and prove that after the correct Fricke twist this entry is a normalized trace or Jacobi sum over \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\).

Only after [GAP-CARTIER] is proved does it make sense to ask for a Greene/Jacobi evaluation yielding the mod-24 law.

# 9. Arithmetic interpretation of the mod-24 classes

On the relevant two classes for each branch, the empirical rule is equivalent to

\[
\boxed{
\text{vanishing}\iff\left(\frac{-3}{p}\right)=-1.
}
\tag{9.1}
\]

Indeed:

- on \(p\equiv1,5\pmod{24}\), the \(\tau\)-quarter position is integral, and \((-3/p)=-1\) selects class \(5\);
- on \(p\equiv19,23\pmod{24}\), the \(\sigma\)-quarter position is integral, and \((-3/p)=-1\) selects class \(23\).

This is an exact reformulation of the empirical table, not a proof of it.

There is an important genus-theory caution. The primitive reduced forms of discriminant \(-24\) are represented by

\[
X^2+6Y^2,\qquad 2X^2+3Y^2.
\]

For odd primes not dividing \(6\), the form \(2X^2+3Y^2\) represents the split nonprincipal classes \(5,11\pmod{24}\), not the union \(5,23\). For example, \(23\) is not of the form \(2X^2+3Y^2\). The \(p\equiv23\) phenomenon belongs to the other square-root branch and involves a Fricke/twist distinction; it cannot be merged with \(p\equiv5\) by a single elementary representation law.

Thus the likely arithmetic structure is **branch character plus the quadratic character \((-3/p)\)**, not one unqualified quadratic-form representation theorem.

Prove the quarter law

# 11. Recommended next computation

The most discriminating experiment is not another raw quarter-zero table. It is a direct Cartier comparison.

For each prime \(p<5000\):

1. form the truncated level-six polynomial
   \[
   U_p(T)=\sum_{m=0}^{J}u_mT^m
   \quad\text{or}\quad
   V_p(T)=\sum_{m=0}^{J}v_mT^m;
   \]
2. verify (7.8)/(7.9) independently;
3. over \(\mathbf F_{p^2}\), choose an order-twelve character \(\chi_{12}\);
4. compute Greene/Beukers--Cohen--Mellit values for
   \[
   (\chi_{12},\chi_{12}^{5};\varepsilon)
   \]
   at the level-six modular parameter, with all Frobenius conjugates and plausible quartic gauges;
5. regress the exact \(\mathbf F_p\) quarter coefficient against trace, norm, and the two Frobenius eigenspaces of these values.

The decisive output is whether there is a **single normalization, independent of \(p\)**, such that

\[
\tau_J\quad\text{or}\quad\sigma_J
\]

is one fixed Cartier/trace expression. A positive result would turn [GAP-CARTIER] into a concrete theorem target. A negative result would show that even the extension-field Gauss value is not the correct finite-field avatar and that one must work directly with the Heun local system.

# Final assessment

The initial “solve the recurrence by finding \(A,B,C,\lambda\)” program is decisively wrong: the coefficient sequences are not hypergeometric terms, nor sums of two such terms. The right rank-two object is a **modular Heun local system**.

At the analytic/modular level this local system is unusually explicit: it descends from the universal Gauss parameters \((1/12,5/12;1)\), and its coefficients have exact finite binomial formulas. At the finite-field level, however, the empirical quarter law concerns a Cartier coefficient after an algebraic Fricke pullback, not an ordinary Gauss value. The extension-field order-twelve hypergeometric system is a plausible source, but a precise Cartier/descent theorem is still missing.

Therefore the honest result is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{quarter-zero law}
\ \Longleftarrow\ 
\text{an explicit level-six Cartier/Jacobi identity}
\quad\text{[GAP],}
}
\]

with every analytic, modular, and finite-sum ingredient on the left side of that gap now explicit.
