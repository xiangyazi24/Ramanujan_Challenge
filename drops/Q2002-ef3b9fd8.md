# P3.2: exact no-go for balanced recurrence / multi-label / Pluecker descendants

## Verdict

The broad statement “every fixed-order recurrence descendant is forced to retain the Padé gcd carrier” is **false**.  The exact invariant is not fixed order but **balance with respect to the cardinal vector** `lambda`.

Let the label set have at least two elements and write

\[
\lambda=(\lambda_j)_j\in\mathbf Z^m,
\qquad \gcd_j\lambda_j=1,
\qquad
c_j=\lambda_j(B-b_j),
\]

\[
D=\gcd_j c_j,
\qquad
\Delta_{ij}=\lambda_i c_j-\lambda_j c_i,
\qquad
R=\gcd_{i<j}\Delta_{ij}>0.
\]

Then the exact balanced-value lattice is

\[
\boxed{
\left\{
\sum_j u_jc_j:\ u_j\in\mathbf Z,
\ \sum_j u_j\lambda_j=0
\right\}=R\mathbf Z .
}
\tag{1}
\]

This equality is integral and saturated.  Consequently:

* every integer recurrence row whose coefficient vector annihilates `lambda` gives a scalar in `R Z`;
* every recurrence obtained as an integral **left multiple** of such a row remains in `R Z`;
* every recurrence row stays there after division by the gcd of its integer coefficients;
* for the normalized labels
  \[
  x_j:=c_j/\lambda_j=B-b_j,
  \]
  a finite stencil \(\sum_r a_r x_{j+r}\), after clearing denominators against the `lambda`'s, is balanced **iff** \(\sum_r a_r=0\); thus genuine finite differences of `x` are trapped, while a general fixed-order operator is not;
* more generally, every integral polynomial descendant belonging to the saturated Pluecker ideal
  \[
  I_\lambda=(\lambda_iX_j-\lambda_jX_i:i<j)
  \subset\mathbf Z[X_1,\ldots,X_m]
  \tag{2}
  \]
  remains in that ideal after primitive **coefficient** normalization, and every nonzero specialization at \(X=c\) is a multiple of `R`.

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
F\in I_\lambda
\iff
F(t\lambda_1,\ldots,t\lambda_m)\equiv0
\text{ in }\mathbf Z[t].
}
\tag{3}
\]

So (3), not “fixed order”, is the broadest clean symbolic criterion: an integral descendant is trapped exactly when it universally kills the rank-one / common-`B` direction.

In the Apéry cardinal application the accepted fact in the question is

\[
\prod_{\lceil n/2\rceil<p\le n}p\mid R.
\]

Hence every nonzero scalar descendant in the class above satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\log|F(c)|\ge\log R
\ge \left(\frac12+o(1)\right)n .
}
\tag{4}
\]

The same applies to a nonzero vector descendant coordinatewise: if all coordinates lie in `I_lambda`, at least one nonzero coordinate has logarithmic height at least `(1/2+o(1))n`.

There is a genuine counterexample **inside the repository's Apéry cardinal normalization** to any broader claim.  At `n=4`, `M=floor(n/2)=2`, the exact cardinal recurrence in `q32_pade_minor_gcd.py` gives

\[
(\lambda_0,\lambda_1)=(-3,4),
\qquad
B=b_4=33001,
\qquad
(b_0,b_1)=(1,5),
\]

so

\[
(c_0,c_1)=(-99000,131984),
\qquad D=8,
\qquad R=\Delta_{01}=48.
\]

The primitive first-order raw finite difference has coefficient vector `(-1,1)` and value

\[
c_1-c_0=230984\equiv8\pmod{48}.
\]

Thus it is **not** an `R`-multiple.  Its imbalance is exactly

\[
(-1)\lambda_0+1\lambda_1=7\ne0.
\]

By contrast the recurrence-adapted first-order row is `(4,3)`:

\[
4\lambda_0+3\lambda_1=0,
\qquad
4c_0+3c_1=-48.
\]

This example pinpoints the correct theorem and rules out the overly broad one.

The escape routes are therefore real but sharply delimited.  Unbalanced **integral** combinations escape the `R` obstruction while remaining automatically divisible by `D`.  Rational division and exterior-power quotients can escape even `D`; integrality of the quotient alone is not enough.  Such a quotient can be certified to remain a `D`-multiple without knowing `D` only if one has an independent ideal-membership certificate after division (for example, an explicit integral combination of the `c_j`), or a separate structural proof that the divisor is coprime to `D`.  In particular, dividing a Pluecker coordinate by its known `lambda_i lambda_j` factor is not safe: in the same `n=4` example,

\[
\frac{\Delta_{01}}{\lambda_0\lambda_1}
=b_0-b_1=-4,
\]

which is not divisible by `D=8`.

That is the rigorously scoped no-go.

# 2. The balanced lattice is exactly `R Z`

Define

\[
\mathcal B_\lambda(c)
:=
\left\{
 u\cdot c:\ u\in\mathbf Z^m,\ u\cdot\lambda=0
\right\}.
\tag{7}
\]

This is an ideal of `Z`, hence of the form `q Z` for a unique `q>=0`.

## Theorem 2.1

If `gcd_j lambda_j=1`, then

\[
\boxed{\mathcal B_\lambda(c)=R\mathbf Z.}
\tag{8}
\]

If all pair coordinates vanish, take `R=0`; then both sides are `{0}`.

## Proof

For every pair `i<j`, put

\[
u^{ij}=\lambda_i e_j-\lambda_j e_i.
\]

Then

\[
u^{ij}\cdot\lambda=0
\]

and

\[
u^{ij}\cdot c
=\lambda_i c_j-\lambda_jc_i
=\Delta_{ij}.
\]

Thus every `Delta_ij` belongs to the balanced lattice.  By Bézout among the finite set of `Delta_ij`, their gcd `R` is an integer combination of them, so

\[
R\mathbf Z\subseteq\mathcal B_\lambda(c).
\tag{9}
\]

For the reverse inclusion, primitivity of `lambda` gives a Bézout vector `v in Z^m` with

\[
v\cdot\lambda=1.
\tag{10}
\]

Let `u·lambda=0`.  Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
u\cdot c
&=u\cdot\bigl(c-(v\cdot c)\lambda\bigr)\\
&=\sum_j u_j
\sum_i v_i(\lambda_i c_j-\lambda_jc_i)\\
&=\sum_{i,j}v_i u_j\Delta_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]

Every term on the right is divisible by `R`.  Hence

\[
\mathcal B_\lambda(c)\subseteq R\mathbf Z.
\]

Together with (9), this proves (8). `square`

### Smith-form interpretation

The same theorem is the rank-two Smith statement for

\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_1&\cdots&\lambda_m\\
c_1&\cdots&c_m
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{12}
\]

The first Smith invariant is `gcd(lambda_j)=1`; the product of the first two is the gcd of the `2 x 2` minors, namely `R`.  Thus the second invariant is exactly `R`.  The balanced lattice is precisely the second Smith direction.

This also explains why adding more labels cannot lower the obstruction while remaining balanced: it merely adds more generators to the same saturated kernel and more minors whose gcd is still the second determinantal divisor.

# 3. Primitive normalization of an integer recurrence row does not help

Let

\[
y=\sum_j u_jc_j,
\qquad
u\cdot\lambda=0,
\tag{13}
\]

and let

\[
g=\gcd_j u_j,
\qquad
u=g u^{\rm prim}.
\]

Then

\[
0=u\cdot\lambda=g(u^{\rm prim}\cdot\lambda),
\]

so, over `Z`,

\[
u^{\rm prim}\cdot\lambda=0.
\tag{14}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_j u^{\rm prim}_j c_j\in R\mathbf Z.
}
\tag{15}
\]

Thus dividing the **integer coefficient vector** by its gcd cannot divide away `R`.

This is exactly the normalization relevant to a recurrence row after denominators have been cleared.  It must not be confused with dividing the **numerical output** by a gcd/content detected after evaluation; that latter operation is an exterior or value-level quotient and is discussed below.

# 4. Exact fixed-order recurrence criterion

There are two natural objects one might finite-difference, and they have different balance tests.

## 4.1 A raw stencil on `c`

Let a finite-band row at a given label be

\[
(\mathcal Lc)_j
=\sum_{r=0}^q a_r(j)c_{j+r},
\qquad a_r(j)\in\mathbf Z.
\tag{16}
\]

Then Theorem 2.1 applies **iff**

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{r=0}^q a_r(j)\lambda_{j+r}=0.
}
\tag{17}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
(\mathcal L\lambda)_j=0.
\]

So the exact recurrence statement is:

> **Every integer finite-band operator that annihilates `lambda` is trapped by `R`; finite order by itself says nothing.**

If a recurrence has rational coefficients, multiply its row by any common denominator first.  Dividing the resulting integer row by its coefficient gcd then preserves (17), by Section 3.

If `L lambda=0`, every integral **left multiple** `A L` also annihilates `lambda`, hence every output of `A L c` is an `R`-multiple.  More generally, any composed operator is covered precisely when its final coefficient row annihilates `lambda`; an Ore-polynomial slogan is not enough unless that annihilation is checked.

## 4.2 A stencil after recurrence normalization by `lambda`

Put

\[
x_j:=\frac{c_j}{\lambda_j}=B-b_j\in\mathbf Z.
\tag{18}
\]

Consider

\[
S_j=\sum_{r=0}^q a_r(j)x_{j+r},
\qquad a_r(j)\in\mathbf Q.
\tag{19}
\]

Choose a nonzero integer `Q_j` clearing all coefficient and `lambda` denominators so that

\[
u_{j+r}:=Q_j\frac{a_r(j)}{\lambda_{j+r}}\in\mathbf Z.
\]

Then

\[
Q_jS_j=\sum_r u_{j+r}c_{j+r}
\tag{20}
\]

and

\[
\sum_r u_{j+r}\lambda_{j+r}
=Q_j\sum_r a_r(j).
\tag{21}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
Q_jS_j\in R\mathbf Z
\iff
\sum_{r=0}^q a_r(j)=0.
}
\tag{22}
\]

After primitive normalization of the integer coefficient row `u`, the same equivalence remains.

For a constant-coefficient shift polynomial

\[
P(E)=\sum_{r=0}^q a_rE^r,
\]

this is simply

\[
\boxed{P(1)=0.}
\tag{23}
\]

Thus genuine finite differences of the normalized data `x=B-b` are trapped because they kill constants.  An arbitrary fixed-order operator with `P(1) != 0` is not.

This is the cleanest recurrence-normalized formulation of the no-go.

# 5. The actual cardinal recurrence is balanced

From (6), with `lambda_j=L_j(n)`, one has the exact first-order relation

\[
(n-j)(M-1-j)\lambda_j
+(j+1)(n-j-1)\lambda_{j+1}=0.
\tag{24}
\]

Therefore the corresponding recurrence descendant

\[
\boxed{
(n-j)(M-1-j)c_j
+(j+1)(n-j-1)c_{j+1}
\in R\mathbf Z.
}
\tag{25}
\]

Any common gcd of the two integer coefficients may be removed: the primitive row still annihilates `lambda`.

This is the precise sense in which a **recurrence-adapted** first-order construction is closed by the no-go.

An ordinary difference

\[
c_{j+1}-c_j
\]

has coefficient row `(-1,1)` and is balanced only if

\[
\lambda_{j+1}=\lambda_j,
\]

which is false generically for the cardinal weights.  That distinction is not cosmetic; it gives the concrete counterexample in Section 8.

# 6. Broad polynomial / finite-difference / Pluecker no-go

The linear theorem has an exact nonlinear saturation.

Let

\[
S=\mathbf Z[X_1,\ldots,X_m]
\]

and define

\[
I_\lambda
=(\lambda_iX_j-\lambda_jX_i:i<j).
\tag{26}
\]

## Theorem 6.1: exact rank-one ideal

Because `lambda` is primitive,

\[
\boxed{
I_\lambda
=\ker\left(
S\longrightarrow\mathbf Z[T],
\quad X_j\longmapsto\lambda_jT
\right).
}
\tag{27}
\]

Moreover `S/I_lambda` is a polynomial ring over `Z`; in particular `I_lambda` is prime and is saturated with respect to every nonzero integer:

\[
\boxed{
qF\in I_\lambda,\ q\in\mathbf Z\setminus\{0\}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F\in I_\lambda.
}
\tag{28}
\]

## Proof

Since `gcd(lambda_j)=1`, there is a unimodular matrix `U in GL_m(Z)` such that

\[
\lambda U=(1,0,\ldots,0).
\tag{29}
\]

Apply the corresponding integral linear change of variables

\[
Y=XU.
\]

Column operations by `U` send the `2 x 2` minors of

\[
\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\\X\end{pmatrix}
\]

to the `2 x 2` minors of

\[
\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\Y_1&Y_2&\cdots&Y_m\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Thus `I_lambda` is carried to

\[
(Y_2,\ldots,Y_m).
\tag{30}
\]

The quotient is

\[
\mathbf Z[Y_1],
\]

which proves (27) and has no nonzero integer torsion, proving (28). `square`

### Consequence 6.2: primitive polynomial normalization

Suppose an integral symbolic descendant `F(X)` lies in `I_lambda`, and all its integer coefficients have common factor `g`.  Write

\[
F=gF^{\rm prim}.
\]

By (28),

\[
\boxed{F^{\rm prim}\in I_\lambda.}
\tag{31}
\]

Thus primitive **coefficient-content** normalization cannot leave the trapped class.

The same holds coordinatewise for vector-valued descendants.

### Consequence 6.3: evaluation at the Padé carrier

At `X=c`, every generator becomes

\[
\lambda_ic_j-\lambda_jc_i=\Delta_{ij}.
\]

Since every `Delta_ij` is divisible by `R`, every element of the ideal evaluates to an `R`-multiple:

\[
\boxed{
F\in I_\lambda
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F(c_1,\ldots,c_m)\in R\mathbf Z.
}
\tag{32}
\]

Equations (27) and (32) give the broadest precise symbolic no-go available from these definitions:

> **Any integral polynomial construction that vanishes identically on the rank-one line `X=t lambda` is forced to retain `R`, even after primitive normalization of its integer polynomial coefficients.**

This class includes, without requiring any fixed-order assumption:

* every balanced integral linear form;
* every finite-difference or recurrence row whose coefficient vector annihilates `lambda`;
* every integral left multiple or integer combination of such recurrence rows;
* every pair Pluecker coordinate;
* every integer linear combination or finite difference of Pluecker coordinates;
* every polynomial in the `Delta_ij` with zero constant term;
* every determinant or multi-label polynomial that belongs to the rank-one ideal;
* arbitrary integral polynomial multiples and sums of the preceding objects.

The phrase “belongs to the rank-one ideal” is essential.  Merely being assembled from a bounded number of neighboring labels is not enough.

# 7. Height theorem in the Apéry cardinal application

Assume the accepted cardinal fact

\[
\prod_{\lceil n/2\rceil<p\le n}p\mid R.
\tag{33}
\]

The prime number theorem in Chebyshev form gives

\[
\sum_{\lceil n/2\rceil<p\le n}\log p
=\frac n2+o(n).
\tag{34}
\]

Hence

\[
\log R\ge\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)n.
\tag{35}
\]

## Theorem 7.1: balanced-descendant height no-go

Let `F_n` be any sequence of integral scalar descendants such that, for each `n`, after all denominator clearing and primitive **integer coefficient** normalization,

\[
F_n\in I_{\lambda(n)}.
\]

If

\[
F_n(c(n))\ne0,
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
\log|F_n(c(n))|
\ge
\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)n.
}
\tag{36}
\]

No bounded-order, fixed-degree, finite-difference, recurrence, or multi-label optimization inside this class can yield `exp(o(n))` height.

For a vector output all of whose coordinates lie in `I_lambda`, if the evaluated vector is nonzero, then

\[
\boxed{
\log\max_k |F_{n,k}(c(n))|
\ge
\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)n.
}
\tag{37}
\]

The qualification “evaluated output is nonzero” is necessary: a symbolic descendant may vanish at special data, and zero has no positive logarithmic-height lower bound.

This theorem is stronger than a “fixed-order no-go”: order is irrelevant once ideal membership is known.

# 8. Genuine counterexample to the overly broad claim

The counterexample can be taken directly from the repository's cardinal formula; no synthetic `lambda` is needed.

Take

\[
n=4,
\qquad M=2.
\]

From (5),

\[
\lambda_0=-\binom31=-3.
\]

From (6),

\[
\lambda_1
=-(-3)\frac{4\cdot1}{1\cdot3}=4.
\]

The Apéry values are

\[
b_0=1,
\qquad b_1=5,
\qquad B=b_4=33001.
\]

Therefore

\[
c_0=-3(33001-1)=-99000,
\]

\[
c_1=4(33001-5)=131984.
\]

The carrier gcd is

\[
D=\gcd(99000,131984)=8.
\tag{38}
\]

There is only one pair coordinate, so

\[
R=|\Delta_{01}|.
\]

Directly,

\[
\Delta_{01}
=(-3)(131984)-4(-99000)
=48.
\tag{39}
\]

Now apply the fixed first-order finite-difference operator `E-1` to the **raw carrier entries**:

\[
(E-1)c_0=c_1-c_0=230984.
\]

But

\[
230984=48\cdot4812+8,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{R\nmid(c_1-c_0).}
\tag{40}
\]

The coefficient vector `(-1,1)` is already primitive.  Its balance defect is

\[
(-1)(-3)+1\cdot4=7.
\tag{41}
\]

Thus primitive normalization does not rescue the broad claim: this is a fixed-order primitive recurrence/difference descendant and it is not trapped by `R`.

Now compare the **cardinal-recurrence-adapted** row.  At `n=4,j=0`, (24) gives coefficients `(4,3)`:

\[
4(-3)+3(4)=0.
\]

Applying it to `c` gives

\[
4(-99000)+3(131984)=-48.
\tag{42}
\]

This is exactly an `R`-multiple, as the theorem predicts.

The same contrast is visible after normalization by `lambda`.  Put

\[
x_0=c_0/\lambda_0=33000,
\qquad
x_1=c_1/\lambda_1=32996.
\]

Then

\[
12(x_1-x_0)
=4c_0+3c_1
=-48.
\tag{43}
\]

So the normalized first difference, after integral denominator clearing, is balanced and trapped; the raw first difference is not.

This is the exact counterexample/boundary the no-go must preserve.

# 9. Relation to `D`: what remains certifiably a gcd multiple?

Since

\[
D=\gcd_j c_j,
\]

one always has

\[
D\mid c_j
\quad\text{for every }j.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{D\mid\Delta_{ij}\quad\text{and hence}\quad D\mid R.}
\tag{44}
\]

But `D` has a much larger safe class than `R`.

## 9.1 Any unbalanced integral combination is still a `D`-multiple

For arbitrary `u_j in Z`, with no balance condition,

\[
\sum_j u_jc_j\in D\mathbf Z.
\tag{45}
\]

Thus an unbalanced integral construction is a genuine route around the **linear-height `R` obstruction** while preserving a certified multiple of the original gcd `D`.

Primitive normalization of its integer coefficient vector is still safe: after division by the coefficient gcd it remains an explicit integral combination of the `c_j`.

More generally, every integral polynomial in the `c_j` with zero constant term belongs to the integer ideal

\[
(c_1,\ldots,c_m)=D\mathbf Z
\]

after evaluation, so it is a certified `D`-multiple.

The `n=4` counterexample illustrates the distinction perfectly:

\[
R=48\nmid(c_1-c_0),
\qquad
D=8\mid(c_1-c_0).
\]

Therefore the balanced no-go does **not** close unbalanced integral carriers.

# 10. Rational division: coefficient division is safe; value division is not

Suppose an integral numerator `N` is known to be a `D`-multiple and a known nonzero integer `q` divides `N`, so

\[
Y=N/q\in\mathbf Z.
\]

From these facts alone one cannot infer

\[
D\mid Y.
\]

The denominator may remove a prime factor of `D`.

There are three useful sufficient certificates that do not require first computing `D` as an integer.

### Certificate A: explicit ideal membership after division

If one can exhibit integers `v_j` such that

\[
\boxed{Y=\sum_jv_jc_j,}
\tag{46}
\]

then `D|Y` immediately.  This is the cleanest certificate.

For polynomial outputs, the analogous sufficient condition is an explicit integral polynomial representation with zero constant term in the `c_j`.

### Certificate B: coefficientwise division

If

\[
N=\sum_j u_jc_j
\]

and `q` divides every coefficient `u_j`, then

\[
Y=\sum_j(u_j/q)c_j
\]

is again an integral combination.  This is exactly why primitive **coefficient-vector** normalization is safe.

### Certificate C: structural coprimality

If one independently proves

\[
\gcd(q,D)=1,
\]

then `D|N` and `q|N` imply `D|N/q`.

One can certify this without explicitly writing `D`, for example by proving that for every prime dividing `q` at least one `c_j` is a unit at that prime.  But this is genuine extra gcd-avoidance information; it does not follow from integrality of the quotient.

Absent one of these mechanisms, rational division is outside the no-go and outside automatic `D` preservation.

# 11. Exterior-power quotients are a real escape and can destroy `D`

The column vectors

\[
v_j=(\lambda_j,c_j)\in\mathbf Z^2
\]

have exterior coordinates

\[
v_i\wedge v_j=\Delta_{ij}.
\]

The numerical exterior vector

\[
(\Delta_{ij})_{i<j}
\]

has content exactly `R`.  Dividing it by that **evaluated numerical content** is therefore a quotient operation, not primitive normalization of an underlying integer coefficient row.  It deliberately removes the obstruction and is outside Theorems 2.1 and 6.1.

More importantly, even division by a completely known symbolic exterior factor can remove `D`.

From

\[
\Delta_{ij}=\lambda_i\lambda_j(b_i-b_j),
\]

one has the integral quotient

\[
\frac{\Delta_{ij}}{\lambda_i\lambda_j}=b_i-b_j.
\tag{47}
\]

In the actual `n=4` cardinal example,

\[
\lambda_0\lambda_1=-12,
\qquad
\Delta_{01}=48,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Delta_{01}}{\lambda_0\lambda_1}
=-4.
}
\tag{48}
\]

But `D=8`, hence

\[
\boxed{D\nmid -4.}
\tag{49}
\]

Thus:

> **An integral exterior-power quotient is not automatically a multiple of the original Padé gcd, even when the divided factor is completely explicit and does not involve knowing `D` or `R`.**

To retain a `D` certificate after such a quotient one again needs an independent representation such as (46), or a structural coprimality argument for the divided factor.

This is why one must distinguish two notions of “primitive”:

1. **symbolic coefficient primitivity** inside the saturated rank-one ideal — safe, and still trapped by `R`;
2. **numerical exterior/value primitivity** obtained by dividing evaluated minors by a content or known factor — an actual escape route, but no automatic `D` divisibility survives.

# 12. What the no-go covers, and what it does not

## Covered

The linear-height obstruction is rigorous for every nonzero evaluated integral descendant satisfying any equivalent form of the following condition:

\[
\boxed{
F\in I_\lambda
\iff
F(t\lambda)=0\text{ identically}.
}
\tag{50}
\]

Concretely this includes:

* recurrence rows `u` with `u·lambda=0`;
* fixed-order or variable-order operators `L` with `L lambda=0`;
* integral left multiples and integer combinations of such rows;
* normalized-data finite differences whose stencil coefficients sum to zero;
* all pair Pluecker coordinates;
* all integral polynomial / determinant / finite-difference descendants lying in the Pluecker ideal;
* all of the above after removal of a common **integer coefficient** factor.

Every nonzero scalar output in this class is at least `R` in absolute value, hence has logarithmic height at least `(1/2+o(1))n` in the cardinal application.

## Not covered

The theorem does **not** rule out:

1. **Unbalanced integral combinations.**  They can avoid `R` while still being explicit `D`-multiples.
2. **Operators that do not annihilate `lambda`.**  Fixed order alone gives no obstruction; Section 8 is an actual counterexample.
3. **Normalized-data stencils with nonzero coefficient sum.**  They retain a `B` component and need not lie in `R Z`.
4. **Rational division by a known factor** unless the quotient is independently certified to remain in the integer ideal generated by the `c_j` (or the divisor is independently coprime to `D`).
5. **Exterior-power or subdeterminant quotients.**  Integrality of the quotient does not imply `D` divisibility.
6. **Numerical content normalization of evaluated descendants.**  Dividing an evaluated Pluecker vector by its gcd `R`, or by another value-level common factor, is not the coefficient normalization protected by saturation.
7. **Constructions using extra evaluation-dependent information** that are not polynomial rank-one-killing identities in the original label variables.

These are genuine logical gaps in the no-go, not merely technical omissions.

# 13. Final scoped theorem

The clean statement to bank is the following.

## Theorem

For primitive nonzero `lambda in Z^m`, define

\[
c_j=\lambda_j(B-b_j),
\quad
D=\gcd_jc_j,
\quad
R=\gcd_{i<j}(\lambda_ic_j-\lambda_jc_i).
\]

Then:

1. The balanced integral value lattice is exactly
   \[
   \ker_{\mathbf Z}(\lambda)\cdot c=R\mathbf Z.
   \]
2. Dividing a balanced integer coefficient vector by its coefficient gcd preserves balance and therefore preserves divisibility by `R`.
3. The saturated polynomial closure of the balanced constructions is
   \[
   I_\lambda=(\lambda_iX_j-\lambda_jX_i)
   =\ker(X_j\mapsto\lambda_jT).
   \]
   Primitive integer **polynomial-coefficient** normalization preserves this ideal.
4. Hence every nonzero evaluated integral polynomial/finite-difference/Pluecker descendant in `I_lambda` is a nonzero multiple of `R`.
5. In the Apéry cardinal application, using the accepted top-prime support of `R`, every such nonzero descendant has
   \[
   \log\text{height}\ge(1/2+o(1))n.
   \]
6. The statement cannot be enlarged to “all fixed-order recurrence descendants”: already at `n=4`, the primitive raw first difference `c_1-c_0` is not divisible by `R`.
7. Unbalanced **integral** descendants remain certified `D`-multiples and are not ruled out.  Rational or exterior-power quotients preserve `D` only with an additional post-division ideal-membership or coprimality certificate; integrality alone is insufficient.

That is the broadest no-go justified by the algebra without silently assuming away the actual escape routes.
