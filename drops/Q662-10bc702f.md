# Wronskian and the second Apéry fundamental solution

## Executive conclusion

The observed swap matrix identity is correct, but the proposed implication has the logical direction reversed. The reflection law for the second solution `c_r` does **not** follow formally from the Wronskian alone or from the reflection of `b_r`. It requires a second piece of structure: a symmetry of the full two-dimensional solution space.

The right statement is:

* the recurrence modulo `p` has a reflection involution;
* the involution acts linearly on the two-dimensional solution space;
* the Apéry solution `b` is an eigenvector of this involution;
* the second solution `c` is reflected to `c` plus possibly a multiple of `b`.

The Wronskian fixes the determinant of this action but does not kill the possible shear term. Therefore a proof of `c_r = c_{p-1-r}` needs one additional normalization argument.

---

## 1. Reflection as an operator on the solution space

Let

$$
R(u)_r=u_{p-1-r}.
$$

Because the Apéry recurrence coefficients satisfy the appropriate index reversal identity modulo `p`, `R` sends solutions of the recurrence to solutions. Hence on the two-dimensional solution space

$$
V_p=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf F_p}\{b,c\}
$$
we have a matrix

$$
R\binom bc = \binom bc A
$$

for some constant matrix `A` over `F_p`.

The known reflection law for `b` gives

$$
R(b)=b.
$$

Therefore the first column of `A` is `(1,0)^T`, so

$$
R(c)=\alpha c+\beta b.
$$

Because reflection is an involution,

$$
R^2=1,
$$
we get

$$
\alpha^2=1.
$$

The Wronskian normalization rules out the sign `\alpha=-1` (the determinant of the reflection action on the fundamental solution basis is fixed), leaving

$$
R(c)=c+\beta b.
$$

The remaining question is exactly whether `\beta=0`.

The Wronskian identity alone does not imply this.

---

## 2. Why the Wronskian is insufficient

Suppose

$$
\tilde c=c+\lambda b.
$$

Then

$$
b_r\tilde c_{r-1}-b_{r-1}\tilde c_r
=
 b_rc_{r-1}-b_{r-1}c_r,
$$

because the extra terms cancel.

Hence every shear

$$
c\mapsto c+\lambda b
$$

has exactly the same Wronskian.

Therefore the identity

$$
b_rc_{r-1}-b_{r-1}c_r=-1/r^3
$$

cannot distinguish

$$
R(c)=c
$$
from

$$
R(c)=c+\beta b.
$$

This is the obstruction.

---

## 3. What would prove the reflection law

There are two natural routes.

### Route A: use the Frobenius/symplectic structure

The Apéry equation is a rank-two Picard--Fuchs subsystem. The Frobenius action preserves the polarization (Wronskian pairing). The reflection symmetry is induced by the same geometric correspondence that gives

$$
b_r\equiv b_{p-1-r}\pmod p.
$$

If one proves that this correspondence fixes the chosen integral lattice basis

$$
(b,c),
$$
not merely the line generated by `b`, then

$$
R(c)=c.
$$

This is the missing geometric input.

### Route B: use initial conditions

Let

$$
d_r=c_{p-1-r}-c_r.
$$

If `d` satisfies the same recurrence, then it is enough to prove two initial values vanish. For example, if one can establish

$$
c_{p-1}=c_0=0,
\qquad
c_{p-2}=c_1=1,
$$
then uniqueness gives

$$
c_r=c_{p-1-r}
$$
for every `r`.

But these endpoint identities are precisely what is not obtained from the Wronskian alone.

---

## 4. Consequence for the matrix `M(p-1)`

If both reflection laws hold, then indeed

$$
\Phi=
\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
$$

and

$$
\Phi'=
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\5&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since

$$
\Phi^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&-5\end{pmatrix},
$$

we get

$$
M(p-1)=\Phi'\Phi^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

So the observed swap matrix is equivalent to the two endpoint reflection identities.

---

## 5. If `c` does not reflect

In general write

$$
R(c)=c+\beta b.
$$

Then

$$
\Phi'
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&\beta\\
5&1+5\beta
\end{pmatrix}
$$

and

$$
M(p-1)=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
+
\beta
\begin{pmatrix}1&5\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\Phi^{-1}
$$

(up to the chosen basis convention).

Thus a nonzero shear changes the matrix away from swap. The computational observation that `M(p-1)=swap` for many primes is strong evidence that `\beta=0`, but it is not a formal consequence of the Wronskian.

---

## Final answer

(A) The reflection law for `c` is **not proved by the Wronskian**. The most one gets is

$$
c_{p-1-r}=c_r+\beta_p b_r.
$$

(B) The K3/Picard--Fuchs picture strongly suggests `\beta_p=0`, because both periods should transform under the same geometric involution, but one needs the action on the full integral period lattice, not only on the holomorphic period.

(C) Checking only zeros of `b_r` cannot detect the shear term, because there `\beta b_r=0`. A full-range check of `c_r=c_{p-1-r}` is exactly the required test.

(D) A direct Galois/Frobenius proof would amount to proving that the reflection correspondence acts diagonally on the chosen fundamental basis. This is a plausible geometric route, but it is an additional theorem beyond the currently known Apéry Lucas/reflection identities.
