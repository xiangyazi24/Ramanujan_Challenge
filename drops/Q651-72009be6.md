# Steinberg matrix coefficient bound for Apéry orbit

## Short answer

The proposed Steinberg-sheaf argument does not currently prove AFP. The obstruction is exactly the missing cancellation. Katz/Deligne gives a square-root bound for a fixed nontrivial sheaf:

\[
\left|\sum_r t_{\mathcal G}(r)\right|\ll C\sqrt p.
\]

It does not automatically improve this to O(1). The Apéry data suggest extra structure, but the known Katz machinery does not identify the Steinberg coefficient as a constant sheaf or a telescoping coboundary.

The most likely explanation is a mixture of (2) and (3): the palindromic involution gives additional pairing, and the special Apéry cocycle may kill the main square-root contribution. However, this requires a new argument.

---

# 1. The Steinberg decomposition

For \(G=SL_2(\mathbf F_p)\), the permutation representation on \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\) decomposes as

\[
\mathrm{Ind}_B^G(1)=1\oplus St.
\]

Hence

\[
1_{g v=w}=\frac1{p+1}+\mathrm{St}_{v,w}(g).
\]

Therefore

\[
Z_p=\frac{p-1}{p+1}+\sum_{r=1}^{p-1}\mathrm{St}_{v,w}(M(r)).
\]

AFP is exactly a bound for the Steinberg matrix coefficient sum.

---

# 2. Why the naive Katz estimate gives only O(sqrt(p))

The Steinberg representation has dimension p. It is not a fixed-rank representation as p varies.

If one tries to realize \(St\) as a sheaf obtained from \(\mathrm{Sym}^{p-1}(F)\), the rank grows with p. The conductor is therefore not bounded independently of p.

This is the key issue.

Katz's theorem gives roughly:

\[
\sum_r t_{\rho}(M(r))=O(\mathrm{cond}(\rho)\sqrt p).
\]

For a fixed representation \(\rho\), this is useful. But for Steinberg,

\[
\mathrm{cond}(St(F))
\]

is potentially of size comparable to p. Therefore the direct application is much weaker than AFP.

The "sum over all representations" viewpoint is therefore misleading: the relevant representation is not a bounded-complexity object.

---

# 3. Possibility 1: is it a constant sheaf?

Probably not.

A constant sheaf would imply an exact identity

\[
\sum_r St_{v,w}(M(r))=0
\]

(up to the finitely many exceptional points).

The observed statistics are instead Poisson-like:

\[
Z_p/2\approx \mathrm{Poisson}(1/2).
\]

This indicates small fluctuations, not exact cancellation.

So the Steinberg coefficient is unlikely to be geometrically trivial.

---

# 4. Possibility 2: does the swap matrix telescope?

The swap identity is very important:

\[
M(p-1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

It implies a reflection symmetry of the cocycle. Let

\[
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then the second half of the orbit is related to the first half by an involution involving S.

For the Steinberg coefficient this gives a relation of the form

\[
St_{v,w}(M(p-1-r))
=
St_{Sv,Sw}(M(r))
\]

(up to the precise convention for right/left cocycles).

This can reduce the problem by half. However, it does not imply cancellation unless

\[
St_{Sv,Sw}(g)=-St_{v,w}(g).
\]

For the points involved here there is no obvious reason for this sign relation.

Thus the swap identity explains the evenness of Z_p, but does not by itself prove AFP.

---

# 5. Possibility 3: special vanishing of the sqrt(p) term

This is the most plausible explanation.

The generic Katz bound is

\[
O(\sqrt p).
\]

An O(1) result requires showing that the relevant cohomology group vanishes.

By Grothendieck-Lefschetz:

\[
\sum_r t_{\mathcal G}(r)
=\sum_i(-1)^i\mathrm{Tr}(Frob|H_c^i).
\]

The square-root term comes from weight-one cohomology. To get O(1), one needs the middle cohomology contribution to disappear or have exceptional cancellation.

So the correct formulation is:

AFP would follow if the Steinberg coefficient sheaf satisfies

\[
H_c^1(\mathbf A^1,\mathcal G)=0
\]

or has a controlled one-dimensional contribution.

This is much stronger than monodromy being SL_2.

---

# 6. Explicit Steinberg coefficient

For \(g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\), the Steinberg representation can be realized as functions on \(\mathbf P^1\) with quotient by constants.

The matrix coefficient is essentially a correction of the permutation coefficient:

\[
\mathrm{St}_{x,y}(g)
=
1_{gx=y}-\frac1{p+1}.
\]

Therefore for the Apéry orbit:

\[
\sum_r \mathrm{St}_{[5:1],[0:1]}(M(r))
=
Z_p-\frac{p-1}{p+1}.
\]

This formula is exact, but it does not reveal cancellation because the indicator itself is the hard object.

---

# 7. What feature of Apéry could improve Katz?

The candidates are:

1. **Bounded conductor rank-2 cocycle.**  The original Apéry sheaf has rank 2 and bounded singularity complexity.

2. **Large monodromy.**  \(G_p\supset SL_2\) prevents algebraic concentration.

3. **Palindromic functional equation.**  The swap matrix gives an involution on the orbit.

4. **Purity.**  The trace function has weight zero after normalization.

But none of these alone gives O(1).

The missing theorem would be a bounded-complexity replacement for the Steinberg representation: a way to detect a single projective point without paying rank p.

---

# Conclusion

The three possibilities:

- (1) constant sheaf: unlikely;
- (2) swap telescoping: gives symmetry but not enough cancellation;
- (3) special vanishing of the sqrt(p) contribution: the most plausible mechanism.

The real missing statement is:

> The Apéry rank-2 cocycle has a projective-point detector whose associated trace sheaf has vanishing weight-one cohomology.

If proved, this would upgrade Katz's generic \(\sqrt p\) bound to the observed O(1) AFP behavior.
