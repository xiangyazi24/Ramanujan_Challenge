# Bounded column norm does **not** imply \(\sum R_{ij}=o(N)\)

## Verdict

The proposed statement is false in the strongest possible sense.

There is a deterministic family with

- \(|Z_p|=1\) for every prime \(\sqrt N<p\le N\),
- \(\operatorname{col}(r)\le 1\) for every integer \(r\),
- \(\lambda\to\log 2\),
- no colliding residue-pairs at all,

but

\[
\sum_{p<q}R_{pq}
=\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}.
\]

There is also a reflection-symmetric version with \(|Z_p|\le2\), \(r\in Z_p\iff p-1-r\in Z_p\), and \(\operatorname{col}(r)\le2\), for which the same lower bound holds. Thus reflection symmetry, bounded zero-set size, bounded column norm, injectivity of the hit residues, and even the prime-side condition “one reflection pair per prime” do not prevent a maximal common-row star.

The key logical point is:

> A column norm bounds repetitions of the **same literal residue** across primes. It does not bound the number of different residues of the form \(n_0\bmod p\) that align at one common integer \(n_0\).

Indeed, for \(p,q>\sqrt N\), the map \(p\mapsto N\bmod p\) is injective. That very injectivity makes the counterexample have column norm one while every prime hits the same row \(n=N\).

The exact additional ingredient needed is therefore a **moving-target / congruence-graph mixing theorem**, not a static column bound. At second order it must control intersections with the curves

\[
\Gamma_n=\{(p,n\bmod p):\sqrt N<p\le N\},
\]

or, equivalently, prove the factorial-moment asymptotic

\[
\sum_{n\le N}(B(n))_2
=N\left(\lambda^2-\sigma^2\right)+o(N).
\]

That statement is exactly equivalent to \(\sum_{p<q}R_{pq}=o(N)\).

# 1. A column-norm-one counterexample

Let

\[
\mathcal P_N=\{p\text{ prime}:\sqrt N<p\le N\},
\qquad L=|\mathcal P_N|.
\]

For every \(p\in\mathcal P_N\), define

\[
a_p=N\bmod p\in[0,p),
\qquad Z_p=\{a_p\}.
\]

Then \(|Z_p|=1\), and by the prime number theorem

\[
L=\pi(N)-\pi(\sqrt N)
=\left(1+o(1)\right)\frac{N}{\log N}.
\]

## Lemma 1: the residues \(a_p\) are pairwise distinct

Suppose distinct \(p,q\in\mathcal P_N\) satisfy

\[
a_p=a_q=a.
\]

Then both \(p\) and \(q\) divide \(N-a\), hence

\[
pq\mid N-a.
\]

But \(a<p\le N\), so \(N-a>0\), and

\[
0<N-a\le N<pq,
\]

because \(p,q>\sqrt N\). This is impossible. Therefore \(p\mapsto a_p\) is injective.

Consequently

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{col}(r)\le1\quad\text{for every }r.}
\]

In particular, the number of “colliding pairs” in the terminology of the question is exactly zero:

\[
\sum_r\binom{\operatorname{col}(r)}2=0.
\]

## Lemma 2: every prime hits the common row \(n=N\)

By construction,

\[
N\bmod p=a_p\in Z_p
\]

for every \(p\in\mathcal P_N\). Thus

\[
B(N)=L.
\]

For distinct \(p,q\), the simultaneous congruences

\[
n\equiv a_p\pmod p,
\qquad
n\equiv a_q\pmod q
\]

have one class modulo \(pq\). The integer \(N\) lies in that class. Since

\[
N<pq,
\]

\(N\) is the unique representative of that class in \([0,pq)\). Hence among \(1\le n\le N\), the pair has exactly one hit:

\[
\#\{n\le N:n\bmod p\in Z_p,\ n\bmod q\in Z_q\}=1.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\operatorname{CRT}_{p,q}(a_p,a_q)=N
\]

for every pair \(p\ne q\).

Thus every pair is a **non-colliding residue pair**, yet all CRT values are perfectly aligned.

## Exact residual sum

Here \(|Z_p|=|Z_q|=1\), so

\[
R_{pq}=1-\frac{N}{pq}>0.
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_{p<q}R_{pq}
=\binom L2-N\sum_{p<q}\frac1{pq}.
\]

Put

\[
H_N=\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac1p,
\qquad
Q_N=\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac1{p^2}.
\]

Then

\[
\sum_{p<q}\frac1{pq}=\frac12(H_N^2-Q_N).
\]

Mertens' theorem for primes gives

\[
H_N
=\log\log N-\log\log\sqrt N+o(1)
=\log2+o(1),
\]

while \(Q_N=o(1)\). Hence

\[
N\sum_{p<q}\frac1{pq}=O(N).
\]

On the other hand,

\[
\binom L2
=\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}.
\]

It follows that

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}R_{pq}
=\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}.
}
\]

This is much larger than \(N\), let alone \(o(N)\).

The harmonic mass also has exactly the scale requested in the question:

\[
\lambda=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}\frac{|Z_p|}{p}
=H_N=\log2+o(1)=O(1).
\]

Thus every hypothesis in the formal statement holds with the strongest possible column constant \(C_2=1\), but the conclusion fails by a factor of order \(N/(\log N)^2\).

# 2. Reflection-symmetric counterexample with column norm at most two

The preceding singleton construction already disproves the formal theorem. The following upgrade addresses the Apéry-like conditions \(|Z_p|=2\) and reflection symmetry.

For \(p\in\mathcal P_N\), retain

\[
a_p=N\bmod p
\]

and define its reflection

\[
b_p=p-1-a_p.
\]

Set

\[
Z_p=\{a_p,b_p\}.
\]

If \(a_p=b_p\), this is a singleton; those exceptional primes may be deleted. Indeed,

\[
a_p=b_p
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p\mid 2N+1,
\]

so only \(O(1)\) primes larger than \(\sqrt N\) are exceptional. Deleting them leaves

\[
|Z_p|=2,
\qquad
r\in Z_p\iff p-1-r\in Z_p,
\]

for \((1+o(1))N/\log N\) primes.

## Lemma 3: the reflected residues \(b_p\) are also pairwise distinct

Write

\[
N=k_pp+a_p,
\qquad
k_p=\left\lfloor\frac Np\right\rfloor.
\]

Then

\[
b_p=p-1-a_p=(k_p+1)p-N-1.
\]

Suppose distinct \(p,q\in\mathcal P_N\) satisfy

\[
b_p=b_q=b.
\]

Then

\[
T:=N+b+1=(k_p+1)p=(k_q+1)q.
\]

Thus \(p\) and \(q\) both divide \(T\), so \(pq\mid T\). Since \(b<\min(p,q)\),

\[
0<T=N+b+1<N+\min(p,q)<2N.
\]

But \(pq>N\), hence the only possible positive multiple of \(pq\) below \(2N\) is

\[
T=pq.
\]

It follows that

\[
q=k_p+1,
\qquad
p=k_q+1.
\]

Because \(p>\sqrt N\),

\[
k_p=\left\lfloor\frac Np\right\rfloor<\sqrt N,
\]

and hence \(q<\sqrt N+1\). Similarly \(p<\sqrt N+1\). As integers satisfying \(p,q>\sqrt N\), both would have to equal the unique possible integer \(\lceil\sqrt N\rceil\), contradicting \(p\ne q\). Therefore \(p\mapsto b_p\) is injective.

The family \((a_p)_p\) is injective by Lemma 1, and the family \((b_p)_p\) is injective by Lemma 3. A given integer \(r\) can therefore occur in at most one \(a\)-slot and at most one \(b\)-slot. Hence

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{col}(r)\le2\quad\text{for every }r.}
\]

Cross-collisions \(a_p=b_q\) may occur, but they can contribute at most one column of each type to a fixed \(r\), which is exactly why the bound is two.

## The common-row star remains

For every active prime,

\[
N\bmod p=a_p\in Z_p,
\]

so again

\[
B(N)=L.
\]

For every pair \(p<q\), the direct-direct residue pair \((a_p,a_q)\) contributes the common solution \(n=N\). Thus the actual pair count \(C_{pq}\) satisfies

\[
C_{pq}\ge1.
\]

Since \(|Z_p|,|Z_q|\le2\),

\[
R_{pq}
=C_{pq}-\frac{|Z_p||Z_q|N}{pq}
\ge1-\frac{4N}{pq}.
\]

Summing gives

\[
\sum_{p<q}R_{pq}
\ge\binom L2-4N\sum_{p<q}\frac1{pq}
=\binom L2-O(N).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}R_{pq}
\gg\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}
}
\]

also in the reflection-symmetric model with \(C_1=2\) and \(C_2=2\).

Moreover,

\[
\lambda
=\sum_p\frac{|Z_p|}{p}
=2\log2+o(1)
\]

after deleting the \(O(1)\) central exceptions.

### Further Apéry-like local properties

For large \(N\), all relevant primes are odd. If \(a_p\ne b_p\), then

\[
a_p-b_p=2a_p-(p-1)
\]

is even, so the two reflected residues are never consecutive. By deleting the \(O(1)\) primes for which a residue is one of \(0,1,p-2,p-1\), one may also impose the usual endpoint exclusions without affecting any asymptotic.

Each active prime has only one reflection pair, so its prime-side PTR3 contribution is zero:

\[
\binom{m_p}{3}=0.
\]

Thus a bounded-column, reflection-symmetric common-row star is compatible with very strong prime-side sparsity.

One may also make any prescribed positive proportion of the primes inactive by setting \(Z_p=\varnothing\). For example, a “roughly 30% active, 70% empty” model still has \(\asymp N/\log N\) active primes, column norm at most two, and residual sum \(\asymp N^2/(\log N)^2\). The observed Apéry proportions \(0\) versus \(2\) therefore do not by themselves address this obstruction.

# 3. Why injectivity is not an anti-concentration theorem

The structural fact in the question is correct:

> If \(p,q>\sqrt N\), \(p,q\le n\le N\), and \(n\bmod p=n\bmod q\), then \(p=q\).

But its interpretation must be reversed. It does not say that a large common-row star is impossible. It says that the residue labels along such a star are automatically distinct.

For the counterexample,

\[
r_p=N\bmod p
\]

is injective, yet

\[
\operatorname{CRT}_{p,q}(r_p,r_q)=N
\]

for every pair.

Thus “different residues” does not mean “generic CRT value.” Distinct residues can be perfectly coordinated as a function of the modulus.

The decomposition into colliding and non-colliding pairs therefore misses the principal obstruction:

- colliding pair: \(r_p=r_q\);
- coherent non-colliding pair: \(r_p\ne r_q\), but both lie on the same moving graph \(r_p=n_0\bmod p\).

The singleton counterexample has no colliding pairs at all, while every non-colliding pair has the same CRT lift \(n_0=N\).

In normalized form,

\[
\frac{\operatorname{CRT}_{p,q}(r_p,r_q)}{pq}
=\frac{N}{pq}.
\]

The cutoff in the residual is also \(N/(pq)\). Hence every point lies exactly at the relevant moving cutoff. There is no equidistribution.

# 4. Geometric formulation: vertical fibers versus congruence graphs

Define the incidence set

\[
\mathcal A=\{(p,r):p\in\mathcal P_N,\ r\in Z_p\}.
\]

The column norm controls fibers of the static projection

\[
(p,r)\longmapsto r.
\]

For a fixed integer \(n\), define the congruence graph

\[
\Gamma_n=\{(p,n\bmod p):p\in\mathcal P_N\}.
\]

Then

\[
B(n)=|\mathcal A\cap\Gamma_n|.
\]

The singleton counterexample is simply

\[
\mathcal A=\Gamma_N.
\]

Its vertical fibers have size one, but its intersection with \(\Gamma_N\) has size \(L\).

The reflection-symmetric counterexample is essentially

\[
\mathcal A=\Gamma_N\cup\Gamma_{-N-1},
\]

because

\[
(-N-1)\bmod p=p-1-(N\bmod p).
\]

Its vertical fibers have size at most two, while \(|\mathcal A\cap\Gamma_N|=L\).

This identifies the exact conceptual gap:

> Static vertical sparsity does not imply transversality to the moving congruence graphs \(\Gamma_n\).

Any valid additional hypothesis must see the dependence of the residue on the modulus \(p\). A condition depending only on the multiset of literal residue labels cannot exclude the counterexample.

# 5. Exact factorial-moment identity

Let

\[
C_{ij}
=\#\{n\le N:n\bmod p_i\in Z_i,\ n\bmod p_j\in Z_j\},
\]

and put

\[
\rho_i=\frac{|Z_i|}{p_i},
\qquad
\lambda=\sum_i\rho_i,
\qquad
\sigma^2=\sum_i\rho_i^2.
\]

Since an integer with \(B(n)=k\) contributes \(\binom{k}{2}\) unordered prime pairs,

\[
\sum_{n\le N}\binom{B(n)}2
=\sum_{i<j}C_{ij}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\sum_{n\le N}(B(n))_2
=2\sum_{i<j}C_{ij}.
\]

Also

\[
2\sum_{i<j}\rho_i\rho_j
=\lambda^2-\sigma^2.
\]

Therefore the residual definition gives the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
2\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}
=\sum_{n\le N}(B(n))_2
-N(\lambda^2-\sigma^2).
}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}=o(N)
}
\]

is exactly equivalent to

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}(B(n))_2
=N(\lambda^2-\sigma^2)+o(N).
}
\]

This is the precise second-order moving-target mixing theorem that is missing.

It is not a consequence of bounded column norm: in the singleton star,

\[
(B(N))_2=(L)_2\asymp\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}
\]

already overwhelms the entire expected \(O(N)\) factorial-moment scale.

# 6. Small correction to the stated “exact variance identity”

On the finite interval \(1\le n\le N\), the one-column count is not generally exactly \(N|Z_i|/p_i\). Define

\[
A_i=\#\{n\le N:n\bmod p_i\in Z_i\},
\qquad
\mu=\frac1N\sum_iA_i
=\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}B(n).
\]

Then the genuinely exact variance identity is

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(B)
=\mu-\sigma^2+\lambda^2-\mu^2
+\frac2N\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}.
}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\operatorname{Var}(B)
=\lambda-\sigma^2+rac2N\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}
+(\mu-\lambda)(1-\mu-\lambda).
\]

The identity stated in the question is exact only when \(\mu=\lambda\), for example after averaging over a complete common period or when the finite marginal is used in place of \(|Z_i|/p_i\).

For the present range,

\[
|A_i-N|Z_i|/p_i|\le |Z_i|,
\]

so

\[
|\mu-\lambda|
\le\frac1N\sum_i|Z_i|
=O\left(\frac1{\log N}\right)
\]

under the bounded-size hypothesis. This correction does not affect the counterexample or the intended implication: \(\sum R_{ij}=O(N)\) would still give \(\operatorname{Var}(B)=O(1)\).

Also, for the pointwise conclusion, \(O(N)\)—not necessarily \(o(N)\)—would already suffice. The counterexample shows that even \(O(N)\) is false under bounded column norm alone.

# 7. Evaluation of the proposed approaches

## (a) Discrepancy of the CRT point set

This is the right language only after adding a genuine arithmetic input. It cannot be deduced from the column norm.

In the singleton counterexample,

\[
\operatorname{CRT}_{p,q}(a_p,a_q)=N
\]

for every pair. Hence the entire point set is maximally concentrated relative to the variable cutoff.

There is also a technical issue: the relevant threshold is not one fixed interval \([0,\alpha]\) on the unit circle. It is

\[
\alpha_{pq}=\frac N{pq},
\]

which varies with \((p,q)\). A proof must control discrepancy with modulus-dependent cutoffs, usually after dyadically grouping \(p\) and \(q\).

## (b) Injectivity plus column norm

This route is disproved exactly by the construction above. The counterexample has the strongest possible injectivity and column norm one.

Injectivity only says that a common row uses different residue labels. It gives no randomness in the map

\[
p\longmapsto n_0\bmod p.
\]

## (c) Fourth moments or averaging over shifts

No fourth-moment argument can prove the deterministic statement from the stated hypotheses, because the statement itself is false.

A fourth-moment or dispersion argument can become useful after one adds averaging over a translation, over \(N\), over the zero sets, or over another arithmetic family. Such averaging may destroy the fixed star \(Z_p=\{N\bmod p\}\). But a conclusion for one fixed deterministic Apéry family requires an arithmetic theorem showing that the actual zeros do not behave like this star.

## (d) Probabilistic model

If the residues were independent and uniform, then for a singleton pair

\[
\mathbb P(\operatorname{CRT}_{p,q}(a_p,a_q)\le N)=\frac N{pq},
\]

so \(\mathbb E R_{pq}=0\). Ignoring secondary dependencies, the total variance would have scale

\[
\sum_{p<q}\frac N{pq}
\asymp N,
\]

and the natural fluctuation would be \(O(\sqrt N)\). This is consistent with the empirical Apéry behavior.

However, conditioning only on “all literal residues are distinct” does not approximate independence. The deterministic assignment

\[
a_p=N\bmod p
\]

has distinct residues and produces complete alignment. Thus the probabilistic heuristic becomes a proof only after establishing a pseudorandomness theorem for the arithmetic family \(p\mapsto Z_p\).

# 8. The exact additional ingredient

There are several equivalent ways to state it.

## 8.1 Congruence-graph second-order discrepancy

The minimal exact hypothesis is

```text
(MTD2)
Sum over n <= N of (B(n))_2
  = N * (lambda^2 - sigma^2) + o(N).
```

Equivalently,

\[
\sum_{n\le N}\binom{|\mathcal A\cap\Gamma_n|}{2}
=N\sum_{i<j}\frac{|Z_i||Z_j|}{p_ip_j}+o(N).
\]

This says that the incidence set \(\mathcal A\) is second-order transverse, in aggregate, to the moving congruence graphs \(\Gamma_n\).

## 8.2 Aggregate CRT discrepancy

For \(a\in Z_p\), \(b\in Z_q\), let \(c_{pq}(a,b)\in[0,pq)\) be the CRT lift. Then the needed statement is

\[
\sum_{p<q}\sum_{a\in Z_p}\sum_{b\in Z_q}
\left(
\mathbf 1_{\{1\le c_{pq}(a,b)\le N\}}
-\frac N{pq}
\right)
=o(N).
\]

This is exactly \(\sum R_{pq}=o(N)\). To turn it into a usable theorem rather than a restatement, one needs a dyadic or spectral bound that exploits the specific arithmetic origin of \(Z_p\).

## 8.3 Spectral quasi-orthogonality

Let

\[
f_p(n)=\mathbf 1_{Z_p}(n\bmod p),
\qquad
\rho_p=\frac{|Z_p|}{p}.
\]

Up to the harmless finite-interval marginal correction, the desired result is off-diagonal quasi-orthogonality:

\[
\sum_{p\ne q}\sum_{n\le N}
(f_p(n)-\rho_p)(f_q(n)-\rho_q)
=o(N).
\]

A stronger and more reusable input would be a large-sieve-type asymptotic, uniform in coefficients \(c_p\):

\[
\sum_{n\le N}
\left|\sum_p c_p(f_p(n)-\rho_p)\right|^2
=
N\sum_p|c_p|^2(\rho_p-\rho_p^2)
+o(N)\|c\|^2_{\mathrm{appropriate}}.
\]

Setting \(c_p=1\) would give the required cancellation. A mere upper-bound large sieve with a large \(P^2\) term is not enough; one needs a quasi-orthogonality estimate at the length \(N\).

## 8.4 Exact Fourier form of the missing cancellation

For \(m=pq>N\), let

\[
\widehat I_m(h)=\sum_{n=1}^N e_m(-hn),
\qquad
F_p(t)=\sum_{a\in Z_p}e_p(ta).
\]

If \(\bar q\) denotes the inverse of \(q\bmod p\), and \(\bar p\) the inverse of \(p\bmod q\), Fourier inversion gives

\[
R_{pq}
=\frac1{pq}\sum_{h=1}^{pq-1}
\widehat I_{pq}(h)
F_p(h\bar q)
F_q(h\bar p).
\]

Therefore the exact analytic input is cancellation in

\[
\sum_{p<q}\frac1{pq}\sum_{h=1}^{pq-1}
\widehat I_{pq}(h)
F_p(h\bar q)
F_q(h\bar p).
\]

The modular inverses couple the two primes. Static column norm gives no control over these inverse-twisted phases. A bilinear large sieve, trace-function correlation theorem, or dispersion estimate for this expression would be a genuine additional ingredient.

## 8.5 Quotient-cell moving-target form

For \(p>\sqrt N\) and \(n\le N\), write

\[
n=q p+r,
\qquad q=\lfloor n/p\rfloor<\sqrt N,
\qquad r=n-qp.
\]

The event is

\[
r=n-qp\in Z_p.
\]

Thus one needs anti-concentration of the zero sets against the affine moving targets

\[
p\longmapsto n-qp
\]

inside quotient cells. This is the natural LGTQ/moving-target formulation. A fixed-residue column bound controls horizontal lines \(r=\text{constant}\); the obstruction lies on the slanted families \(r=n-qp\).

# 9. Consequences for the Apéry application

For the actual Apéry zero sets,

\[
Z_p=\{r<p:p\mid b_r\},
\]

a bound

\[
\operatorname{col}(r)=\#\{p:r\in Z_p\}\le C_2
\]

is useful information about the fixed integer \(b_r\): it says only boundedly many primes in the chosen range divide that same \(b_r\).

But a hit at a fixed \(n\) uses the varying integers

\[
b_{n\bmod p}.
\]

The residues \(n\bmod p\) are normally all distinct for \(p>\sqrt N\). Hence a fixed-index divisor bound does not couple the events across primes.

To rule out the counterexample pattern, one needs an arithmetic theorem of the form

\[
\#\{p:\sqrt N<p\le N,\ p\mid b_{N\bmod p}\}
\text{ is small},
\]

or, at the variance level, an aggregate pair correlation theorem for

\[
p\mid b_{n\bmod p},
\qquad
q\mid b_{n\bmod q}.
\]

That is precisely the original cross-prime moving-target problem. It cannot be recovered from:

- reflection symmetry;
- bounded \(|Z_p|\);
- bounded literal column norm;
- no consecutive zeros;
- PTR3 or other prime-side higher moments;
- pairwise coprimality of fixed gap polynomials;
- bounded codegree between two distinct rows.

All of those are compatible with an anchored rainbow-star model at the level relevant here.

A successful Apéry proof therefore needs one of the following genuinely cross-prime inputs:

1. **CRT discrepancy:** cancellation in the aggregate CRT residual over \((p,q,a,b)\);
2. **moving-target large sieve:** quasi-orthogonality of \(1_{Z_p}(n\bmod p)\) across primes;
3. **arithmetic pinning/resultant theorem:** many hits at one row force many primes to divide one common nonzero integer of controlled height;
4. **sheaf/trace-function independence across varying primes:** enough to control the inverse-twisted Fourier expression;
5. **direct rainbow-star exclusion:** a uniform bound for \(B(n)\) exploiting the Apéry recurrence rather than fixed-residue multiplicity.

The column norm may be one component of such a proof, but it cannot be the decisive component.

# 10. Final answers to the specific questions

### Can bounded column norm prove \(\sum R=o(N)\)?

No. Even \(\operatorname{col}(r)\le1\) permits

\[
\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}
\asymp\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^2}.
\]

### Are the non-colliding CRT values automatically equidistributed?

No. Taking \(r_p=N\bmod p\) makes all residues pairwise distinct while

\[
\operatorname{CRT}_{p,q}(r_p,r_q)=N
\]

for every pair.

### Does injectivity help?

Not by itself. The counterexample satisfies injectivity exactly. Injectivity is compatible with, and in this construction guarantees, column norm one.

### Can a fourth-moment or probabilistic argument prove the deterministic claim?

Not from the stated assumptions. A random model predicts \(O(\sqrt N)\) fluctuations, but bounded column norm is far weaker than randomness. A fourth-moment proof needs an additional arithmetic averaging or pseudorandomness input.

### What is the exact missing theorem?

At second order it is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}(B(n))_2
=N(\lambda^2-\sigma^2)+o(N),
}
\]

or any equivalent aggregate CRT / spectral / moving-target discrepancy estimate.

### What should replace “bounded column norm” in the proof plan?

A condition controlling intersections with the congruence graphs

\[
\Gamma_n=\{(p,n\bmod p)\},
\]

not merely vertical fibers \(r=\text{constant}\). The right slogan is:

> **bounded static columns do not exclude moving rainbow stars; one needs congruence-graph transversality.**
