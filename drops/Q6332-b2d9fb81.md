# Executive verdict

1. **The proposed quadratic-form parametrization is arithmetically false as stated.** For primes \(p\nmid 6\),
   \[
   p=x^2+6y^2 \iff p\equiv 1,7\pmod{24},
   \qquad
   p=2x^2+3y^2 \iff p\equiv 5,11\pmod{24}.
   \]
   The class \(p\equiv23\pmod{24}\) is inert in \(\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-6})\), so it has **no** representation by either form. Thus the \(\tau\)-quarter class \(5\bmod24\) is the odd-odd part of the nonprincipal form class, but the \(\sigma\)-quarter class \(23\bmod24\) is not a form class at all. The correct common language is branch-dependent Frobenius in the conductor-\(24\) cyclotomic extension, not one binary quadratic form.

2. **The full zero set of the square-root coefficients does not determine the Apéry coefficient zero set.** In the \(\tau\) branch, \(b_r\) is the Cauchy convolution
   \[
   b_r=\sum_{i+j=r}\tau_i\tau_j.
   \]
   In the \(\sigma\) branch, if \(c_r=\sum_{i+j=r}\sigma_i\sigma_j\), then
   \[
   b_r=c_r-34c_{r-1}+c_{r-2}.
   \]
   These depend on the **values and additive phases** of the nonzero coefficients, not merely their support. A quarter coefficient zero deletes one or two summands; it does not generally force the corresponding convolution coefficient to vanish. The exact remaining problem is an additive-character correlation estimate for these convolutions.

3. **The Bernoulli formula is a theorem.** For every prime \(p\ge5\), with Bernoulli convention
   \[
   \frac{z}{e^z-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}B_n\frac{z^n}{n!},
   \]
   one has
   \[
   \boxed{
   b_p\equiv 5-\frac{14}{3}p^3B_{p-3}\pmod{p^4},
   }
   \]
   and hence
   \[
   \boxed{
   \beta_p:=\frac{b_p-5}{p^3}\equiv-\frac{14}{3}B_{p-3}\pmod p.
   }
   \]
   A direct binomial/harmonic proof is given below. There is also an independent published-preprint route: Theorem 1.1 of Ji-Cai Liu’s arXiv:2404.16636 specializes exactly to this congruence. No identification with the same rank-\(2\) square-root object follows from the supplied data; that would require an integral Frobenius lift, not just the mod-\(p\) square factorization.

# 1. The two quadratic forms and the quarter classes

Put
\[
f_1(X,Y)=X^2+6Y^2,
\qquad
f_2(X,Y)=2X^2+3Y^2.
\]
Both are primitive positive definite binary quadratic forms of discriminant
\[
0^2-4\cdot1\cdot6=0^2-4\cdot2\cdot3=-24.
\]
Throughout this section, \(p\) is an odd prime with \(p\nmid6\).

## Lemma 1.1: necessary residue classes

If \(p=f_1(x,y)\), then \(p\equiv1\) or \(7\pmod{24}\). If \(p=f_2(x,y)\), then \(p\equiv5\) or \(11\pmod{24}\).

### Proof

For \(p=x^2+6y^2\), reduction modulo \(3\) gives \(p\equiv x^2\equiv1\pmod3\). Since \(p\) is odd, \(x\) is odd. Modulo \(8\),

- if \(y\) is even, then \(p\equiv1\pmod8\);
- if \(y\) is odd, then \(p\equiv1+6\equiv7\pmod8\).

Combining with \(p\equiv1\pmod3\) gives \(p\equiv1,7\pmod{24}\).

For \(p=2x^2+3y^2\), \(y\) must be odd, since otherwise both summands are even. Also \(3\nmid x\), and modulo \(3\),
\[
p\equiv2x^2\equiv2\pmod3.
\]
Modulo \(8\),

- if \(x\) is odd, then \(p\equiv2+3\equiv5\pmod8\);
- if \(x\) is even, then \(p\equiv3\pmod8\).

Combining with \(p\equiv2\pmod3\) gives \(p\equiv5,11\pmod{24}\). ∎

## Lemma 1.2: these conditions are also sufficient

For \(p\nmid6\),
\[
\boxed{
 p=x^2+6y^2 \iff p\equiv1,7\pmod{24},
}
\tag{1.1}
\]
\[
\boxed{
 p=2x^2+3y^2 \iff p\equiv5,11\pmod{24}.
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

### Proof

The reduced primitive positive forms of discriminant \(-24\) are exactly
\[
[1,0,6],\qquad[2,0,3].
\]
Indeed, for a reduced form \([a,b,c]\), one has
\[
|b|\le a\le c,
\qquad
 a\le\sqrt{24/3}=\sqrt8<3,
\]
and \(b\) is even. If \(a=1\), integrality forces \(b=0,c=6\); if \(a=2\), it forces \(b=0,c=3\). Hence the class number of discriminant \(-24\) is \(2\).

The exact form-ideal theorem used here is the following classical statement:

> If \(D<0\) is a fundamental discriminant and \(p\nmid D\) is prime, then \(p\) is represented by a primitive positive form of discriminant \(D\) if and only if \((D/p)=1\). The proper equivalence class of the representing form is the ideal class of a prime of \(\mathbf Q(\sqrt D)\) above \(p\).

For \(D=-24\),
\[
\left(\frac{-24}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=1
\iff p\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{24}.
\]
Thus every prime in those four classes is represented by one of the two reduced forms. Lemma 1.1 separates the two possibilities, proving (1.1) and (1.2). ∎

## Lemma 1.3: uniqueness

For every represented prime \(p\nmid6\), the positive pair \((x,y)\) is unique. Equivalently, all integral representations by the indicated form are obtained from one pair by
\[
(x,y)\longmapsto(\pm x,\pm y).
\]

### Proof

A primitive representation of a split prime determines one of the two prime ideals above \(p\). Prime-ideal factorization gives only the conjugate pair. Since the class group has order \(2\), inversion fixes each class. The proper automorphism group of either form is \(\{\pm I\}\), and complex conjugation changes the sign of \(y\). Thus there are precisely the four independent sign variants. Since \(p\nmid6\), neither coordinate is zero, so there is exactly one representation with \(x,y>0\). ∎

## The exact genus and class-field statement

Let
\[
K=\mathbf Q(\sqrt{-6}),
\qquad \operatorname{disc}(K)=-24.
\]
Since the class group has order \(2\), its square subgroup is trivial. Therefore the two form classes are also the two genera:

- \(x^2+6y^2\) is the principal genus;
- \(2x^2+3y^2\) is the nonprincipal genus.

The genus field is already the Hilbert class field:
\[
H=K(\sqrt2)=\mathbf Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3}).
\tag{1.3}
\]
Here is a direct check. The three quadratic subfields of \(H\) have discriminants
\[
8,\quad-3,\quad-24,
\]
so the biquadratic discriminant formula gives
\[
|\operatorname{disc}(H)|=8\cdot3\cdot24=576=|-24|^2.
\]
Consequently the relative discriminant of \(H/K\) has norm \(1\), so \(H/K\) is unramified. Its degree is \(2=h_K\), hence it is the Hilbert class field.

For a rational prime \(p\) that splits in \(K\), and a prime \(\mathfrak p\mid p\), Artin reciprocity gives
\[
\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathfrak p}(H/K)=1
\iff [\mathfrak p]=1
\iff p=x^2+6y^2.
\]
Because \(H=K(\sqrt2)\), this is also equivalent to
\[
\left(\frac2p\right)=1.
\]
Thus the full table is

| \(p\bmod24\) | \(({-6}/p)\) | \((2/p)\) | form status |
|---:|:---:|:---:|:---|
| \(1\) | \(+1\) | \(+1\) | \(x^2+6y^2\) |
| \(5\) | \(+1\) | \(-1\) | \(2x^2+3y^2\) |
| \(7\) | \(+1\) | \(+1\) | \(x^2+6y^2\) |
| \(11\) | \(+1\) | \(-1\) | \(2x^2+3y^2\) |
| \(13,17,19,23\) | \(-1\) | varies | inert in \(K\); neither form represents \(p\) |

This is the correct class-field/genus formulation.

## Application to the fixed quarter coefficients

In the \(\tau\) branch, the reflection interval has length
\[
m_+=\frac{p-1}{2}.
\]
Its fixed coefficient \(m_+/2=(p-1)/4\) is integral precisely for \(p\equiv1\pmod4\). Within the split branch \(({-6}/p)=+1\), these are \(p\equiv1,5\pmod{24}\). The supplied quarter theorem therefore says
\[
\tau_{(p-1)/4}=0
\iff p\equiv5\pmod{24}.
\]
By (1.2), this can be restated as
\[
\boxed{
 p=2x^2+3y^2\text{ with }x,y\text{ odd}.
}
\tag{1.4}
\]
Indeed, for the nonprincipal form, \(p\equiv5\pmod8\) corresponds to \(x\) odd, while \(p\equiv11\pmod{24}\) corresponds to \(x\) even; \(y\) is always odd.

In the \(\sigma\) branch,
\[
m_-\ =\frac{p-3}{2},
\]
and \(m_-/2=(p-3)/4\) is integral for \(p\equiv3\pmod4\). Within the inert branch \(({-6}/p)=-1\), these are \(p\equiv19,23\pmod{24}\). The supplied theorem says
\[
\sigma_{(p-3)/4}=0
\iff p\equiv23\pmod{24}.
\]
But \(23\bmod24\) is inert in \(K\). Therefore
\[
\boxed{
 p\equiv23\pmod{24}
 \Longrightarrow
 p\ne x^2+6y^2,
 \quad
 p\ne2x^2+3y^2.
}
\tag{1.5}
\]
There is no representation to be unique; uniqueness is vacuous.

A particularly elementary obstruction to \(2x^2+3y^2\) is modulo \(8\): every odd value of that form is \(3\) or \(5\pmod8\), whereas \(23\equiv7\pmod8\).

## The logical flaw in the proposed slogan

Calling both quarter-vanishing classes “exactly primes represented by \(2x^2+3y^2\)” makes two errors.

1. That form represents both \(5\) and \(11\pmod{24}\), whereas the integral \(\tau\)-quarter theorem singles out only \(5\pmod{24}\). The missing condition is the parity condition \(x,y\) odd, or equivalently \(p\equiv1\pmod4\).
2. The \(\sigma\)-zero class \(23\pmod{24}\) is inert and is represented by neither form.

The correct common abelian language for all eight residue classes is
\[
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbf Q(\zeta_{24})/\mathbf Q)
\simeq(\mathbf Z/24\mathbf Z)^\times,
\qquad
\operatorname{Frob}_p(\zeta_{24})=\zeta_{24}^{\,p}.
\]
The two quarter-zero classes are two different rational Frobenius elements, \(5\) and \(23\), and they occur in different square-root branches. Only the split branch admits the binary-form/ideal-class interpretation.

Finally, a representation of \(p\) is global data about the prime. Even where it exists and is unique, it does not by itself give an index-by-index parametrization of the full coefficient zero set
\[
Y_p=\{j:s_{p,j}=0\}.
\]
To make “parametrization” mathematically substantive, one would need an explicit rule producing every zero index \(j\) from the representation coordinates, and a converse. The quarter theorem gives one reflection-fixed index; it gives no such rule for the remaining reflected pairs. **[GAP-QF]** No full-index parametrization by richer ray-class data is established here, but the proposed parametrization by \(2x^2+3y^2\) is already disproved by (1.5).

# 2. What the square-root coefficients honestly say about \(Z_p\)

Let
\[
\psi_p(z)=e^{2\pi i\widetilde z/p}
\]
be the standard additive character of \(\mathbf F_p\), where \(\widetilde z\) is any integer lift.

## 2.1 The \(\tau\) branch

Assume \(({-6}/p)=+1\), put
\[
m=\frac{p-1}{2},
\qquad
s_p(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}t_jX^j,
\qquad t_j=\tau_j\pmod p,
\]
and extend \(t_j=0\) outside \(0\le j\le m\). Since \(2m=p-1\), there is no wraparound, and
\[
s_p(X)^2=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}c_rX^r,
\qquad
c_r=\sum_{i\in\mathbf Z}t_i t_{r-i}.
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{
 b_r=c_r
 =\sum_{i=\max(0,r-m)}^{\min(m,r)}t_i t_{r-i}
 \quad(0\le r<p).
}
\tag{2.1}
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{
 Z_p=\left\{0\le r<p:
 \sum_{i=\max(0,r-m)}^{\min(m,r)}t_i t_{r-i}=0
 \right\}.
}
\tag{2.2}
\]

## 2.2 The \(\sigma\) branch

Assume \(({-6}/p)=-1\), put
\[
m=\frac{p-3}{2},
\qquad
s_p(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}s_jX^j,
\qquad s_j=\sigma_j\pmod p,
\]
and define
\[
c_r=\sum_i s_i s_{r-i},
\qquad c_r=0\quad\text{for }r\notin[0,p-3].
\]
Since the Apéry truncation is
\[
(1-34X+X^2)s_p(X)^2,
\]
one has the exact coefficient relation
\[
\boxed{
 b_r=c_r-34c_{r-1}+c_{r-2}
 \quad(0\le r<p).
}
\tag{2.3}
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{
 Z_p=\{0\le r<p:c_r-34c_{r-1}+c_{r-2}=0\}.
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

These are the strongest unconditional coefficient formulas furnished by the square factorization itself.

## 2.3 Exact dependence on the coefficient zero set

For the \(\tau\) branch, let
\[
Y=\{0\le i\le m:t_i=0\},
\qquad
I_r=\{i:\max(0,r-m)\le i\le\min(m,r)\}.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
 c_r=\sum_{i\in I_r\setminus(Y\cup(r-Y))}t_it_{r-i},
}
\tag{2.5}
\]
where \(r-Y=\{r-y:y\in Y\}\). Formula (2.5) is exact, but it still contains every nonzero coefficient value.

An equivalent value-distribution formulation is useful. Define
\[
N_r(a)=\#\{i\in I_r:t_it_{r-i}=a\},
\qquad a\in\mathbf F_p.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
 c_r=\sum_{a\in\mathbf F_p}aN_r(a).
}
\tag{2.6}
\]
The support \(Y\) determines only \(N_r(0)\). It gives no information about the weighted first moment in (2.6).

The same statements hold for the intermediate convolution \(c_r\) in the \(\sigma\) branch, after which (2.3) introduces an additional cancellation among three adjacent values.

## 2.4 Exact additive-character formula

Orthogonality gives
\[
\mathbf1_{z=0}=\frac1p\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbf F_p}\psi_p(\lambda z).
\]
Therefore, in the \(\tau\) branch,
\[
\boxed{
 |Z_p|
 =1+\frac1p\sum_{\lambda\ne0}
 \sum_{r=0}^{p-1}
 \psi_p\!\left(\lambda\sum_i t_it_{r-i}\right).
}
\tag{2.7}
\]
In the \(\sigma\) branch,
\[
\boxed{
 |Z_p|
 =1+\frac1p\sum_{\lambda\ne0}
 \sum_{r=0}^{p-1}
 \psi_p\!\left(\lambda(c_r-34c_{r-1}+c_{r-2})\right).
}
\tag{2.8}
\]

Pointwise, the phase in (2.7) can be paired as
\[
\psi_p(\lambda c_r)
=
\psi_p\!\left(
 \lambda\mathbf1_{\substack{r\text{ even}\\r/2\in I_r}}t_{r/2}^2
 \right)
\prod_{\substack{i<r-i\\i\in I_r}}
\psi_p(2\lambda t_it_{r-i}).
\tag{2.9}
\]
Thus the relevant data are the phases of all pair products \(t_it_{r-i}\), not merely which factors vanish.

Equivalently, using the histogram \(N_r(a)\),
\[
\psi_p(\lambda c_r)
=\prod_{a\in\mathbf F_p}\psi_p(\lambda a)^{N_r(a)}.
\tag{2.10}
\]
Formula (2.10) pinpoints the missing input exactly.

## 2.5 Why a quarter zero does not control convolution zeros

Suppose the reflection interval has even length \(m=2q\), so the quarter index \(q\) is its fixed point, and normalize \(t_0=1\). Then
\[
c_q=2t_q+\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}t_it_{q-i},
\tag{2.11}
\]
while
\[
c_{2q}=t_q^2+2\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}t_it_{2q-i}.
\tag{2.12}
\]
If \(t_q=0\), these become
\[
c_q=\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}t_it_{q-i},
\qquad
c_{2q}=2\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}t_it_{2q-i}.
\]
Neither is forced to vanish. The exceptional smallest case \(q=1\) has an empty internal sum in (2.11), but that edge accident does not persist for larger primes.

For a general \(r\), the zero \(t_q=0\) merely deletes
\[
t_qt_{r-q}+t_{r-q}t_q
\]
when those indices are valid, or the single central term when \(r=2q\). All other summands remain.

In the \(\sigma\) branch, even a vanishing convolution coefficient \(c_r\) would not by itself imply \(b_r=0\), because (2.3) mixes \(c_r,c_{r-1},c_{r-2}\).

There is a simple algebraic counterexample showing that a reflection-symmetric zero set cannot determine convolution zeros. Over \(\mathbf F_5\), take the two coefficient vectors
\[
(1,1,1),
\qquad
(1,1,2).
\]
Both have the same zero set, namely the empty set, which is reflection-symmetric. Their degree-\(2\) convolution coefficients are respectively
\[
1+1+1=3,
\qquad
2+1+2=0\pmod5.
\]
This example is not asserted to satisfy the \(\tau\) recurrence; it proves the logical point that **no argument using only the set \(Y_p\)** can recover \(Z_p\).

## 2.6 The extra law that would be sufficient

Two kinds of additional input would be genuinely useful.

### Exact-value route

Find a uniform formula for every \(t_j\) or \(s_j\) as a finite-field trace/Jacobi sum, including compatible phases, and then evaluate the pair-product sums in (2.1) or (2.3). An exact vanishing classification would require more than the absolute values of those traces.

### Character-sum route

A square-root cancellation theorem of the form
\[
\boxed{
 \max_{\lambda\ne0}
 \left|
 \sum_{r=0}^{p-1}
 \psi_p(\lambda c_r)
 \right|
 \ll p^{1/2+o(1)}
}
\tag{PH-\tau}
\]
would imply from (2.7)
\[
|Z_p|\ll p^{1/2+o(1)}.
\]
The \(\sigma\) analogue is
\[
\boxed{
 \max_{\lambda\ne0}
 \left|
 \sum_{r=0}^{p-1}
 \psi_p(\lambda(c_r-34c_{r-1}+c_{r-2}))
 \right|
 \ll p^{1/2+o(1)}.
}
\tag{PH-\sigma}
\]

**[GAP-PHASE]** No bounded-conductor trace-function realization or proof of either estimate is supplied here. This is the precise phase/value law missing from the quarter-zero information.

# 3. The Bernoulli defect \(\beta_p\)

All rational congruences below are interpreted in the localization \(\mathbf Z_{(p)}\). Put
\[
H_{p-1}^{(j)}=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac1{k^j}.
\]
The Bernoulli number \(B_{p-3}\) is \(p\)-integral: by the von Staudt–Clausen denominator theorem, \(p\) could occur in its denominator only if \(p-1\mid p-3\), which is impossible for \(p\ge5\).

## Lemma 3.1: the needed harmonic congruences

For every prime \(p\ge5\),
\[
\boxed{
H_{p-1}^{(3)}\equiv0\pmod p,
}
\tag{3.1}
\]
\[
\boxed{
H_{p-1}^{(2)}\equiv\frac23pB_{p-3}\pmod{p^2},
}
\tag{3.2}
\]
\[
\boxed{
H_{p-1}^{(1)}\equiv-\frac13p^2B_{p-3}\pmod{p^3}.
}
\tag{3.3}
\]

### Proof of (3.1)

Modulo \(p\), \(k^{-3}=k^{p-4}\) on \(\mathbf F_p^\times\). Since \(p-1\nmid p-4\), the power sum over \(\mathbf F_p^\times\) is zero.

### Proof of (3.2) from Faulhaber and Kummer

Set
\[
N=\varphi(p^2)-2=p^2-p-2.
\]
Euler’s theorem gives
\[
k^{-2}\equiv k^N\pmod{p^2}
\qquad(1\le k<p).
\]
Faulhaber’s formula gives
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}k^N\equiv pB_N\pmod{p^2}.
\tag{3.4}
\]
Indeed, the \(p^2B_{N-1}\) term vanishes because \(N-1>1\) is odd, and every term of order \(p^j\), \(j\ge3\), has \(p\)-adic valuation at least \(2\), even allowing the possible single \(p\) in a Bernoulli denominator.

The exact Kummer congruence used is:

> If \(u,v>0\) are even, \(u\equiv v\pmod{p-1}\), and neither is divisible by \(p-1\), then
> \[
> \frac{B_u}{u}\equiv\frac{B_v}{v}\pmod p.
> \]

Here \(N\equiv p-3\pmod{p-1}\), so
\[
B_N\equiv\frac{N}{p-3}B_{p-3}\equiv\frac23B_{p-3}\pmod p.
\]
Combining this with (3.4) proves (3.2).

The same congruence is recorded directly, in the stronger prime-power form,
\[
\sum_{\substack{1\le k<p^m\\p\nmid k}}\frac1{k^2}
\equiv\frac23p^mB_{p-3}\pmod{p^{m+1}},
\]
as equation (2.10) of Lemma 2.3 in Ji-Cai Liu’s paper cited below.

### Proof of (3.3)

For \(1\le k<p\),
\[
\frac1{p-k}
\equiv
-\frac1k-\frac p{k^2}-\frac{p^2}{k^3}\pmod{p^3}.
\]
Summing over \(k\), and noting that \(k\mapsto p-k\) permutes \(1,\dots,p-1\), gives
\[
2H_{p-1}^{(1)}
\equiv-pH_{p-1}^{(2)}-p^2H_{p-1}^{(3)}\pmod{p^3}.
\]
Now use (3.1) and (3.2). ∎

## Lemma 3.2: the middle binomial terms

For \(1\le k\le p-1\),
\[
\boxed{
\binom pk^2\binom{p+k}{k}^2
\equiv
\frac{p^2}{k^2}+\frac{2p^3}{k^3}\pmod{p^4}.
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

### Proof

Use
\[
\binom pk
=(-1)^{k-1}\frac pk
\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac pj\right),
\qquad
\binom{p+k}{k}
=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac pj\right).
\]
After squaring,
\[
\binom pk^2\binom{p+k}{k}^2
=
\frac{p^2}{k^2}
\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac pj\right)^2
\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac pj\right)^2.
\]
The product in brackets is
\[
1+2p(H_k-H_{k-1})+O(p^2)
=1+\frac{2p}{k}+O(p^2).
\]
Multiplication by \(p^2/k^2\) proves (3.5). ∎

Summing (3.5) and using Lemma 3.1 gives
\[
\boxed{
\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}
\binom pk^2\binom{p+k}{k}^2
\equiv
\frac23p^3B_{p-3}\pmod{p^4}.
}
\tag{3.6}
\]

## Lemma 3.3: the endpoint term

For every prime \(p\ge5\),
\[
\boxed{
\binom{2p-1}{p-1}
\equiv1-\frac23p^3B_{p-3}\pmod{p^4}.
}
\tag{3.7}
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{
\binom{2p}{p}^2
\equiv4-\frac{16}{3}p^3B_{p-3}\pmod{p^4}.
}
\tag{3.8}
\]

### Proof

Write
\[
\binom{2p-1}{p-1}
=\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}\left(1+\frac pk\right).
\]
Expanding by elementary symmetric functions through order \(p^3\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\binom{2p-1}{p-1}
={}&1+pH^{(1)}
+\frac{p^2}{2}\bigl((H^{(1)})^2-H^{(2)}\bigr)\\
&+\frac{p^3}{6}\bigl((H^{(1)})^3-3H^{(1)}H^{(2)}+2H^{(3)}\bigr)
\pmod{p^4}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here all \(H^{(j)}\) have upper index \(p-1\). By Lemma 3.1,
\[
pH^{(1)}\equiv-\frac13p^3B_{p-3},
\qquad
-\frac{p^2}{2}H^{(2)}\equiv-\frac13p^3B_{p-3},
\]
and every other displayed term is divisible by \(p^4\). This proves (3.7). Since
\[
\binom{2p}{p}=2\binom{2p-1}{p-1},
\]
squaring (3.7) gives (3.8). ∎

## Theorem 3.4: the exact \(p^4\) Apéry congruence

For every prime \(p\ge5\),
\[
\boxed{
 b_p\equiv5-\frac{14}{3}p^3B_{p-3}\pmod{p^4}.
}
\tag{3.9}
\]

### Proof

Separate the defining sum into \(k=0\), \(1\le k\le p-1\), and \(k=p\):
\[
b_p
=1+
\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\binom pk^2\binom{p+k}{k}^2
+\binom{2p}{p}^2.
\]
Apply (3.6) and (3.8):
\[
\begin{aligned}
b_p
&\equiv
1+rac23p^3B_{p-3}
+4-\frac{16}{3}p^3B_{p-3}\\
&=5-\frac{14}{3}p^3B_{p-3}\pmod{p^4}.
\end{aligned}
\]
∎

Dividing by \(p^3\) proves
\[
\boxed{
\beta_p\equiv-\frac{14}{3}B_{p-3}\pmod p.
}
\tag{3.10}
\]

The decomposition
\[
-\frac{14}{3}
=\underbrace{-\frac{16}{3}}_{k=p\text{ endpoint}}
+\underbrace{\frac23}_{1\le k<p\text{ interior}}
\tag{3.11}
\]
is worth retaining: the coefficient is not produced by one isolated central binomial term.

## 3.5 Independent literature derivation

Ji-Cai Liu defines
\[
A_n^{(r,s,t)}
=\sum_{k=0}^{n}
\binom nk^r\binom{n+k}{k}^s\binom{2k}{n}^t.
\]
His Theorem 1.1 states, for \(p\ge5\), \(m,n\ge1\), \(r\ge2\),
\[
A_{np^m}^{(r,s,t)}
\equiv
A_{np^{m-1}}^{(r,s,t)}
+p^{3m}B_{p-3}\,\mathcal A_n^{(r,s,t)}
\pmod{p^{3m+1}},
\tag{3.12}
\]
with an explicit \(p,m\)-independent finite-sum formula for \(\mathcal A_n^{(r,s,t)}\).

Our sequence is \(A_n^{(2,2,0)}\). Substituting \(n=m=1\), \(r=s=2\), \(t=0\) into Liu’s three-sum formula for \(\mathcal A_1^{(2,2,0)}\) gives
\[
\mathcal A_1^{(2,2,0)}
=-\frac13(16)+\frac16(-10)+\frac16(14)
=-\frac{14}{3}.
\tag{3.13}
\]
Since \(A_1^{(2,2,0)}=5\), (3.12) becomes exactly (3.9). Thus the experimental \(31/31\) observation is covered by a general theorem, independently of the short derivation above.

## 3.6 The Wolstenholme qualification

Glaisher’s congruence (3.7) shows that, for \(p\ge5\),
\[
\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\equiv1\pmod{p^4}
\iff B_{p-3}\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
This is the standard Wolstenholme-prime criterion.

For \(\beta_p\), the coefficient \(-14/3\) is a unit only when \(p\ne7\). Hence
\[
\boxed{
 p\ge11:\quad
 \beta_p=0
 \iff p\mid\operatorname{num}(B_{p-3}).
}
\tag{3.14}
\]
At \(p=7\), \(14\equiv0\pmod7\), so \(\beta_7=0\) automatically even though \(B_4\not\equiv0\pmod7\). Thus “exactly the Wolstenholme class” requires the explicit qualification \(p\ge11\), with \(p=7\) as an extra exceptional zero.

# 4. Jacobi sums, Gross–Koblitz, and the rank-\(2\) square root

## 4.1 What Gross–Koblitz could do

The Gross–Koblitz formula converts Gauss sums into products of values of Morita’s \(p\)-adic gamma function. Jacobi sums are ratios of Gauss sums, so finite-field hypergeometric functions can often be converted into \(p\)-adic gamma products. Expanding such products one order deeper produces logarithmic derivatives of \(\Gamma_p\), and these are represented concretely by harmonic sums such as (3.1)–(3.3).

Therefore a Gross–Koblitz proof of (3.9) is plausible in the following precise sense:

1. produce a finite-field or \(p\)-adic hypergeometric identity whose integral lift agrees with \(b_p\pmod{p^4}\);
2. apply Gross–Koblitz to its Gauss sums;
3. expand the resulting \(\Gamma_p\)-ratios through order \(p^3\);
4. identify the derivatives with (3.1)–(3.3).

**[GAP-GK]** No such finite-field identity and order-\(p^3\) gamma expansion is supplied here. Thus Gross–Koblitz is a viable repackaging route, not an established connection in this audit.

The direct proof shows what any such derivation must reproduce: an endpoint contribution \(-16/3\) and an interior contribution \(+2/3\). It is therefore unlikely that the answer is the phase of one isolated Jacobi sum.

## 4.2 Why the mod-\(p\) rank-\(2\) factorization is insufficient

The \(\tau/\sigma\) square-root structure is a statement about the reduction of the Apéry truncation polynomial modulo \(p\). By contrast, \(\beta_p\) is the third divided \(p\)-adic digit of \(b_p-5\).

A mod-\(p\) square factorization cannot determine a \(p^3\)-divided defect. Formally, replacing any integral lift \(\widetilde F_p\) by
\[
\widetilde F_p+p^3R_p
\]
leaves its mod-\(p\) square root unchanged while changing third-order Frobenius data. Therefore the supplied rank-\(2\) reduction alone cannot imply (3.10).

A genuine common-object theorem would need substantially more:

> Construct an integral rank-\(2\) \(F\)-crystal \(\mathcal V_{\mathbf Z_p}\) whose symmetric square is the Apéry crystal, identify \(\tau\) or \(\sigma\) as its Hasse invariant according to \(({-6}/p)\), and prove that \(\beta_p\) is a specified entry of the normalized third-order Frobenius defect
> \[
> \frac{\Phi_p-\Phi_p^{(0)}}{p^3}\pmod p.
> \]

**[GAP-R2]** This integral/crystalline identification is not proved here. Without it, the quarter-zero/class-field story and the Bernoulli defect should be treated as distinct layers:

- quarter zeros: semisimple mod-\(p\) coefficient/Frobenius information, sensitive to \(p\bmod24\);
- \(\beta_p\): a higher \(p\)-adic extension or derivative class, sensitive to the irregular Bernoulli residue \(B_{p-3}\bmod p\).

The latter is not determined by any fixed congruence class modulo \(24\), so it cannot be explained solely by the same finite class-field datum.

# 5. Precise answers to the three questions

## (1)

No full parametrization by \(p=2x^2+3y^2\) is compatible with the arithmetic as stated.

- \(x^2+6y^2\) represents exactly \(p\equiv1,7\pmod{24}\).
- \(2x^2+3y^2\) represents exactly \(p\equiv5,11\pmod{24}\).
- Representations are unique up to independent signs.
- A prime \(p\equiv23\pmod{24}\) has no representation by either form.
- The \(\tau\)-quarter class can be described as the nonprincipal discriminant-\(-24\) form with the additional odd-odd parity condition.
- The \(\sigma\)-quarter class is an inert rational Frobenius class, not a form class.

The right genus field is \(\mathbf Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3})\); the right joint residue-class field is \(\mathbf Q(\zeta_{24})\).

## (2)

The exact formulas are (2.2) and (2.4), or equivalently the additive-character formulas (2.7) and (2.8). Quarter zeros only remove summands. To obtain a useful theorem for \(Z_p\), one needs the nonzero coefficient values and their pair-product phases, or a trace-function estimate such as \((\mathrm{PH}\text{-}\tau)\) and \((\mathrm{PH}\text{-}\sigma)\). The reflection symmetry of \(Y_p\) alone is insufficient.

## (3)

The formula
\[
\beta_p\equiv-\frac{14}{3}B_{p-3}\pmod p
\]
is rigorously proved for all \(p\ge5\) by the binomial expansion in Section 3, and independently follows from Liu’s Theorem 1.1. For \(p\ge11\), its zeros are exactly the Wolstenholme-prime Bernoulli class; \(p=7\) is an extra coefficient-induced exception. A Gross–Koblitz derivation is plausible but not supplied. No theorem currently identified here equates this defect with the same mod-\(p\) rank-\(2\) square-root object.
