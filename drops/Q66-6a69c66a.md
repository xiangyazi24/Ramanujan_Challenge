# Direct finite-field hypergeometric zero density analysis

## Conclusion

The desired theorem

\[\#\{p\in(m,2m]:p\mid b_m\}\ll \log m\]

is not currently accessible from finite-field hypergeometric theory alone. The obstruction is that this is a moving-characteristic problem: the prime defining the finite field is the same prime being counted.

For fixed p, Katz theory controls the distribution of trace functions as parameters vary. Here m is fixed and p varies, so the usual finite-field equidistribution machinery does not directly apply.

## Hypergeometric interpretation

For p>m,

\[b_m=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom mk^2\binom{m+k}{k}^2\pmod p.\]

Every summand is a unit in F_p. The condition p|b_m is therefore cancellation among m+1 structured units.

Weil bounds control the archimedean size of the corresponding hypergeometric trace, but they do not control the probability that the trace vanishes after reduction modulo the defining characteristic.

## Casoratian structure

The identity

\[a_{m-1}b_m-a_mb_{m-1}=-6/m^3\]

implies strong rigidity. After clearing denominators, every prime p>3 dividing b_m also divides the companion value a_{m-1}. Thus large prime divisors of b_m are not arbitrary.

However this does not immediately give sparsity, because a_n and b_n are coupled solutions of the same recurrence. It converts the problem into a question about simultaneous projective orbit behavior modulo varying primes.

## What theorem would suffice

A breakthrough statement would be a primitive-divisor or moving-characteristic zero-density theorem:

\[\#\{p>m:p\mid b_m\}=o(m/\log m).\]

Even

\[\#\{p\in(m,2m]:p\mid b_m\}\ll m/(\log m)^{1+\epsilon}\]

would imply the GCD conjecture.

## Likely missing ingredient

The correct framework is probably not classical finite-field hypergeometric theory, but arithmetic dynamics of the Apéry recurrence:

\[v_{n+1}=M(n)v_n\]

on P^1(F_p). A prime divisor corresponds to the orbit hitting a distinguished point. The Casoratian prevents adjacent hits, but a global bound requires proving that many characteristics cannot force the same orbit collision.

Thus the final obstacle is a primitive-divisor theorem for the Apéry motive rather than a standard character-sum estimate.
