# Q3284 — exact classification of the `C*` carrier defect

## Verdict

There is a clean exact abstract answer, but it is negative for the hoped-for unconditional compression.

Let `R*` be the lattice generated by the three-row Bezout relations whose `T`-values are the numbers `C*_{s,K}`, and let `K` be the full integral relation lattice among all rows from slopes 4 and 7. Then

\[
\boxed{\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}
 = [\tau(K):\tau(R^*)]
 = [K:R^*+(K\cap\ker\tau)]}
\]

and in fact

\[
\boxed{
K/(R^*+(K\cap\ker\tau))\simeq
\mathbf Z/(\Gamma/\Delta)\mathbf Z .}
\]

Thus `Gamma/Delta` is exactly the **`T`-visible saturation defect** of the three-row carrier lattice. It is not the ordinary saturation index of `R*`: the latter has free corank at least three. Those three missing free directions are, generically, exactly

1. one internal direction on the slope-4 interval;
2. one internal direction on the slope-7 interval;
3. one cross-slope selector direction.

The special hypergeometric vector `e_K` controls a finite torsion defect of the *full adjacent-relation lattice*, but it does **not** control the missing free direction left after replacing the adjacent values `V_K` by the `C*_{s,K}`. Consequently, from the hypotheses in the question alone there is no bound at all on `Gamma/Delta`, even with the two `e`-vectors fixed to the actual q=6 hypergeometric vectors and even with the selector values `B_s^H` prescribed. For every integer `M` one can choose integral `T_K` satisfying all stated lattice hypotheses with

\[
D_4=D_7=1,\qquad \Delta=1,\qquad M\mid\Gamma.
\]

So `Gamma/Delta` is **not** forced to divide any fixed endpoint expression, any product depending only on the local recurrence coefficients, or any polynomial in `m,H`; its logarithmic height can be made arbitrarily large with `e`, the intervals, and the selector values held fixed.

There is, however, a sharp conditional repair. Define the complementary value

\[
W_{s,K}:=
 \frac{(A_K+2)V_K-A_KV_{K+1}}{g_K},
\qquad A_K=X_s+2K+1,
\qquad g_K=\gcd(A_K,2).
\]

Then `(C*_{s,K},W_{s,K})` is a unimodular transform of `(V_K,V_{K+1})`. In particular

\[
\boxed{\gcd(C^*_{s,K},W_{s,K})=\gcd(V_K,V_{K+1})}
\]

and, over the whole slope interval,

\[
\boxed{
\gcd\bigl(\{C^*_{s,K}\}_K\cup\{W_{s,K}\}_K\bigr)
 =\gcd_K V_K .}
\]

So `W` is exactly the complementary information discarded by `C*`. One additional `W`-type congruence per slope, together with one cross-slope selector congruence, is the minimal kind of new Apéry-specific input needed. With such input one gets an explicit product bound whose logarithmic height is `O(L_s log(|X_s|+B_s))`, where `L_s=B_s-A_s`; in the usual short-window regime this is of the desired `O(H^2+(log m)^2)` scale. Without those three missing scalar constraints, no theorem of that form follows.

The rest gives the exact statements and proofs.

## 1. Relation lattices

Fix one slope `s`, and abbreviate its cutoff interval by

\[
I_s=[a,b]\cap\mathbf Z,
\qquad a=A_s,\quad b=B_s.
\]

Let

\[
F_s:=\bigoplus_{K=a}^{b}\mathbf Z\,\delta_K,
\]

and define homomorphisms

\[
\varepsilon_s:F_s\to\mathbf Z,
\qquad \varepsilon_s(\delta_K)=e_K,
\]

\[
\tau_s:F_s\to\mathbf Z,
\qquad \tau_s(\delta_K)=T_K.
\]

Because the vector `(e_K)` is primitive,

\[
\gcd_K e_K=1,
\]

`epsilon_s` is onto. Hence

\[
K_s:=\ker\varepsilon_s
\]

is a saturated direct summand of `F_s` of rank `b-a`.

For `a <= K <= b-1`, put

\[
a_{s,K}:=(X_s+K)\delta_K+(K+1)\delta_{K+1}.
\]

The recurrence

\[
(K+1)e_{K+1}=-(X_s+K)e_K
\]

says exactly that

\[
a_{s,K}\in K_s.
\]

Moreover

\[
\tau_s(a_{s,K})=(X_s+K)T_K+(K+1)T_{K+1}=V_K.
\]

Write

\[
R_s^{\rm adj}:=\langle a_{s,K}:a\le K\le b-1\rangle_{\mathbf Z}.
\]

For `a <= K <= b-2`, let

\[
\alpha_K:=X_s+2K+1,
\qquad g_K:=\gcd(\alpha_K,2)=\gcd(\alpha_K,\alpha_K+2),
\]

choose

\[
u_K\alpha_K+v_K(\alpha_K+2)=g_K,
\]

and define

\[
c_{s,K}:=u_Ka_{s,K}+v_Ka_{s,K+1}.
\]

Then

\[
\tau_s(c_{s,K})=u_KV_K+v_KV_{K+1}=C^*_{s,K}.
\]

Thus the three-row carrier lattice is

\[
R_s^*:=\langle c_{s,K}:a\le K\le b-2\rangle_{\mathbf Z}
 \subset R_s^{\rm adj}\subset K_s.
\]

There is also a useful explanation for the number `g_K`. Let

\[
\sigma\!\left(\sum x_J\delta_J\right):=\sum x_J.
\]

Then

\[
\sigma(a_{s,K})=X_s+2K+1=\alpha_K,
\]

and therefore

\[
\sigma(c_{s,K})
 =u_K\alpha_K+v_K(\alpha_K+2)
 =g_K.
\]

Equivalently, if the three consecutive lifted rows are written as

\[
q_K=(e_K,1,T_K),
\]

then

\[
(X_s+K)q_K+(K+1)q_{K+1}=(0,\alpha_K,V_K),
\]

\[
(X_s+K+1)q_{K+1}+(K+2)q_{K+2}
 =(0,\alpha_K+2,V_{K+1}),
\]

and the Bezout combination gives

\[
(0,g_K,C^*_{s,K}).
\]

This is the exact three-row origin of `C*`.

## 2. Why `D_s` is the full relation-value gcd

The row map

\[
\Phi_s:F_s\to\mathbf Z^2,
\qquad
\Phi_s(x)=(\varepsilon_s(x),\tau_s(x))
\]

has image `L_s`. By hypothesis

\[
L_s=\mathbf Z(1,B_s^H)+\mathbf Z(0,D_s).
\]

Because `epsilon_s` is onto, the pure vertical part of `L_s` is exactly

\[
L_s\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbf Z)
 =\{0\}\times\tau_s(K_s).
\]

The displayed Smith form therefore gives

\[
\boxed{\tau_s(K_s)=D_s\mathbf Z.}
\]

This also proves directly the standard statement that `D_s` is the gcd of all `2 x 2` minors of the rows `(e_K,T_K)`.

Define

\[
G_s:=\gcd_{a\le K\le b-1}V_K,
\qquad
\Gamma_s:=\gcd_{a\le K\le b-2}C^*_{s,K}.
\]

Since

\[
R_s^*\subset R_s^{\rm adj}\subset K_s,
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{D_s\mid G_s\mid\Gamma_s.}
\]

The first useful factorization is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma_s}{D_s}
 =\frac{\Gamma_s}{G_s}\frac{G_s}{D_s}.}
\]

The two factors have very different meanings:

- `G_s/D_s` is a **finite saturation defect of the full adjacent recurrence relations**. The hypergeometric form of `e_K` controls it explicitly.
- `Gamma_s/G_s` is the defect created by throwing away one free adjacent mode and retaining only the `C*` combinations. This factor is not controlled by `e_K`.

This distinction is the main point.

## 3. Exact hypergeometric/Smith index for the adjacent relations

The relations `a_{s,K}` form the rows of the bidiagonal `(b-a) x (b-a+1)` matrix

\[
\mathcal A_s=
\begin{pmatrix}
X_s+a & a+1 \\
& X_s+a+1 & a+2 \\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&X_s+b-1&b
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For `j in [a,b]`, delete column `j`. The resulting maximal minor is, up to sign,

\[
M_{s,j}:=
 \prod_{i=a}^{j-1}(X_s+i)
 \prod_{i=j}^{b-1}(i+1).
\]

Hence the top determinantal divisor is

\[
\boxed{
I_s:=\gcd_{j=a}^{b}|M_{s,j}|.}
\]

Because all `X_s+i` and `i+1` are nonzero on the legal interval, `A_s` has rank `b-a`. Its rational row space is `ker epsilon_s tensor Q`; since `K_s` is the integral saturation of that rational space,

\[
\boxed{[K_s:R_s^{\rm adj}]=I_s.}
\]

There is an even more direct hypergeometric interpretation. The signed cofactor vector

\[
z_j:=(-1)^{j-a}M_{s,j}
\]

spans the one-dimensional integer nullspace of `A_s` and satisfies

\[
(i+1)z_{i+1}=-(X_s+i)z_i.
\]

The primitive integer solution of this recurrence is exactly the given `e`-vector. Therefore

\[
\boxed{z_j=\pm I_s e_j}
\]

with one global choice of sign. This is the exact Smith meaning of the `content` normalization in

\[
e_K=(-1)^K\binom{X_s+K-1}{K}/\operatorname{content}.
\]

Now apply `tau_s`. The quotient map gives a surjection

\[
K_s/R_s^{\rm adj}	woheadrightarrow
D_s\mathbf Z/G_s\mathbf Z.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{\frac{G_s}{D_s}\mid I_s.}
\]

This is a genuine uniform theorem, not a finite-scan statement.

There are two useful exact corollaries.

First, for every prime `p`,

\[
\boxed{
v_p(I_s)=
\min_{a\le j\le b}
\left(
\sum_{i=a}^{j-1}v_p(X_s+i)
+
\sum_{i=j}^{b-1}v_p(i+1)
\right).}
\]

Second, when `a >= 0`, since `I_s` divides every maximal minor, in particular the left-end minor,

\[
\boxed{
I_s\mid\prod_{i=a}^{b-1}(i+1)=\frac{b!}{a!}.}
\]

Likewise

\[
I_s\mid\prod_{i=a}^{b-1}|X_s+i|.
\]

Thus the full-adjacent defect has the endpoint-height bound

\[
\log\frac{G_s}{D_s}
\le \log I_s
\le \log\frac{b!}{a!}
\le (b-a)\log b
\]

for `1 <= a < b`; the obvious variant holds when `a=0`.

This is about as sharp an unconditional use of the hypergeometric `e_K` as one can get. It does **not** bound `Gamma_s/G_s`.

## 4. The exact missing mode in `C*`

Identify `R_s^{adj}` with a free lattice on the adjacent relations:

\[
N_s:=\bigoplus_{K=a}^{b-1}\mathbf Z f_K,
\qquad f_K\longmapsto a_{s,K}.
\]

This is an isomorphism because the bidiagonal rows are rationally independent.

Under this identification, `R_s^*` corresponds to

\[
B_s:=\langle
u_K f_K+v_K f_{K+1}:a\le K\le b-2
\rangle.
\]

(Here the first coefficient is `u_K`; the displayed `nu_K` is only the ASCII name for `u_K`, not a new quantity.)

Define

\[
\nu_s:N_s\to\mathbf Z,
\qquad \nu_s(f_K)=V_K.
\]

Then

\[
\nu_s(N_s)=G_s\mathbf Z,
\qquad
\nu_s(B_s)=\Gamma_s\mathbf Z.
\]

Therefore the one-slope defect has the exact formula

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma_s}{G_s}
=[\nu_s(N_s):\nu_s(B_s)]
=[N_s:B_s+(N_s\cap\ker\nu_s)].}
\]

The matrix of `B_s` has only `b-a-1` rows in the free lattice `N_s` of rank `b-a`. Thus there is one free missing mode (in the nondegenerate case, exactly one). A finite Smith index of `B_s` cannot see it because the ordinary index `[N_s:B_s]` is infinite.

That is why a determinantal/content argument on the `C*` rows alone cannot classify the excess.

## 5. A canonical complementary carrier `W`: unimodularity

Set

\[
\omega_{s,K}:=
\frac{\alpha_K+2}{g_K}a_{s,K}
-
\frac{\alpha_K}{g_K}a_{s,K+1}.
\]

This is an integral relation. Its `T`-value is

\[
\boxed{
W_{s,K}:=\tau_s(\omega_{s,K})
=\frac{(\alpha_K+2)V_K-\alpha_KV_{K+1}}{g_K}.}
\]

Now compare the two relations `(c_{s,K},omega_{s,K})` with `(a_{s,K},a_{s,K+1})`. The coefficient matrix is

\[
\begin{pmatrix}
u_K&v_K\\
(\alpha_K+2)/g_K&-\alpha_K/g_K
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is

\[
-\frac{u_K\alpha_K+v_K(\alpha_K+2)}{g_K}=-1.
\]

Hence this matrix is unimodular. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf Zc_{s,K}+\mathbf Z\omega_{s,K}
=\mathbf Za_{s,K}+\mathbf Za_{s,K+1}.}
\]

Applying `tau_s` gives the local gcd identity

\[
\boxed{
\gcd(C^*_{s,K},W_{s,K})
=\gcd(V_K,V_{K+1}).}
\]

Taking all `K` gives

\[
\boxed{
\gcd\bigl(
\{C^*_{s,K}:a\le K\le b-2\}
\cup
\{W_{s,K}:a\le K\le b-2\}
\bigr)
=G_s.}
\]

This identifies the loss exactly: `C*` is one coordinate of a unimodular pair, and `W` is the omitted coordinate.

It also shows explicitly that `Gamma_s` depends on the chosen Bezout coefficients. Every other Bezout solution has the form

\[
u'_K=u_K+t\frac{\alpha_K+2}{g_K},
\qquad
v'_K=v_K-t\frac{\alpha_K}{g_K},
\]

so

\[
\boxed{C^{*\prime}_{s,K}=C^*_{s,K}+tW_{s,K}.}
\]

Thus even the numerical value of `Gamma` is not a Bezout-choice invariant unless one fixes the choices or includes the complementary `W` values. Any claimed universal classification of `Gamma/Delta` must account for this.

## 6. The combined two-slope lattice and the exact formula for `Gamma/Delta`

Let

\[
F:=F_4\oplus F_7,
\]

and define

\[
\varepsilon(x_4,x_7):=\varepsilon_4(x_4)+\varepsilon_7(x_7),
\]

\[
\tau(x_4,x_7):=\tau_4(x_4)+\tau_7(x_7).
\]

Let

\[
K:=\ker\varepsilon,
\qquad
R^*:=R_4^*\oplus R_7^*\subset K.
\]

Because each slope row lattice is

\[
L_s=\mathbf Z(1,B_s^H)+\mathbf Z(0,D_s),
\]

the combined row lattice is generated by

\[
(1,B_4^H),\ (0,D_4),\ (1,B_7^H),\ (0,D_7).
\]

Equivalently it is generated by

\[
(1,B_4^H),\ (0,D_4),\ (0,D_7),\
(0,B_7^H-B_4^H).
\]

Hence its pure vertical subgroup is

\[
\{0\}\times
\gcd(D_4,D_7,B_7^H-B_4^H)\mathbf Z.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{\tau(K)=\Delta\mathbf Z.}
\]

On the other hand, by definition of `Gamma`,

\[
\boxed{\tau(R^*)=\Gamma\mathbf Z.}
\]

This reproves `Delta | Gamma` and gives

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}
=[\Delta\mathbf Z:\Gamma\mathbf Z]
=[\tau(K):\tau(R^*)].}
\]

By the first isomorphism theorem,

\[
K/(K\cap\ker\tau)\simeq\Delta\mathbf Z.
\]

The image of `R*` in this quotient is `Gamma Z`. Thus

\[
\boxed{
K/(R^*+(K\cap\ker\tau))
\simeq
\Delta\mathbf Z/\Gamma\mathbf Z
\simeq
\mathbf Z/(\Gamma/\Delta)\mathbf Z.}
\]

This is the requested exact abstract lattice/index formula.

### Rank anatomy

Let

\[
n_s=|I_s|=b_s-a_s+1,
\qquad N=n_4+n_7.
\]

Because the combined `e`-list is primitive,

\[
\operatorname{rank}K=N-1.
\]

But `R_s^*` has at most `n_s-2` generators, so

\[
\operatorname{rank}R^*
\le(n_4-2)+(n_7-2)=N-4.
\]

Thus

\[
\operatorname{rank}(K/R^*)\ge3.
\]

In the legal nondegenerate case the `C*` rows have full possible rank, and this is equality. The three free directions are exactly the two one-slope missing modes plus the one cross-slope relation.

To see the cross direction concretely, choose `w_s in F_s` with

\[
\varepsilon_s(w_s)=1,
\qquad
\tau_s(w_s)=B_s^H.
\]

Then

\[
h_\times:=(-w_4,w_7)\in K
\]

and

\[
\tau(h_\times)=B_7^H-B_4^H.
\]

This is the third scalar mode that `R_4^* direct-sum R_7^*` never sees.

## 7. Exact prime-by-prime decomposition of the global excess

Put

\[
E_\times:=B_7^H-B_4^H.
\]

Since `Delta | Gamma`, an elementary valuation calculation gives the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}
=\operatorname{lcm}\left(
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,D_4)},
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,D_7)},
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,E_\times)}
\right).}
\]

Indeed, for a prime `p`, if

\[
a=v_p(\Gamma),\quad d_s=v_p(D_s),\quad e=v_p(E_\times),
\]

then

\[
v_p(\Gamma/\Delta)=a-\min(d_4,d_7,e),
\]

while the valuation of the displayed lcm is

\[
\max\bigl(
\max(0,a-d_4),
\max(0,a-d_7),
\max(0,a-e)
\bigr),
\]

which is the same number because `min(d_4,d_7,e) <= a` follows from `Delta | Gamma`.

This formula is useful diagnostically: every excess prime is caused by at least one of the two internal modes or by the cross selector mode. There is no fourth source.

## 8. A parametrized counterexample with the actual hypergeometric `e` fixed

The preceding rank count is not merely suggestive. It yields an unconditional counterexample mechanism.

### Theorem (unbounded excess for fixed recurrence vectors)

Fix:

- the slope-4 and slope-7 intervals, each of length at least three;
- the two primitive nonzero integer vectors `e_K` satisfying the stated first-order recurrence (in particular, one may fix them to the actual q=6 hypergeometric vectors);
- all chosen Bezout pairs `(u_K,v_K)`;
- arbitrary prescribed selector integers `beta_4,beta_7`.

Then for every integer `M>=1` there exist integer values `T_K` on both slopes such that

\[
L_s=\mathbf Z(1,\beta_s)+\mathbf Z(0,1),
\]

so `D_s=1` and one may take `B_s^H=beta_s`, while every `C^*_{s,K}` is divisible by `M` and at least one of them is nonzero. Consequently

\[
\Delta=1,\qquad M\mid\Gamma,
\]

and therefore `Gamma/Delta` is arbitrarily large although `e`, the intervals, the Bezout choices, and the selector values are fixed.

### Proof

For one slope, let

\[
S_s:=\operatorname{Sat}_{K_s}(R_s^*)
=K_s\cap(R_s^*\otimes\mathbf Q).
\]

Since `R_s^*` has rank at most `n_s-2` whereas `K_s` has rank `n_s-1`, `S_s` is a proper saturated sublattice of `K_s`. Therefore

\[
K_s=S_s\oplus C_s
\]

for a nonzero free complement `C_s`.

Pick a nonzero `r_s in R_s^*`. Because `S_s` is free, there is a homomorphism

\[
\lambda_s:S_s\to\mathbf Z
\]

with `lambda_s(r_s) != 0`. Choose a primitive element `y_s in C_s` and a homomorphism on `C_s` taking `y_s` to `1`. Define

\[
\tau_s|_{S_s}:=M\lambda_s,
\qquad
\tau_s(y_s):=1
\]

and extend arbitrarily on the rest of a basis of `C_s`.

Then

\[
\tau_s(K_s)=\mathbf Z,
\]

but

\[
\tau_s(R_s^*)\subset M\mathbf Z,
\]

with at least one nonzero value.

Since `e` is primitive, `K_s` is a direct summand of `F_s`. Choose a selector `w_s` with `epsilon_s(w_s)=1`, so

\[
F_s=K_s\oplus\mathbf Zw_s.
\]

Extend `tau_s` from `K_s` to `F_s` by prescribing

\[
\tau_s(w_s)=\beta_s.
\]

Finally set

\[
T_K:=\tau_s(\delta_K).
\]

These are integers. The row lattice contains `(1,beta_s)` from `w_s` and `(0,1)` from `y_s`, hence

\[
L_s=\mathbf Z(1,\beta_s)+\mathbf Z(0,1).
\]

Thus `D_s=1`. Repeat independently for both slopes. All `C*` values are multiples of `M`, at least one is nonzero, and

\[
\Delta=\gcd(1,1,\beta_7-\beta_4)=1.
\]

So `M | Gamma/Delta`. QED.

### Consequences

This counterexample already uses the exact hypergeometric `e` if desired. Therefore no theorem based only on

- the recurrence for `e`;
- primitivity/nonvanishing of `e`;
- the cutoff endpoints;
- the two selector values;
- the local Bezout gcds `g_K`;

can imply any finite bound for `Gamma/Delta`.

In particular, there is no universal statement of the form

\[
\Gamma/\Delta\mid P(m,H)
\]

for a fixed nonzero polynomial `P`, no universal divisor by a fixed endpoint expression, and no `o(m)` logarithmic-height bound from those data alone. Given any proposed fixed expression, choose `M` with a prime factor not dividing it. Given any proposed height bound, choose `M` larger than its exponential.

The observed q=6 values `1,2,11,...` therefore cannot be promoted to a finite classification without an Apéry-specific statement about `T_K`.

## 9. The smallest useful additional premise: one complementary scalar per free mode

The counterexample proof also proves a minimality statement. In the nondegenerate case `K/R*` has free rank three. If one leaves any one of these three directions arithmetically uncontrolled, `tau` can be modified on that direction exactly as in the preceding construction while preserving all information on `R*`; the excess can then acquire an arbitrary new factor.

So a genuine Apéry theorem must control **three independent scalar modes**:

1. one complementary value on the slope-4 adjacent chain;
2. one complementary value on the slope-7 adjacent chain;
3. the cross-slope selector value.

The `W_{s,K}` above gives an explicit natural choice for the first two.

### One-anchor determinant bound

Fix one slope and one anchor `j in [a,b-2]`. Consider the square matrix whose rows are

- all `c_{s,K}`, `a <= K <= b-2`, and
- the single complementary relation `omega_{s,j}`.

Relative to the basis `a_{s,a},...,a_{s,b-1}`, its determinant has absolute value

\[
\boxed{
H_{s,j}:=
\left|
\prod_{i=a}^{j-1}u_i
\prod_{i=j+1}^{b-2}v_i
\right|.}
\]

Proof: the pair `(c_{s,j},omega_{s,j})` is a unimodular transform of the two basis vectors `(a_{s,j},a_{s,j+1})`; after replacing that pair by the basis pair, the remaining left and right bidiagonal blocks are triangular, contributing respectively `prod_{i<j}u_i` and `prod_{i>j}v_i`.

Let `gamma` be any positive integer dividing all `C^*_{s,K}` and also dividing `W_{s,j}`. Since `V/G_s` is a primitive integer vector, the adjugate identity for this square matrix gives

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\gamma}{\gcd(\gamma,G_s)}\mid H_{s,j}.}
\]

Combining this with `G_s/D_s | I_s` yields

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\gamma}{\gcd(\gamma,D_s)}
\mid I_s H_{s,j}.}
\]

Taking `gamma=Gamma` is legitimate because `Gamma` divides every `C*` on each slope. Hence the single extra premise

\[
\boxed{\Gamma\mid W_{s,j_s}}
\]

for one anchor `j_s` on slope `s` implies

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,D_s)}
\mid I_sH_{s,j_s}.}
\]

This is the sharp explicit form of “one more scalar per slope”.

If instead one can prove

\[
\Gamma\mid W_{s,K}
\quad\text{for every }K,
\]

then the unimodular gcd identity gives immediately

\[
\Gamma\mid G_s,
\]

so the free-mode factor disappears completely and

\[
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,D_s)}\mid I_s.
\]

## 10. Explicit local product for the natural Bezout choice

Because `alpha_{K+1}=alpha_K+2`, the parity of `alpha_K` is constant along a slope interval. There is a particularly simple coherent Bezout choice.

### Case 1: `alpha_K` even

Then `g_K=2`, and one may take

\[
u_K=-1,\qquad v_K=1,
\]

since

\[
-\alpha_K+(\alpha_K+2)=2.
\]

Thus

\[
H_{s,j}=1
\]

for every anchor. So, in this parity,

\[
\boxed{
\Gamma\mid W_{s,j}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,D_s)}\mid I_s.}
\]

No extra local product is lost.

### Case 2: `alpha_K` odd

Then `g_K=1`. Put

\[
t_K:=\frac{\alpha_K+1}{2}.
\]

Since `t_{K+1}=t_K+1`, one may take

\[
u_K=t_K,
\qquad
v_K=-(t_K-1).
\]

Indeed

\[
t_K\alpha_K-(t_K-1)(\alpha_K+2)=1.
\]

For the legal positive q=6 ranges (so that no degenerating zero coefficient is encountered), the anchor determinant simplifies to a value independent of the anchor:

\[
\boxed{
H_s=
\prod_{i=a}^{b-3}|t_i|
=\prod_{i=a}^{b-3}
\left|\frac{X_s+2i+2}{2}\right|.}
\]

The empty product is `1` when `b-a=2`.

Equivalently, this is the top determinantal divisor of the `(b-a-1) x (b-a)` coefficient matrix of the `C*` relations. To see it directly, its maximal minors are

\[
\prod_{i=a}^{j-1}u_i
\prod_{i=j}^{b-2}v_i,
\qquad a\le j\le b-1.
\]

After factoring

\[
P=\prod_{i=a}^{b-3}t_i,
\]

the remaining factors are the consecutive integers

\[
t_a-1,t_a,t_a+1,\ldots,t_{b-2},
\]

whose gcd is `1`; hence the determinantal divisor is exactly `|P|`.

Therefore one Apéry-specific anchor congruence gives

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,D_s)}
\mid
I_s
\prod_{i=a}^{b-3}
\left|\frac{X_s+2i+2}{2}\right|.}
\]

This is the strongest simple local-product theorem I see. It is conditional on precisely the missing scalar congruence; it is not a consequence of the hypergeometric `e` alone.

## 11. Conditional global divisor and height bound

Recall

\[
E_\times=B_7^H-B_4^H.
\]

Suppose that for the actual Apéry `T_K` one proves:

- for each `s in {4,7}`, there is one anchor `j_s` such that
  \[
  \Gamma\mid W_{s,j_s};
  \]
- the cross missing mode has a divisor bound
  \[
  \frac{\Gamma}{\gcd(\Gamma,E_\times)}\mid H_\times
  \]
  for some explicit integer `H_x`.

Then the exact lcm formula gives

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}
\mid
\operatorname{lcm}
\bigl(I_4H_{4,j_4},\ I_7H_{7,j_7},\ H_\times\bigr).}
\]

In particular it divides the product of those three integers.

A stronger cross congruence

\[
\Gamma\mid E_\times
\]

makes `H_x=1`, so

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}
\mid
\operatorname{lcm}
\bigl(I_4H_{4,j_4},I_7H_{7,j_7}\bigr).}
\]

For nonnegative cutoffs,

\[
I_s\mid B_s!/A_s!,
\]

and for the canonical Bezout choices above `H_s` is either `1` or the displayed product of linear factors. Hence

\[
\log(I_sH_s)
\ll
(B_s-A_s)\log(B_s+1)
+
(B_s-A_s)\log(|X_s|+B_s+2).
\]

Thus, writing `L_s=B_s-A_s`,

\[
\boxed{
\log(I_sH_s)
=O\bigl(L_s\log(|X_s|+B_s+2)\bigr).}
\]

Whenever the q=6 legal window has `L_s=O(H)` and `|X_s|+B_s` polynomially bounded in `m+H`, this becomes

\[
O(H\log(m+H))
\subset O(H^2+(\log(m+H))^2).
\]

So the desired short logarithmic height is available **after** the three missing scalar modes are controlled. It is not available before that.

Notice also that the product in the odd-parity case has degree growing with the interval length. Therefore the abstract argument does not produce a fixed-degree polynomial in `m,H`; what it produces is a controlled product with logarithmic height linear in the interval length.

## 12. What must be proved for the actual q=6 Apéry `T_K`

The smallest genuinely new premise is not another fact about `e_K`. The `e`-side is already exhausted by the exact adjacent Smith index `I_s`.

What is missing is an Apéry-specific identity constraining the three free `T`-modes.

A particularly concrete target is:

### Internal anchor, slope 4
Prove for one legal `j_4`

\[
\Gamma\mid
\frac{(\alpha_{4,j_4}+2)V_{4,j_4}
      -\alpha_{4,j_4}V_{4,j_4+1}}
     {g_{4,j_4}}.

### Internal anchor, slope 7
Prove for one legal `j_7`

\[
\Gamma\mid
\frac{(\alpha_{7,j_7}+2)V_{7,j_7}
      -\alpha_{7,j_7}V_{7,j_7+1}}
     {g_{7,j_7}}.

### Cross anchor
Prove either

\[
\Gamma\mid B_7^H-B_4^H
\]

or, more generally, prove an explicit small divisor bound for

\[
\Gamma/\gcd(\Gamma,B_7^H-B_4^H).
\]

These are three scalar assertions, matching the three missing free directions. If one can derive them from a second Apéry recurrence, a reflection identity, an endpoint formula, or a common hypergeometric antiderivative for `T_K`, the rest is automatic from the lattice algebra above.

Conversely, controlling fewer than three independent scalar modes cannot be sufficient in the abstract: the direct-summand counterexample can vary `tau` on any remaining free mode while fixing all already-controlled data and insert an arbitrary factor `M` into `Gamma/Delta`.

This is the precise sense in which those additional premises are minimal.

## 13. Interpretation of the observed factors `1,2,11,...`

The observations are completely compatible with the theory, but they do not imply a finite classification.

A prime in `Gamma/Delta` can enter through:

1. the finite adjacent Smith defect `G_s/D_s`, which divides `I_s`;
2. the one-slope free-mode defect `Gamma_s/G_s`, i.e. failure of the omitted `W`-coordinate to share the `C*` divisibility;
3. the cross-slope selector defect `Gamma/gcd(Gamma,B_7^H-B_4^H)`;
4. for a noncanonical Bezout choice, extra arithmetic in the chosen coefficient-chain determinant, although the underlying missing-mode phenomenon is the same.

The factors `2` and `11` may therefore be genuine residual saturation factors in the particular Apéry cells, but there is no theorem from the present hypotheses restricting the list to those numbers or to any finite set.

In particular, the fact that `Gamma` is a sufficient target carrier is exactly the reason it can be larger than `Delta`: it sees only a chosen projection of the relation lattice.
