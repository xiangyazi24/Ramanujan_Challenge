# Verdict

The proposed route separates into one correct implication and one unproved—and genuinely difficult—input.

1. **Correct:** a uniform variance bound of size \(O(1)\) on every interval of length \(N\) would imply a pointwise bound \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)=O(\sqrt N)\) on that interval. More generally, variance \(N^{o(1)}\) gives \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\le N^{1/2+o(1)}\), which is enough for \(o(N/\log N)\).
2. **Not supplied by CRT:** neither CRT nor the average zero-mass hypothesis
   
   \[
   \sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll \pi(x)
   \]
   
   implies the required variance estimate. The aggregate remainder
   
   \[
   \sum_{p\ne q}R_{pq}
   \]
   
   is precisely a short-interval, cross-prime discrepancy term. For arbitrary sparse sets \(Z_p\), it can be as large as \(\asymp N^2/\log^2N\), even if every \(z_p\le2\), the sets have Apéry reflection symmetry, and no two residues in a set are consecutive.
3. **Standard large sieve does not close the gap:** after Fourier expansion it gives the classical \(N+Q^2\) bound. Here \(Q\asymp N\), so it gives total mean square \(O(N^2)\), hence average variance \(O(N)\), rather than \(O(1)\). The problem lies entirely beyond the large-sieve square-root barrier \(Q\le\sqrt N\).

Thus the CRT–Chebyshev route is a valid **conditional reduction**, but not an elementary proof. It replaces \((\mathrm{HM})_3\) or the Sparse Bridge Theorem by a new and very strong statement that can be called **short-interval \(\mathrm{HM}_2\)**, **quenched pair dispersion**, or **moving-target second-moment control**.

This diagnosis agrees with the repository state:

- `problems/3.2/pairpalm_result.tex` proves the deterministic second-factorial-moment estimate on an interval of length \(X^2\), the natural size of a two-prime modulus;
- the all-\(n\) problem requires comparable dispersion on an interval of length only \(X\);
- `problems/3.2/UNDERSTANDING.md` identifies the remaining issue as **quenched diagonal anti-concentration across primes**.

# 1. Use a fixed dyadic majorant

The literal definition

\[
B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)
 =\#\{p:\sqrt n<p\le n,\ n\bmod p\in Z_p\}
\]

has a prime range depending on \(n\). For a clean variance calculation, fix a dyadic integer interval

\[
I=(N,2N],\qquad |I|=N,
\]

and define

\[
\mathcal P_N=\{p\text{ prime}:\sqrt N<p\le2N\},
\]

\[
Y_N(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}X_p(n),
\qquad
X_p(n)=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}.
\]

For \(n\in I\), every prime counted by \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\) lies in \(\mathcal P_N\). Hence

\[
B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\le Y_N(n).
\]

It therefore suffices to control \(Y_N\) uniformly on every dyadic interval. This also removes the harmless cutoffs \(p\le n\) and \(p>\sqrt n\) from the variance algebra.

Write

\[
M=|I|=N,
\qquad
\rho_p=\frac{z_p}{p},
\qquad
\lambda_N=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}\rho_p.
\]

Under the proposed arithmetic input

\[
A(x):=\sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll\pi(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log x},
\]

partial summation gives

\[
\lambda_N=O(1),
\]

while

\[
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}z_p\ll\frac{N}{\log N}.
\]

The precise empirical value \(\lambda_N\approx0.5\) is not needed for the argument; boundedness is enough.

# 2. Correct the one-prime and two-prime endpoint errors

## 2.1 Marginals

For any interval \(I\) of length \(M\), each residue class modulo \(p\) occurs \(M/p+O(1)\) times. Therefore

\[
A_p(I):=\#\{n\in I:n\bmod p\in Z_p\}
       =M\frac{z_p}{p}+E_p(I),
\qquad
|E_p(I)|\le z_p.
\]

Thus

\[
\mathbb E_I X_p
 =\frac{z_p}{p}+O\!\left(\frac{z_p}{M}\right).
\]

The error written in the question as \(O(1/p^2)\) is not generally valid. For \(p\asymp M\), a single residue class has probability either \(1/M\) or \(2/M\), whereas \(1/p^2\asymp1/M^2\). The unavoidable endpoint error in probability is \(O(1/M)\) per residue, not \(O(1/p^2)\).

Summing the marginal errors gives

\[
e_N:=\frac1M\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}E_p(I)
 \ll\frac1M\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}z_p
 \ll\frac1{\log N}=o(1).
\]

## 2.2 Pairs

For distinct primes \(p,q\), define

\[
C_{pq}(I)
 =\#\{n\in I:n\bmod p\in Z_p,\ n\bmod q\in Z_q\}.
\]

CRT gives exactly \(z_pz_q\) residue classes modulo \(pq\). Hence

\[
C_{pq}(I)
 =M\frac{z_pz_q}{pq}+R_{pq}(I),
\qquad
|R_{pq}(I)|\le z_pz_q.
\]

Because \(p,q>\sqrt N\) and \(M=N\), one has \(pq>M\). Each individual CRT class therefore contributes either zero or one point of \(I\). For a fixed class the count is

\[
\frac{M}{pq}+O(1),
\]

and after division by \(M\) the probability is

\[
\frac1{pq}+O\!\left(\frac1M\right).
\]

The \(O(1/M)\) error is larger than the main term whenever \(pq>M\). Thus CRT gives no pairwise equidistribution in this range; it merely identifies the discrepancy \(R_{pq}\).

# 3. Exact variance identity

Let the pair sum be over **ordered** pairs \(p\ne q\), and put

\[
R_N^{(2)}(I)
 =\sum_{\substack{p,q\in\mathcal P_N\\p\ne q}}R_{pq}(I).
\]

Then

\[
\mathbb E_I Y_N=\lambda_N+e_N,
\]

and

\[
\mathbb E_I (Y_N)_2
 =\sum_{p\ne q}\rho_p\rho_q+rac{R_N^{(2)}(I)}M
 =\lambda_N^2-\sum_p\rho_p^2+rac{R_N^{(2)}(I)}M.
\]

Since \(Y^2=Y+(Y)_2\), we obtain the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}_I(Y_N)
 =\lambda_N-\sum_p\rho_p^2
  +\frac{R_N^{(2)}(I)}M
  +e_N(1-2\lambda_N)-e_N^2.}
\]

The actual Apéry zero sets satisfy the uniform bound

\[
z_p=O(p^{2/3})
\]

from `problems/3.2/proof.tex`. Hence, for \(p>\sqrt N\),

\[
\max_p\rho_p=O(N^{-1/6}),
\]

and therefore

\[
\sum_p\rho_p^2
 \le \left(\max_p\rho_p\right)\sum_p\rho_p
 =o(1).
\]

Together with \(e_N=o(1)\), this gives

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}_I(Y_N)
 =\lambda_N+rac{R_N^{(2)}(I)}N+o(1).}
\]

Consequently:

- \(R_N^{(2)}(I)=o(N)\) implies the Poisson-scale asymptotic
  
  \[
  \operatorname{Var}_I(Y_N)=\lambda_N+o(1);
  \]
- the weaker one-sided bound
  
  \[
  R_N^{(2)}(I)\ll N
  \]
  
  already implies \(\operatorname{Var}_I(Y_N)=O(1)\);
- even
  
  \[
  R_N^{(2)}(I)\ll N^{1+o(1)}
  \]
  
  suffices for the final \(N^{1/2+o(1)}\) pointwise bound.

Thus the absolute estimate

\[
\left|\sum_{p\ne q}R_{pq}\right|=o(N)
\]

asked for in the question is stronger than necessary. Only an upper bound for the aggregate covariance is needed. But neither version follows from CRT alone.

# 4. Variance \(O(1)\) really would give the maximum bound

Suppose uniformly for all dyadic \(I=(N,2N]\),

\[
\frac1N\sum_{n\in I}
 |Y_N(n)-\overline Y_N|^2\le C.
\]

For any individual \(n\in I\),

\[
|Y_N(n)-\overline Y_N|^2
 \le \sum_{m\in I}|Y_N(m)-\overline Y_N|^2
 \le CN.
\]

Hence

\[
Y_N(n)\le\overline Y_N+\sqrt{CN}=O(\sqrt N),
\]

because \(\overline Y_N=O(1)\). Since \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\le Y_N(n)\),

\[
B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)=O(\sqrt n).
\]

This is simply the deterministic finite-set inequality

\[
\|f\|_\infty\le\|f\|_2.
\]

Chebyshev is not needed. If one does use Chebyshev, setting its upper bound equal to \(1\) proves only that there is at most one exceptional integer; taking a slightly larger threshold, or using the direct \(L^2\)-to-\(L^\infty\) inequality, removes that endpoint issue.

Therefore steps (c) and (d) of the proposed proof chain are correct. The entire problem is step (b).

# 5. Why CRT plus bounded average \(z_p\) cannot prove the needed remainder estimate

There is a simple counterexample for general sparse residue sets.

Choose a rapidly increasing sequence \(N_j\), for example with

\[
N_{j+1}>N_j^3,
\]

so the prime blocks \((N_j/2,N_j]\) are disjoint. For each odd prime

\[
p\in(N_j/2,N_j],
\]

put

\[
r_p=N_j-p=N_j\bmod p
\]

and define

\[
Z_p=\{r_p,\ p-1-r_p\},
\]

collapsing a duplicate at the central residue. Omit the at most two endpoint primes for which one of these residues is \(0\). Put \(Z_p=\varnothing\) outside these blocks.

This artificial family satisfies:

- \(z_p\le2\) for every prime;
- \(\sum_{p\le x}z_p\le2\pi(x)\);
- reflection symmetry
  
  \[
  r\in Z_p\iff p-1-r\in Z_p;
  \]
- \(0,p-1\notin Z_p\);
- no two ordinary consecutive residues in \(Z_p\): the difference between the two reflected residues is even, hence if nonzero it has absolute value at least \(2\).

Nevertheless, at \(n=N_j\), every retained prime in the whole top window satisfies

\[
N_j\bmod p=N_j-p=r_p\in Z_p.
\]

Thus

\[
B_{\mathrm{low}}(N_j)
 \ge \pi(N_j)-\pi(N_j/2)-O(1)
 \asymp\frac{N_j}{\log N_j}.
\]

The forbidden diagonal spike occurs despite the strongest possible local cardinality bound \(z_p\le2\).

The aggregate pair error is correspondingly enormous. For distinct retained \(p,q\in(N_j/2,N_j]\), the integer \(N_j\) is a simultaneous representative of the CRT classes selected by \(r_p,r_q\), so

\[
C_{pq}([1,N_j])\ge1.
\]

On the other hand,

\[
N_j\frac{z_pz_q}{pq}
 \le N_j\frac4{(N_j/2)^2}
 =\frac{16}{N_j}.
\]

Hence

\[
R_{pq}([1,N_j])\ge1-\frac{16}{N_j},
\]

and summing over ordered pairs gives

\[
R_{N_j}^{(2)}
 \gg\left(\frac{N_j}{\log N_j}\right)^2.
\]

This exceeds \(N_j\) by the factor \(N_j/\log^2N_j\). Equivalently, the one spike alone contributes

\[
\frac1{N_j}
 \left(B_{\mathrm{low}}(N_j)-O(1)\right)^2
 \gg\frac{N_j}{\log^2N_j}
\]

to the variance.

This construction is not asserted to come from the Apéry recurrence. Its conclusion is narrower and rigorous:

> No argument using only CRT, the sizes \(z_p\), reflection symmetry, and no-consecutive-zero information can prove \(R_N^{(2)}=O(N)\). A genuinely Apéry-specific cross-prime theorem is indispensable.

For the actual Apéry sets, \(R_N^{(2)}=o(N)\) remains a plausible but presently unproved moving-target discrepancy statement.

# 6. Comparison with the repository’s proved \((\mathrm{HM})_2\)

For a dyadic prime block

\[
\mathcal P_X=\{p:X<p\le2X\},
\]

set

\[
K_X(m)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}1_{m\bmod p\in Z_p},
\qquad
\lambda_X=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}\frac{z_p}{p}.
\]

In `problems/3.2/pairpalm_result.tex`, the interval length is

\[
M=X^2.
\]

The file proves the sharpened deterministic estimate

\[
\sum_{0\le m<M}(K_X(m))_2
 \le4M\lambda_X^2.
\]

The proof is exactly the one-representative CRT argument:

\[
|\Omega_p\cap\Omega_q|\le z_pz_q
\]

because \(pq>X^2=M\), and therefore

\[
\sum_{m<M}(K_X(m))_2
 \le\left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}z_p\right)^2
 \le(2X\lambda_X)^2
 =4X^2\lambda_X^2.
\]

This is the natural deterministic scale: the interval length matches a two-prime modulus.

For integers \(n\asymp X\), however, the relevant interval has length only \(X\). The same argument gives at best

\[
\sum_{m\in I}(K_X(m))_2
 \le\left(\sum_pz_p\right)^2
 \ll X^2\lambda_X^2,
\]

whereas independent sparse behavior predicts

\[
\sum_{m\in I}(K_X(m))_2
 \asymp |I|\lambda_X^2
 \asymp X\lambda_X^2.
\]

The missing saving is a complete factor \(X\).

Under bounded average zero mass, one expects

\[
\lambda_X\asymp\frac1{\log X}.
\]

Thus the two scales are

\[
\text{proved deterministic scale}
 \asymp\frac{X^2}{\log^2X},
\]

and

\[
\text{desired short-interval scale}
 \asymp\frac{X}{\log^2X}.
\]

A single row with

\[
K_X(m)\asymp\frac{X}{\log X}
\]

contributes \(X^2/\log^2X\) by itself, saturating the existing deterministic \((\mathrm{HM})_2\) budget. The \(X^2\)-length theorem therefore permits exactly the isolated spike that the all-\(n\) problem must rule out.

So the proposed route does not merely reuse the proved pair moment. It asks for a substantially stronger **localization** of that moment from length \(X^2\) to length \(X\).

# 7. What the standard large sieve gives

Define the centered \(p\)-periodic function

\[
f_p(n)=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}-\frac{z_p}{p}.
\]

Use normalized Fourier coefficients

\[
\widehat f_p(a)
 =\frac1p\sum_{r\bmod p}f_p(r)e_p(-ar).
\]

Then

\[
\widehat f_p(0)=0,
\qquad
f_p(n)=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\widehat f_p(a)e_p(an).
\]

Parseval gives

\[
\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}|\widehat f_p(a)|^2
 =\frac{z_p}{p}\left(1-\frac{z_p}{p}\right)
 \le\frac{z_p}{p}.
\]

For primes \(p\le Q\), the reduced fractions \(a/p\) are separated modulo \(1\) by at least \(1/Q^2\). The dual large sieve therefore yields, for any interval \(I\) of length \(M\),

\[
\sum_{n\in I}
 \left|\sum_{p\le Q}f_p(n)\right|^2
 \ll (M+Q^2)
 \sum_{p\le Q}\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}|\widehat f_p(a)|^2.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\in I}
 \left|\sum_{p\le Q}f_p(n)\right|^2
 \ll (M+Q^2)\sum_{p\le Q}\frac{z_p}{p}.}
\]

For the range relevant to \(B_{\mathrm{low}}\),

\[
M\asymp N,
\qquad
Q\asymp N,
\qquad
\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le2N}\frac{z_p}{p}=O(1).
\]

Thus the standard large sieve gives only

\[
\sum_{n\in I}|\cdots|^2\ll N^2.
\]

After division by \(|I|=N\), this is average variance \(O(N)\), not \(O(1)\). The corresponding \(L^2\)-to-\(L^\infty\) bound is only \(O(N)\), which does not improve the trivial scale.

For a dyadic modulus block \(p\asymp X\), the same statement is

\[
\sum_{n\in I}
 \left|\sum_{X<p\le2X}f_p(n)\right|^2
 \ll (M+X^2)\lambda_X.
\]

This reveals the exact barrier:

- when \(X\le\sqrt M\), the \(M\)-term dominates and the large sieve can give variance-scale control;
- when \(X>\sqrt M\), the \(X^2\)-term dominates;
- \(B_{\mathrm{low}}\) consists precisely of primes \(p>\sqrt n\), so it lies entirely on the bad side of this threshold.

Taking \(a_n=1\) in the primal large sieve only re-proves the elementary distribution of the interval among individual residue classes. It does not control the cross-prime phases of the sets \(Z_p\). Once the indicators \(1_{Z_p}\) are inserted and Fourier-expanded, one is led back to the dual inequality above and its \(Q^2\) term.

Restricting the moduli to primes may save logarithms in refined forms of the large sieve, but it cannot provide the missing factor \(N\). The aligned \(z_p\le2\) construction proves that no large-sieve inequality valid for arbitrary sparse target sets can have the desired short-interval strength.

Even excellent **one-prime** Fourier magnitude estimates do not suffice by themselves. A singleton set has perfectly flat nonzero Fourier magnitudes, yet singleton targets can be phase-aligned across primes at one integer. The missing input concerns cross-prime Fourier phases or, equivalently, aggregate CRT discrepancy.

# 8. The correct sieve-theoretic analogy and references

The closest standard framework is the **Turán sieve** or an abstract **probabilistic sieve**. In that framework one writes a counting function as a sum of local indicators, expands its second moment, and obtains:

- one-modulus density terms;
- two-modulus density terms;
- explicit two-modulus remainder terms.

Here those two-modulus remainders are exactly \(R_{pq}\). A general Turán-sieve identity exposes them; it does not make them small automatically.

Useful references are:

1. A. C. Cojocaru and M. R. Murty, *An Introduction to Sieve Methods and Their Applications*, Chapter 4 (“The Turán sieve”) and Chapter 8 (“The large sieve”).
2. E. Kowalski, *The Large Sieve and its Applications*, Chapter 2 (“The principle of the large sieve”) and Chapter 6 (“Probabilistic sieves”).
3. P. X. Gallagher, “The large sieve,” *Mathematika* **14** (1967), 14–20.
4. H. L. Montgomery and R. C. Vaughan, “The large sieve,” *Mathematika* **20** (1973), 119–134.
5. P. X. Gallagher, “A larger sieve,” *Acta Arithmetica* **18** (1971), 77–81.
6. P. D. T. A. Elliott, *Probabilistic Number Theory I: Mean-Value Theorems*, the chapter “The Turán–Kubilius inequality and its dual,” pp. 147–186.
7. P. D. T. A. Elliott, “A local Turán–Kubilius inequality,” *Acta Arithmetica* **49** (1987), 127–139.

The Turán–Kubilius analogy is informative but not directly transferable. For a genuinely additive function, the basic events are divisibility conditions such as \(p\mid n\). Their residue class is always \(0\), and multiplicativity imposes strong compatibility. In particular, if \(p,q>\sqrt N\), no positive integer \(n\le N\) can be divisible by both \(p\) and \(q\); the large-prime covariance is automatically nonpositive.

For shifted sparse sets \(Z_p\), the opposite can occur: every \(Z_p\) may contain the residue of one common integer. There is no multiplicative exclusivity. This is exactly why the usual Turán–Kubilius proof does not apply.

Gallagher’s larger sieve is not the missing theorem either. It bounds the size of a set of integers that occupies unusually few residue classes modulo many primes. The present problem asks whether many different prime-indexed target sets can all pass through one integer. Occupancy information alone does not prohibit that alignment.

# 9. At what scale does the CRT error become noticeable?

There are two different notions of scale.

## 9.1 Pair-by-pair scale

For primes \(p,q\asymp X\) and an interval of length \(M\), one CRT class has expected count

\[
\frac{M}{pq}\asymp\frac{M}{X^2}.
\]

Its endpoint discrepancy is \(O(1)\). The relative error becomes order \(1\) at

\[
M\asymp pq\asymp X^2.
\]

In the proposed range \(M=N\) and \(p,q>\sqrt N\), one already has \(pq>N=M\). Thus every pair is beyond the equidistribution transition:

- near \(p,q\asymp\sqrt N\), one is at the boundary \(pq\asymp N\);
- near \(p,q\asymp N\), the expected count of one class is only \(\asymp1/N\), while its actual count is \(0\) or \(1\).

So the CRT error is already dominant **pair by pair** throughout the entire range. The empirical Poisson variance can only arise from aggregate cancellation among many such discrepancies.

## 9.2 Aggregate random-model scale

In a model where the rows behave independently with \(Y_N(n)\) approximately Poisson of bounded mean, the sum over \(N\) rows of the centered second-factorial-moment error should fluctuate on the order of \(\sqrt N\). Equivalently,

\[
R_N^{(2)}\approx O_{\mathrm{prob}}(\sqrt N),
\qquad
\frac{R_N^{(2)}}N
 \approx O_{\mathrm{prob}}(N^{-1/2}).
\]

At \(N=10^5\),

\[
N^{-1/2}\approx3.2\times10^{-3}.
\]

That is small enough to be hidden by ordinary finite-sample fluctuations in an observed variance near \(0.5\). Under a genuinely random model there is no later scale at which the normalized CRT correction should suddenly become visible; it should shrink as \(N\) grows.

This is only a heuristic. The periodic events share primes and rows, so they are not independent. The deterministic alternative is radically different: the aligned construction gives

\[
\frac{R_N^{(2)}}N
 \gg\frac{N}{\log^2N}
\]

at arbitrarily large prescribed scales. Therefore there is no universal crossover scale. The computation through \(10^5\) is evidence that the actual Apéry sets are random-like up to that range, but it cannot rule out a sparse sequence of later arithmetic alignments.

# 10. The exact additional theorem that would make the route work

A clean sufficient input is the following dyadic short-interval estimate:

\[
\boxed{
\sup_{I:\,|I|=N}
\sum_{n\in I}
\left|
 \sum_{\sqrt N<p\le2N}
 \left(1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}-\frac{z_p}{p}\right)
\right|^2
\ll N^{1+o(1)}.}
\]

An \(O(N)\) right side would give the literal \(O(\sqrt N)\) maximum. The weaker \(N^{1+o(1)}\) form already gives

\[
B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\le n^{1/2+o(1)}
 =o(n/\log n)
\]

for every \(n\).

After the harmless marginal and diagonal corrections, this estimate is equivalent in strength to the one-sided aggregate pair-dispersion bound

\[
\sup_{I:\,|I|=N}R_N^{(2)}(I)\ll N^{1+o(1)}.
\]

A Poisson asymptotic would ask for the stronger \(R_N^{(2)}(I)=o(N)\).

This theorem would indeed bypass \((\mathrm{HM})_3\), because it directly excludes isolated spikes by \(L^2\)-to-\(L^\infty\). But it should not be described as “pairwise independence from CRT.” It is a new, uniform, Apéry-specific cross-prime anti-concentration theorem—stronger in localization than the repository’s proved \(X^2\)-length \((\mathrm{HM})_2\).

# Direct answers to the four questions

1. **Can one prove \(\left|\sum_{p\ne q}R_{pq}\right|=o(N)\) from CRT and \(\sum z_p\ll\pi(x)\)?**

   No. It is false for general sparse residue families, even with \(z_p\le2\), reflection symmetry, no consecutive residues, and \(0\notin Z_p\). For the actual Apéry sets, such a bound is an open cross-prime discrepancy statement. If proved, it would make the variance route work. A one-sided \(O(N)\) bound is already sufficient.

2. **Can the standard large sieve give the required variance?**

   No. The dual large sieve gives

   \[
   \sum_{n\in I}\left|\sum_pf_p(n)\right|^2
   \ll (|I|+Q^2)\sum_p\frac{z_p}{p}.
   \]

   With \(|I|\asymp N\) and \(Q\asymp N\), this is \(O(N^2)\), not \(O(N)\). The obstruction is exactly the \(Q^2\) square-root barrier.

3. **What is the relevant sieve literature?**

   The exact general framework is the Turán/probabilistic sieve, where the second moment contains explicit two-modulus remainder terms. Turán–Kubilius is the closest analogy, but its additive/divisibility structure is absent here. No standard theorem removes the \(R_{pq}\) terms for arbitrary shifted sparse sets.

4. **When should the CRT error become noticeable?**

   Pairwise, it is already dominant whenever \(pq>N\), hence throughout the proposed range. Empirically small variance means aggregate cancellation. A random model predicts normalized error \(O(N^{-1/2})\), roughly \(0.003\) at \(N=10^5\); an adversarial aligned family can instead create variance \(\gg N/\log^2N\) at arbitrarily large scales.

# Final status of the proposed proof chain

The chain

\[
\sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll\pi(x)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Var}(B_{\mathrm{low}})=O(1)
\]

is not valid from CRT alone.

The corrected conditional chain is

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll\pi(x),\\
&\text{short-interval pair dispersion }R_N^{(2)}\ll N^{1+o(1)}
\end{aligned}}
\]

\[
\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Var}_I(B_{\mathrm{low}})\ll N^{o(1)}
\Longrightarrow
B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\le n^{1/2+o(1)}
\Longrightarrow
G_n=e^{o(n)}.
\]

This is a coherent alternative route, but the second line is the new arithmetic heart of the proof, not a free consequence of CRT.
