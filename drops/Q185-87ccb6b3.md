# The Euler product / Kloosterman interpretation of $S_c$

## Short answer

The analogy with Kloosterman sums is insightful, but the spectral variable $u=\alpha_j$ is not an algebraic summation variable. Therefore the expression

$$
S_c=\sum_j \psi(c(\alpha_j+p^w/\alpha_j))
$$

is **not currently a Kloosterman sum in Katz's sense**.

The obstruction is fundamental: Kloosterman sums arise from applying Fourier transform to a sheaf whose geometric variable is already algebraic. Here the ``variable'' $\alpha_j$ is a Frobenius eigenvalue of another sheaf. It is a spectral parameter, not a point of an algebraic curve.

However, there is a correct sheaf-theoretic replacement: one should regard $\psi(c\,\mathrm{Tr}(M))$ as an **exponential pullback along the trace morphism of the moduli/sheaf parameter space**, not as a Kloosterman sum over eigenvalues.

---

## 1. Why the naive Kloosterman identification fails

For the classical Kloosterman sheaf:

$$
\mathrm{Kl}_2(a)=\sum_{u\in\mathbf F_p^*}\psi(au+a/u),
$$

$u$ is an actual geometric coordinate on $\mathbf G_m$.

The phase

$$
au+a/u
$$

is an algebraic function on the curve $\mathbf G_m$.

Deligne's bound comes from the fact that the Artin--Schreier sheaf

$$
\mathcal L_\psi(au+a/u)
$$

has bounded conductor.

For the Apéry Mellin sheaf $M$ we instead have, at a point $j$,

$$
\mathrm{Frob}_j|M
\sim
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j&0\\0&\beta_j\end{pmatrix},
$$

with

$$
\alpha_j+\beta_j=b_j,
\qquad
\alpha_j\beta_j=p^w.
$$

But $\alpha_j$ is not a function $j\mapsto \alpha(j)$ in the algebraic-geometric sense.

It is only a root of

$$
T^2-b_jT+p^w=0.
$$

The coefficients are algebraic; the roots are defined only after passing to a spectral cover. There is no canonical algebraic map

$$
j\longmapsto \alpha_j.
$$

Hence the Kloosterman variable does not exist geometrically.

---

## 2. The quadratic spectral cover viewpoint

One can introduce the formal spectral curve

$$
Y^2-b(j)Y+p^w=0.
$$

On this double cover, $Y$ is an algebraic coordinate.

Then formally:

$$
\psi(cb_j)
=
\psi(c(Y+p^w/Y)).
$$

This looks exactly like a Kloosterman phase.

But the crucial difference is that the cover itself depends on the Frobenius trace function $b(j)$. It is not an independent algebraic family before knowing the sheaf $M$.

The resulting object is better viewed as an exponential sheaf attached to the trace function:

$$
\mathcal F_c=[c\,\mathrm{Tr}(M)]^*\mathcal L_\psi.
$$

The question is then whether $\mathcal F_c$ has bounded conductor.

---

## 3. Does $\psi\circ\mathrm{Tr}(M)$ define a bounded-conductor sheaf?

Yes, conceptually this construction exists.

Given a lisse sheaf $M$ on a variety $U$, its trace function

$$
t_M(x)=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_x|M)
$$

is an algebraic trace function.

One can compose this function with the Artin--Schreier character:

$$
\psi(ct_M(x)).
$$

This is the trace function of an exponential sheaf obtained from the trace morphism.

However, bounded conductor is **not automatic**.

The conductor depends on the ramification of the function

$$
t_M:U\rightarrow \mathbf A^1.
$$

For a genuine Kloosterman sheaf, the trace map has controlled singularities, giving Swan conductor $1$.

For a general rank-2 sheaf, the composed exponential sheaf can have large Swan conductor if the trace function has complicated poles or wild ramification.

Therefore:

$$
\psi\circ\mathrm{Tr}(M)
$$

exists, but the desired estimate

$$
|S_c|\ll\sqrt p
$$

requires a separate conductor calculation.

---

## 4. Why the symmetric square does not solve the problem

The observation

$$
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Sym}^2 M)
=
\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2
$$

and therefore

$$
=b_j^2-p^w
$$

is correct.

This gives an algebraic trace function:

$$
j\mapsto b_j^2-p^w.
$$

But $S_c$ requires

$$
\psi(cb_j),
$$

not

$$
\psi(c(b_j^2-p^w)).
$$

Knowing all symmetric powers gives moments of $b_j$, but the exponential of the first moment is a nonlinear operation not represented by a finite tensor construction.

One would need the full exponential functor, not merely $\mathrm{Sym}^2$.

---

## 5. The closest Katz framework

Katz's work on exponential sums and convolution studies exactly this general phenomenon:

- start with a sheaf $M$;
- apply Fourier transform or convolution;
- obtain new sheaves whose trace functions are nonlinear transforms of the original trace functions.

The Kloosterman sheaf is one special case where the nonlinear transform is controlled perfectly.

The Apéry situation is closer to a **nonlinear exponential transform of a trace function** than to a classical Kloosterman sum.

The missing theorem would be:

> The exponential sheaf $\mathcal L_\psi(c\,\mathrm{Tr}(M))$ has uniformly bounded conductor as $p$ varies.

If this were proved, then Deligne would indeed give

$$
S_c=O(\sqrt p).
$$

---

## 6. Consequence for Apéry zeros

If one obtained

$$
\left|\sum_j\psi(cb_j)\right|\ll\sqrt p
$$

uniformly for all $c\neq0$, then Fourier inversion gives strong anti-concentration for the values $b_j$.

In particular, the number of zeros

$$
Z_p=\#\{j:b_j=0\}
$$

would satisfy approximately

$$
Z_p\le \frac{p}{p}+O(\sqrt p),
$$

and much stronger estimates could follow after using higher moments.

But the Kloosterman shortcut does not bypass the hard point: proving bounded conductor of the nonlinear exponential transform.

---

# Final conclusion

The spectral Kloosterman picture is a useful heuristic, but not a literal identity.

The correct statement is:

$$
S_c=\sum_j\psi(c\,\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_j|M))
$$

is the trace sum of the exponential sheaf

$$
[c\,\mathrm{Tr}(M)]^*\mathcal L_\psi.
$$

This is a Katz-type object, but unlike the classical Kloosterman sheaf its conductor is not automatically bounded.

The remaining problem is therefore not finding a Kloosterman parametrization of the eigenvalues; it is proving a bounded-conductor theorem for the exponential pullback of the Apéry Mellin trace function.
