# Q678 — Degree of Q_p and the (-2/p) phenomenon

## Executive summary

The observed degree drop is real, but it is not by itself the reason that Apéry zeros are sparse. The polynomial

\[
Q_p(u),\qquad u=r(p-1-r),
\]

is a quotient of a highly structured Frobenius trace function by the reflection involution. Its degree drop detects a missing Frobenius moment, not a generic reduction in complexity. The strong fact is instead:

\[
\#\{u\in S_p:Q_p(u)=0\}=O(1)
\]

empirically, while the degree of Q_p is still about p/2. This sparsity comes from the special trace-function origin of Q_p, not from degree alone.

---

# (A) Exact size of the degree drop

Let

\[
m=(p-1)/2.
\]

Because of reflection symmetry

\[
b_r=b_{p-1-r},
\]

there is a unique polynomial Q_p of degree at most m satisfying

\[
b_r=Q_p(r(p-1-r)).
\]

The leading coefficient is controlled by the top interpolation moment

\[
\sum_{r=1}^{m-1} b_r u_r^m.
\]

The observed identity

\[
\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_r=0
\iff
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=-1
\]

explains the first vanishing coefficient: the top Newton coefficient of Q_p disappears exactly for

\[
p\equiv5,7\pmod8.
\]

However there is currently no theorem that the next coefficient also vanishes. The natural expectation is:

\[
\deg Q_p=m-1
\]

for generic primes with

\[
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=-1,
\]

but additional drops would correspond to higher moment identities

\[
\sum_r b_r u_r^{m-j}=0.
\]

Such identities would be new Frobenius trace identities. They are not forced by the quadratic character condition alone.

So the expected picture is:

| p mod 8 | expected degree |
|---|---|
| 1,3 | (p-1)/2 |
| 5,7 | (p-3)/2 |

with exceptional extra drops possible only when additional Hasse-type moments vanish.

---

# (B) Does degree drop improve the Z_p bound?

Only marginally.

A polynomial of degree d has at most d roots in F_p, hence

\[
|Z_p|\le2d.
\]

The factor 2 comes from the two values of r giving the same

\[
u=r(p-1-r).
\]

If

\[
d=(p-3)/2,
\]

this only gives

\[
|Z_p|\le p-3,
\]

which is far weaker than the observed

\[
|Z_p|\le O(1).
\]

The important issue is not the total root bound but the fact that almost all roots of Q_p lie in the phantom set

\[
F_p\setminus S_p.
\]

The root distribution is highly non-generic.

A degree argument cannot distinguish a polynomial with p/2 roots in S_p from one with only three.

---

# (C) Why the random polynomial model fails

A random degree p/2 polynomial would have about

\[
\frac{|S_p|}{p}\cdot \frac p2 \approx p/4
\]

roots on S_p.

The Apéry polynomial instead has O(1) roots.

The reason is that Q_p is not a random polynomial. It is a Frobenius trace function.

More precisely:

\[
b_r\equiv -\sum_a \theta_p(a)a^r
\]

where \theta_p is the trace of a geometric object (the K3/Kummer motive). Therefore Q_p is obtained by restricting a rank-two/rank-three crystalline object to the reflection quotient.

A random polynomial has independent coefficients. Q_p has:

1. a fixed Picard-Fuchs differential equation;
2. a constrained monodromy group;
3. Frobenius eigenvalue constraints;
4. the involution r↔p-1-r.

The sparsity is therefore a trace-function cancellation phenomenon.

The relevant conjectural statement is not a degree estimate but:

\[
\#\{r:b_r=0\pmod p\}=O(1)
\]

because the zero condition is a codimension-one condition on a bounded-complexity Frobenius orbit.

---

# (D) Factorization and the (-2/p) condition

The factorization pattern is related to the quadratic character, but not simply by splitting into two fixed families.

The condition

\[
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=-1
\]

controls the parity/eigenvalue behavior of the Frobenius trace sum. It forces the trace polynomial to lose its top coefficient.

It does not determine the complete factorization of Q_p.

For small primes one sees:

- a few linear factors;
- several phantom roots;
- large irreducible factors.

This is exactly what one expects from a Frobenius polynomial: the factorization reflects Frobenius orbit structure in the finite field, not merely p mod 8.

A possible refined conjecture is:

*the factorization type of Q_p is governed by the geometric monodromy group of the Apéry motive, with the (-2/p) condition selecting the determinant character component.*

This is analogous to how the factorization of characteristic polynomials of Frobenius elements is controlled by the image of the Galois representation.

---

# (E) Hypergeometric expression for Q_p

There is a partial answer.

The Apéry numbers satisfy a hypergeometric identity:

\[
b_r={}_4F_3
\left(
\begin{matrix}-r,-r,r+1,r+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};1
\right).
\]

Modulo p, the sequence is a finite-field hypergeometric trace function.

The McCarthy-Greene finite-field hypergeometric framework applies to the r-variable sequence. However Q_p is not simply a standard ${}_3F_2$ in u.

The transformation

\[
u=r(p-1-r)
\]

is a quotient by the involution r↔p-1-r. Passing from r to u is a descent problem.

The correct object is closer to a finite-field hypergeometric function on the quotient curve

\[
\mathbb A^1_r/(r\sim -1-r).
\]

Thus Q_p should be viewed as a descended hypergeometric trace function, not a classical one-variable ${}_3F_2$.

A plausible formula would involve a pullback/pushforward of the hypergeometric sheaf under

\[
r\mapsto r(r+1).
\]

This is a geometric explanation for why Q_p has much more structure than a random polynomial.

---

# Final structural picture

The hierarchy is:

\[
\text{Apéry recurrence}
\rightarrow
\text{K3 Frobenius trace}
\rightarrow
\text{hypergeometric sheaf}
\rightarrow
Q_p(u).
\]

The degree drop for p≡5,7 mod 8 is a visible shadow of a Frobenius character identity.

But AFP cannot come from degree alone:

\[
\deg Q_p\sim p/2
\]

still allows O(p) roots.

The missing theorem is a root-distribution theorem for the descended Frobenius trace function:

\[
Q_p(u)=0
\]

should have only O(1) solutions on the physical subset

\[
S_p=\{r(p-1-r):0<r<(p-1)/2\}.
\]

This is a monodromy/equidistribution statement, not a polynomial-degree statement.
