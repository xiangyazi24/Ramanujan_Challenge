# Arithmetic unlikely intersections and the Apéry atom problem

## Short answer

There is a conceptual analogy with Bombieri--Masser--Zannier, Habegger--Pila, and Masser--Zannier unlikely-intersection theorems, but the present Apéry atom problem is not directly covered by them. The obstruction is important: the CRT condition does not create a high-codimension intersection inside a fixed algebraic group or a fixed ambient variety. It creates a family of reductions modulo varying primes, with the parameter prime itself moving.

Pairwise coprimality of the polynomials $b_r(\lambda)$ gives a genuine transversality statement in the $\lambda$-direction, but it does not by itself imply repulsion between different primes.

The correct analogue is closer to a ``moving modulus'' arithmetic sieve problem than to classical unlikely intersections.

---

## 1. Why the BMZ analogy is tempting

For each integer $r$ define

$$
C_r=\{\lambda:b_r(\lambda)=0\}.
$$

The resultant condition

$$
\operatorname{Res}_\lambda(b_r,b_s)\neq0 \qquad(r\neq s)
$$

means that the curves $C_r$ do not meet over characteristic zero. Geometrically, the family

$$
V=\{(r,\lambda):b_r(\lambda)=0\}
$$

has no repeated component in the $λ-direction.

In a classical unlikely-intersection problem, one studies points of a fixed variety that satisfy extra special relations, for example:

- torsion conditions in $\mathbb G_m^n$;
- linear relations among logarithms;
- intersections with special subvarieties.

The extra condition has arithmetic complexity, and height theory shows that too many such intersections force an algebraic explanation.

Here the tempting interpretation is:

$$
m\equiv r_i\pmod {p_i},\qquad b_{r_i}(1)\equiv0\pmod {p_i}
$$

as many specializations of $V$ passing through one CRT point.

However, the analogy breaks at the crucial place.

---

## 2. Why CRT is not a BMZ special relation

The congruence

$$
r_i=m-q_ip_i
$$

is a linear relation, but the coefficients $q_i$ are independent integers depending on the prime. There is no fixed algebraic subgroup containing the tuple

$$
(r_1,\ldots,r_T).
$$

Instead we have

$$
(r_i,\lambda,p_i)\in V(\mathbb F_{p_i}),
$$

with different residue fields for different $i$.

BMZ-type arguments need one ambient arithmetic object. Here the ambient object changes with $p_i$.

The condition

$$
b_{r_i}(1)=0\pmod {p_i}
$$

is a local condition at the prime $p_i$, not an integral point condition on a fixed variety.

Therefore pairwise coprimality gives only:

> the same algebraic root cannot persist simultaneously for two indices $r,s$ over characteristic zero.

It does not give:

> reductions at different primes cannot occur simultaneously.

The latter would require a quantitative independence theorem across primes.

---

## 3. The Chabauty analogy

Chabauty--Coleman works because one has:

- a fixed curve $C/\mathbb Q$;
- a fixed Jacobian $J$;
- rational points lying in a finitely generated subgroup.

The $p$-adic logarithm gives a function vanishing on all rational points, and dimension forces finiteness.

The Apéry atom problem has the opposite geometry:

- the prime $p$ changes;
- the residue field changes;
- the point $r=m\bmod p$ changes.

There is no single $p$-adic analytic space on which all atoms live.

A Chabauty-style method would require constructing a uniform $p$-adic function detecting

$$
b_{m\bmod p}(1)=0
$$

for every $p\in(X,2X]$.

No such uniform annihilating function is currently available.

---

## 4. What transversality does give

The resultant condition is still useful. It rules out a catastrophic algebraic scenario:

$$
b_r(\lambda)=b_s(\lambda)=0
$$

having a common component.

Consequently, if one studies simultaneous zeros at a single prime,

$$
|\{r<p:b_r(1)=0\pmod p\}|=|Z_p|,
$$

one expects strong bounds because there is no common factor forcing many zeros.

This is exactly the direction behind the existing estimates

$$
|Z_p|\ll p^{2/3}.
$$

But the atom problem asks for something different:

$$
K(m)=\#\{p:p\mid b_{m\bmod p}\}.
$$

Here each prime sees a different fiber. Transversality inside one fiber does not directly control intersections between many fibers.

---

## 5. The right replacement: arithmetic sieve / large sieve

The natural object is the family of events

$$
E_p=\{m:m\bmod p\in Z_p\}.
$$

One wants a bound for

$$
\#\{m:\sum_p1_{E_p}\ge T\}.
$$

A BMZ theorem would ideally provide a statement like:

$$
\#\{m:E_{p_1}\cap\cdots\cap E_{p_T}\}\ll X^{2-\delta T},
$$

but this is not a consequence of resultant nonvanishing.

The missing ingredient is a correlation estimate for distinct primes:

$$
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_T}
\#(E_{p_1}\cap\cdots\cap E_{p_T}).
$$

For $T=2$, the existing codegree machinery gives useful control. For $T\ge3$, the CRT lattice becomes the difficulty.

---

## 6. A possible true analogue of unlikely intersections

The closest statement one would want is a theorem of the form:

**Uniform moving-prime unlikely intersection conjecture.**  For distinct primes $p_i\in(X,2X]$,

$$
\#\{m<X^2:m\bmod p_i\in Z_{p_i}\ \forall i\}
$$

is smaller than the random CRT prediction by a power saving unless there is an algebraic obstruction.

Such a theorem would combine:

1. geometric transversality of the family $b_r$;
2. Deligne-type bounds for exponential sums over reductions;
3. a large sieve over varying finite fields.

This is the genuine analogue of BMZ in this setting.

---

## Conclusion

The answer is:

**Not directly.** The pairwise coprimality of the Apéry polynomials is a transversality statement, but classical unlikely-intersection machinery does not apply because the CRT constraints live over varying primes rather than inside one fixed algebraic group.

The correct bridge would be a new arithmetic unlikely-intersection theorem for moving reductions:

$$
(r_i,1)\in V(\mathbb F_{p_i}),\qquad r_i\equiv m\pmod {p_i}.
$$

If such a theorem existed, it would indeed force atom repulsion and could imply the desired $K(m)$ tail bounds. At present, the missing step is precisely a uniform cross-prime independence theorem; resultant transversality alone is insufficient.
