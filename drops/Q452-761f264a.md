# Q452: Polynomial Möbius cocycles over finite fields and the Apéry orbit

## Executive verdict

There is **no general theorem** saying that a bounded-degree polynomial sequence of Möbius transformations has an equidistributed deterministic prefix orbit, and the proposed conclusion

\[
\max_{y\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)}
\#\{r<p:v_r=y\}=O(1)
\]

is false under the stated hypotheses. It is even stronger than the natural random model: if one throws \(p\) independent uniform points into \(p+1\) states, the largest occupation number is typically of order

\[
\frac{\log p}{\log\log p},
\]

not bounded by an absolute constant.

For the Apéry recurrence, the exact situation is:

1. after correcting an index shift, the orbit is a nonautonomous Riccati cocycle
   \[
   [b_r:b_{r-1}]=M(r-1)\cdots M(1)[5:1];
   \]
2. a zero is a visit to the fixed target \([0:1]\);
3. the product is **not** a bounded-degree rational function of the clock \(r\);
4. as a function of the initial projective coordinate, every prefix remains Möbius, so the proposed numerator \(P_r(x)\) has degree **one**, not degree growing linearly with \(r\);
5. group generation or an expander theorem for a random walk does not imply equidistribution of this one deterministic ordered word;
6. the correct algebraic return certificates are the repository's gap/continuant polynomials
   \[
   N_h(r),\qquad \deg N_h=3(h-1),
   \]
   not a high-degree polynomial \(P_r(x)\) in the initial value;
7. fixed-gap algebraic geometry gives useful bounds, but the complexity grows with the gap. It does not currently yield \(z_p=O(1)\).

Thus the transfer-matrix formulation is exact and useful, but it is presently a **certificate language**, not a black-box mixing theorem. The most realistic proof targets are factorial-moment/Poisson estimates for the zero process, or the two-characteristic horizontal dispersion statement already isolated elsewhere in P3.2.

## 1. Correct projective formulation

Put

\[
P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)
     =34n^3+51n^2+27n+5.
\]

The recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}.
\]

For \(n\ge1\), define

\[
M(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)/(n+1)^3&-n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
\binom{b_{n+1}}{b_n}=M(n)\binom{b_n}{b_{n-1}}.
\]

Since \((b_1,b_0)=(5,1)\), the correct prefix formula is

\[
\boxed{
\binom{b_r}{b_{r-1}}
=M(r-1)M(r-2)\cdots M(1)inom51
\qquad(1\le r<p).}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Thus, with

\[
\Phi_p(r)=M_p(r-1)\cdots M_p(1),
\qquad \Phi_p(1)=I,
\]

one has

\[
\boxed{
r\in Z_p
\iff \Phi_p(r)[5:1]=[0:1].}
\tag{1.2}
\]

This corrects two details in the question:

- starting from \([5:1]\), the first step is \(M(1)\), not \(M(0)\);
- it is the first coordinate that vanishes.

Indeed,

\[
M(0)=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\]

is singular and must not be inserted in front of the state \((b_1,b_0)=(5,1)\).

### 1.1 Cleared projective matrix

One may clear the denominator and use

\[
\widetilde M(T)=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(T)&-T^3\\
(T+1)^3&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det\widetilde M(T)=T^3(T+1)^3.
\tag{1.3}
\]

A terminology point matters here. The matrix in `(1.3)` is not an element of
\(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf F_p[T])\), because its determinant is not a unit of the polynomial ring. It defines an element of

\[
\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p(T))
\]

and its specialization is in \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) whenever
\(T\notin\{0,-1\}\). For the first Apéry block, all steps \(1\le n\le p-2\) are regular.

### 1.2 Riccati form

Whenever \(b_{n-1}\ne0\), let

\[
x_n=\frac{b_n}{b_{n-1}}\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
x_{n+1}
=\frac{P(n)}{(n+1)^3}
 -\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3x_n}.}
\tag{1.4}
\]

The zero event is \(x_r=0\). This is a deterministic, time-dependent Riccati equation.

## 2. Question 1: bounded polynomial degree does not imply equidistribution

### 2.1 An explicit degree-one counterexample

Let \(x_0\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\), and choose a fixed
\(C\in\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) with

\[
C(x_0)=\infty.
\]

Set

\[
A(T)=C^{-1}
\begin{pmatrix}1&T\\0&1\end{pmatrix}C.
\tag{2.1}
\]

The entries of \(A(T)\) have degree at most one, its determinant is one, and every specialization \(A(t)\) is invertible. Nevertheless, every \(A(t)\) fixes \(x_0\). Hence the nonautonomous orbit

\[
x_{r+1}=A(r)x_r
\]

is constant and visits one point \(p\) times.

So no statement of the form

> bounded degree of the coefficient matrices \(\Longrightarrow\) bounded multiplicity or equidistribution

can be true without substantial nondegeneracy assumptions.

One can also make examples trapped in a two-point set, in a common Borel orbit, or following a prescribed short cycle. Bounded polynomial degree is a complexity condition on the **increments**; it is not a mixing condition on their ordered prefixes.

### 2.2 “Generic” must be specified

There are several inequivalent meanings of generic:

- Zariski-generic coefficients over a parameter space;
- coefficients sampled randomly for each prime;
- a density-one set of integer coefficient tuples;
- a fixed integral cocycle reduced modulo almost all primes.

An average theorem for randomly sampled coefficient polynomials would not automatically say anything about the one fixed Apéry cocycle. To use a generic theorem, one would need an explicit arithmetic nonexceptionality result placing the Apéry coefficients in the good family uniformly in \(p\).

### 2.3 Even the random model does not predict a uniform \(O(1)\) maximum

Suppose \(X_1,\ldots,X_p\) are independent uniform points of a set of size \(p+1\). For a **fixed** target \(y\),

\[
\#\{r:X_r=y\}
\]

has approximately the \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\) law. Thus bounded occupation of one specified target is a reasonable tightness heuristic.

But after maximizing over all \(p+1\) targets, the classical balls-in-bins law gives

\[
\max_y\#\{r:X_r=y\}
\asymp \frac{\log p}{\log\log p}
\]

with high probability. Therefore the statement “every point is visited at most an absolute constant number of times” is not the correct benchmark for random-like behavior.

For the Apéry target specifically, the random heuristic predicts \(z_p\) of order one in distribution, not a proved absolute bound for every prime. The repository's Poisson model likewise predicts that the maximum \(z_p\) should grow very slowly rather than remain bounded forever.

### 2.4 Ordinary equidistribution is too weak for a singleton target

Assume one could prove a standard discrepancy estimate

\[
\#\{1\le r\le p-2:v_r\in S\}
=\frac{p|S|}{p+1}+O(\sqrt p)
\tag{2.2}
\]

for every fixed subset or sufficiently regular test function. Taking \(S=\{[0:1]\}\) gives only

\[
z_p=1+O(\sqrt p),
\]

not \(O(1)\). The target has density \(1/(p+1)\); this is a **shrinking-target problem at scale \(1/p\)**. Square-root equidistribution errors are larger than the expected main term.

This scale mismatch is one of the central reasons that standard Deligne, expander, or ergodic-theory estimates do not settle the Apéry zero count.

## 3. Question 2: the prefix is not polynomial in the clock

The expression

\[
\Phi_p(r)=M(r-1)\cdots M(1)
\]

is a path-ordered product. For each fixed integer \(r\), its entries are elements of \(\mathbf F_p\). As \(r\) varies, those entries form recurrence sequences. They are not obtained by evaluating one bounded-degree polynomial in a free variable \(r\).

One can always interpolate the finite table

\[
r\longmapsto \Phi_p(r),\qquad r\in\mathbf F_p,
\]

entrywise by polynomials of degree at most \(p-1\), but that is tautological. Every arbitrary function on \(\mathbf F_p\) has such an interpolant. Degree \(p-1\) permits essentially arbitrary zero patterns and yields no nontrivial multiplicity bound.

The repository's direct transfer audit makes the natural complexity precise: a fixed gap of length \(h\) is encoded by a product of \(h-1\) matrices, and eliminating the intermediate states produces a polynomial of exact degree \(3(h-1)\). Keeping local equation degree bounded requires \(O(h)\) auxiliary variables and equations. Thus the direct transfer complexity grows with the word length.

## 4. Question 5 correction: \(P_r(x)\) is linear, not degree \(r\)

Composition of Möbius transformations is again a Möbius transformation. Consequently, as a function of the **initial coordinate** \(x\), the numerator of \(\Phi_r(x)\) never acquires degree greater than one.

This can be written exactly using the canonical companion solution. Let \(u_n\) satisfy the same Apéry recurrence with

\[
u_0=0,\qquad u_1=1,
\]

and put

\[
v_n=b_n-5u_n.
\]

Then

\[
v_0=1,\qquad v_1=0,
\]

and the fundamental matrix is

\[
\boxed{
\Phi(r)=
\begin{pmatrix}
u_r&v_r\\u_{r-1}&v_{r-1}
\end{pmatrix}.}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Indeed, for an arbitrary initial state \((x,1)\) at times \((1,0)\), the corresponding solution is

\[
y_n(x)=xu_n+v_n.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_r(x)
=\frac{u_rx+v_r}{u_{r-1}x+v_{r-1}},
\qquad
P_r(x)=u_rx+v_r=b_r+(x-5)u_r.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

In particular,

\[
P_r(5)=b_r,
\]

but

\[
\deg_x P_r=1.
\]

The unit-Casoratian identity gives

\[
\boxed{
\det\Phi(r)
=u_rv_{r-1}-v_ru_{r-1}
=u_rb_{r-1}-b_ru_{r-1}
=\frac1{r^3}.}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Thus \(\Phi(r)\) is invertible modulo \(p\) for \(1\le r<p\).

### 4.1 Why this does not bound the number of zero times

A degree-one polynomial \(P_r(x)\) is attached to each **different time** \(r\). The event of interest is

\[
P_r(5)=0
\]

as \(r\) ranges. Root counting in the variable \(x\) says nothing about how many members of the family \(\{P_r\}\) vanish at the one fixed point \(x=5\).

The proposed “degree stabilizes at \(p-1\)” argument confuses two variables:

- \(x\), the initial projective coordinate, for which the degree is always one;
- \(r\), the discrete clock, for which no bounded-degree polynomial representation is known.

Beyond the first characteristic block, the relevant structure is the Apéry-Lucas law, not stabilization of a polynomial degree. For example, for \(0\le s<p\),

\[
b_{qp+s}\equiv b_qb_s\pmod p.
\]

The raw projective transfer also meets a singular characteristic boundary at the step \(n=p-1\), so one cannot simply continue an invertible \(p\)-periodic PGL cocycle through that point.

## 5. Question 3: random products mix; the deterministic Apéry word need not

### 5.1 What is true for random increments

If \(G_1,G_2,\ldots\) are independent uniform elements of
\(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\), then for every current state \(x\),

\[
G_nx
\]

is uniform on \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\). In fact the states after the first step are independent uniform points.

If the increments are sampled from a fixed small symmetric generating set, mixing is governed by the spectral gap of the corresponding averaging operator. Bourgain-Gamburd type expansion theorems give uniform spectral gaps for suitable non-elementary generating sets in \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\). This yields rapid mixing for the **random walk**.

The sharp 3-transitivity of \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) explains the uniform stationary measure, but transitivity by itself does not give a mixing rate. The spectral gap comes from averaging over random choices.

### 5.2 Why this does not apply to \(M(1),M(2),\ldots\)

The Apéry prefixes are

\[
g_r=M(r-1)\cdots M(1).
\]

There is no random choice at step \(r\). Even if the set of values

\[
\{M(t):t\in\mathbf F_p\setminus\{0,-1\}\}
\]

generates all of \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\), this only rules out an obvious subgroup confinement. It does not imply that the particular ordered prefixes \(g_r\) are equidistributed.

A Cayley-graph spectral gap controls powers of an averaged operator such as

\[
\mathcal A=\frac1{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}\pi(s).
\]

The deterministic word instead contains the unitary product

\[
\pi(M(r-1))\cdots\pi(M(1)),
\]

with no averaging and hence no automatic contraction.

### 5.3 Exact representation-theoretic formulation

Let

\[
X=\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p),
\qquad x_0=[5:1],
\qquad y_0=[0:1].
\]

Let \(\pi\) be the permutation representation of
\(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) on functions on \(X\), and define

\[
f_x=\delta_x-\frac1{p+1}\mathbf1.
\]

For

\[
N_L(y)=\#\{1\le r\le L:g_rx_0=y\},
\]

one has the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
N_L(y)-\frac{L}{p+1}
=\left\langle
\sum_{r=1}^{L}\pi(g_r)f_{x_0},f_y
\right\rangle.}
\tag{5.1}
\]

The mean-zero part of this permutation representation is the dimension-\(p\) Steinberg representation. Thus a genuine deterministic equidistribution theorem would need strong cancellation in

\[
\sum_{r\le L}\pi(M(r-1)\cdots M(1)).
\tag{5.2}
\]

For the singleton target and \(L\asymp p\), an \(O(1)\) visit bound asks for matrix-coefficient control on an absolute scale, not merely square-root cancellation. No general theorem of this strength is known for polynomially scheduled nonautonomous words.

### 5.4 A possible randomized variant

There is a legitimate theorem template if the clock values are randomized. For example, choose independent \(T_j\) uniformly from a set of regular parameters and use increments \(M(T_j)\). If the resulting support generates a non-elementary subgroup and has a uniform spectral gap, the projective walk mixes rapidly.

But replacing the ordered schedule

\[
1,2,3,\ldots,p-2
\]

by independent samples changes the arithmetic problem. The hard issue is precisely whether this deterministic schedule is pseudorandom enough.

## 6. Autonomizing the cocycle does not remove the difficulty

One can add the clock to the state and define the fixed rational skew product

\[
\mathcal T:
(t,[x:y])
\longmapsto
\left(t+1,
[P(t)x-t^3y:(t+1)^3x]
\right).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Then the Apéry sequence is one orbit of \(\mathcal T\) on

\[
\mathbf A^1\times\mathbf P^1.
\]

This is an exact autonomous reformulation, but it does not create mixing:

- the first coordinate is the deterministic translation \(t\mapsto t+1\);
- it traverses every residue in a fixed order;
- the map has singular fibers at \(t=0,-1\);
- the target is the thin divisor \(x=0\);
- the expected number of intersections in one clock cycle is only order one.

Most finite-field orbit theorems for polynomial/rational dynamics concern iterates of a fixed map whose degree growth, orbit length, or exponential sums can be controlled. Here the base translation makes the orbit length in the clock coordinate tautologically \(p\), while the difficult information is the fiber coordinate's intersection with a shrinking target. The standard autonomous results do not supply an \(O(1)\) intersection theorem for `(6.1)`.

## 7. The correct resultant object: gap polynomials

The recurrence gives a much more effective algebraic certificate when two zero times are compared.

Suppose

\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

The no-consecutive-zero lemma gives

\[
b_{r+1}\not\equiv0\pmod p
\]

for \(p>3\). Define

\[
N_1(X)=1,
\qquad
N_2(X)=P(X+1),
\]

and, for \(h\ge2\),

\[
\boxed{
N_{h+1}(X)
=P(X+h)N_h(X)-(X+h)^6N_{h-1}(X).}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then in the nonwrapping range \(r+h<p\), iterating the recurrence from the state \((b_{r+1},b_r)=(b_{r+1},0)\) gives

\[
\boxed{
b_{r+h}
=\frac{N_h(r)}{\displaystyle\prod_{j=2}^{h}(r+j)^3}\,b_{r+1}\pmod p.}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
b_r\equiv b_{r+h}\equiv0\pmod p
\Longrightarrow N_h(r)\equiv0\pmod p.}
\tag{7.3}
\]

The project proves the exact degree

\[
\boxed{\deg N_h=3(h-1).}
\tag{7.4}
\]

For each fixed \(h\), a nonzero \(N_h\bmod p\) has at most \(3(h-1)\) roots. This is a genuine algebraic restriction on repeated visits to the target.

### 7.1 Why fixed-gap root counting does not give \(O(1)\)

The degree in `(7.4)` grows with the gap. If the orbit has many zero visits, one can force some short gaps and optimize over a cutoff; this is the source of the repository's unconditional power bound for \(z_p\). But the direct argument cannot keep the algebraic complexity bounded when gaps range up to order \(p\).

The transfer-product audit in [`drops/Q7231-dbbed013.md`](https://github.com/xiangyazi24/Ramanujan_Challenge/blob/main/drops/Q7231-dbbed013.md) makes this obstruction explicit:

- the transfer word for gap \(h\) has \(O(h)\) local states;
- eliminating them gives degree \(3(h-1)\);
- its determinant has \(h-1\) moving singular parameters;
- a black-box Deligne bound has an error growing with \(h\), becoming trivial around the square-root range.

This is not a universal impossibility theorem: an unrelated toric or cohomological realization might compress the same arithmetic. It does show why the naive transfer word does not itself provide a bounded-conductor sheaf.

### 7.2 Triple returns and resultants

If

\[
b_r=b_{r+h}=b_{r+k}=0\pmod p,
\]

then

\[
N_h(r)=N_k(r)=0\pmod p.
\]

When \(N_h\) and \(N_k\) are coprime over \(\mathbf Q\), this forces

\[
p\mid\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_k).
\tag{7.5}
\]

This is the useful resultant mechanism. Height bounds for the resultants give a finite prime budget for simultaneous returns at fixed gaps. The repository's coprimality, root-strip, and resultant machinery exploits exactly this point.

The remaining obstruction is quantifier order: the actual gaps may grow with \(p\). Degrees, resultants, discriminants, and effective constants then move with the same prime being counted. Fixed-gap Chebotarev or root bounds cannot simply be made uniform up to \(h\asymp p\).

### 7.3 Resultant of the linear initial-value forms

The linear forms in `(4.2)` have resultant

\[
\operatorname{Res}_x(P_r,P_s)
=u_rv_s-u_sv_r
=u_rb_s-u_sb_r.
\tag{7.6}
\]

If \(P_r(5)=P_s(5)=0\), this resultant vanishes modulo \(p\). But `(7.6)` is an integer Casoratian-type quantity indexed by \((r,s)\), not a bounded-degree polynomial in the time variable. Its coefficients have large height. Conditioning on the first zero and transporting over the gap is precisely what converts this raw determinant into the controlled polynomial \(N_{s-r}(r)\).

So the gap-polynomial construction is the refined version of the proposed resultant idea.

## 8. What the cited literature does and does not provide

### 8.1 Nori

The relevant author is **Madhav Nori**, not Meinolf Nori. Nori's work on subgroups of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbf F_p)\) is useful for identifying large generated subgroups and for strong-approximation/nonconfinement questions.

It can support a statement such as

\[
\langle M(t):t\in S\rangle
\text{ is large},
\]

but it does not imply that the one ordered word

\[
M(r-1)\cdots M(1)
\]

has equidistributed prefixes.

Reference: M. V. Nori, [“On subgroups of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbf F_p)\)”](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01388909), *Inventiones Mathematicae* 88 (1987).

### 8.2 Bourgain-Gamburd and expansion

Bourgain-Gamburd prove uniform expansion for suitable Cayley graphs of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\). This is directly relevant to random products from a fixed generating set, but not to a prescribed polynomial clock schedule.

Reference: J. Bourgain and A. Gamburd, [“Uniform expansion bounds for Cayley graphs of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\)”](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2008/167-2/p08), *Annals of Mathematics* 167 (2008).

### 8.3 Katz

Katz's monodromy and trace-function machinery is the natural language once one has constructed a fixed-rank, uniformly bounded-conductor sheaf whose trace in the **clock variable** records the relevant observable.

The missing step is exactly that construction. The direct transfer word has complexity growing with the word length, and no fixed-conductor sheaf in \(r\) whose trace is \(b_r\bmod p\) is currently banked. Katz's theorems cannot be invoked before this object is supplied.

Reference: N. Katz, *Exponential Sums and Differential Equations*, Annals of Mathematics Studies 124 (1990).

### 8.4 Silverman and autonomous arithmetic dynamics

Silverman's results concern periods/orbits of a fixed rational self-map after reduction modulo primes. Chang-D'Andrea-Ostafe-Shparlinski-Sombra likewise study orbit lengths for fixed or parametrized autonomous polynomial systems.

The Apéry clock changes the map at every step. Autonomization by `(6.1)` is possible, but the translation coordinate and shrinking-target event fall outside the conclusions of those orbit-length theorems.

References:

- J. Silverman, [“Variation of periods modulo \(p\) in arithmetic dynamics”](https://arxiv.org/abs/0707.1505).
- M.-C. Chang, C. D'Andrea, A. Ostafe, I. Shparlinski, and M. Sombra, [“On the functional graph of a polynomial map over finite fields” / orbit-length framework](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01976).

### 8.5 Shparlinski and collaborators

There are strong exponential-sum and orbit-distribution results for iterates of a **fixed** Möbius, polynomial, or rational map, including:

- E. El Abdalaoui and I. Shparlinski, [“Disjointness of the Möbius transformation and Möbius function”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11062);
- L. Mérai and I. Shparlinski, [Möbius-map orbits at prime times](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01089);
- A. Ostafe and I. Shparlinski, [“On the degree growth in some polynomial dynamical systems and nonlinear pseudorandom number generators”](https://arxiv.org/abs/0902.3884).

Their hypotheses exploit powers or iterates of one fixed map, controlled degree growth, or a fixed polynomial dynamical system. They do not furnish a rare-target theorem for a nonautonomous path-ordered product with coefficients \(M(r)\).

### 8.6 Einsiedler-Ward

The ergodic theory of algebraic \(\mathbf Z^d\)-actions studies mixing, entropy, and nonmixing sets for infinite algebraic actions. It does not presently provide the needed quantitative, uniform-in-\(p\), singleton-target bound for this finite deterministic cocycle.

Reference: M. Einsiedler and T. Ward, *Ergodic Theory with a View Towards Number Theory* (Springer, 2011).

## 9. A conditional algebraic route that would work

There is one simple theorem template worth isolating. Suppose one could construct rational functions

\[
R_p(T)\in\mathbf F_p(T)
\]

of degree at most an absolute constant \(D\), with

\[
R_p(r)=[b_r:b_{r-1}]
\]

for every regular \(r\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}\). Then every projective target would have at most \(D\) preimages, unless \(R_p\) were constant. In particular,

\[
z_p\le D.
\]

So bounded-degree interpolation of the **entire state as a function of the clock** would solve the local problem immediately.

But this is exactly what is missing. The natural transfer construction has complexity growing with the gap, and arbitrary finite-field interpolation has degree \(p-1\). A proof of a bounded-degree compression would be a major new theorem, not a consequence of the cubic coefficients.

The repository's toric/Kummer work is a warning not to declare such compression impossible: the direct coefficient formula can have large apparent complexity while a different cohomological realization has fixed rank. The realistic question is therefore:

> Is there an alternative fixed-complexity two-point or gap-kernel realization whose marked coordinate detects a return to \([0:1]\)?

No such realization is presently established.

## 10. What theorem should P3.2 actually target?

### 10.1 Per-prime Poisson/factorial moments

A random-like orbit should not be formulated as an absolute maximum-multiplicity theorem. A more natural local target is bounded factorial moments averaged over primes:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{P<p\le2P}(z_p)_k
\ll_k \frac{P}{\log P}
\qquad(k\text{ fixed}).}
\tag{10.1}
\]

An interval-resolved form on the lower half would be stronger and would lead to a Poisson point-process law for the reflected zero pairs. The gap polynomials are directly adapted to `(10.1)`: a \(k\)-tuple of zero positions produces \(k-1\) gap conditions.

### 10.2 A deterministic cocycle estimate

In pure orbit language, one would need a theorem controlling `(5.2)` in the Steinberg representation, averaged over the characteristic:

\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}
\left|
\left\langle
\sum_{r\in I_p}\pi_p(g_{p,r})f_{x_0},f_{y_0}
\right\rangle
\right|^2
\ll \text{the Poisson scale}.
\tag{10.2}
\]

This is a horizontal large-sieve theorem for deterministic polynomial cocycles. It is not a standard consequence of finite-group expansion.

### 10.3 The P3.2 cross-prime target is stronger than local equidistribution

Even \(z_p=O(1)\) for every prime would only control the marginal number of visits. The all-\(n\) P3.2 bottleneck involves correlations between different primes at moving residue positions.

For a top block, define schematically

\[
X_p(m)
=\mathbf1_{\{\Phi_p(m-p)[5:1]=[0:1]\}}.
\]

The quantifier-correct target already isolated in the project is a centered two-characteristic estimate of the shape

\[
\boxed{
\left|
\sum_{p\ne q}\sum_m X_p(m)X_q(m)
-\frac{T_N^2}{N}
\right|
\ll N^{o(1)}T_N.}
\tag{10.3}
\]

Per-prime orbit equidistribution does not imply `(10.3)`. A family of columns can each look uniform while sharing a scale-dependent common hit. This is the anchored-star obstruction recorded in the analytic oracle.

Thus, for P3.2, the transfer-matrix route must eventually become a **horizontal, cross-characteristic dispersion theorem**, not merely a local mixing theorem on each \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\).

## 11. Direct answers

### Answer to Question 1

No. Bounded polynomial degree of the increments does not imply orbit equidistribution or \(O(1)\) visit multiplicity. There are degree-one nonconstant cocycles with a common fixed point, and even an i.i.d. uniform orbit does not have an absolute \(O(1)\) maximum occupation over all targets.

A valid theorem needs additional hypotheses that control the **ordered prefix products**, not merely the generated subgroup or the degree of each step.

### Answer to Question 2

The product entries are not bounded-degree polynomials in the stopping time \(r\). They are recurrence sequences obtained from a path-ordered product. Finite-field interpolation by degree at most \(p-1\) is always possible but tautological and useless for a sharp zero bound.

As a function of the initial projective coordinate, the prefix remains Möbius and its numerator is linear, explicitly `(4.2)`.

### Answer to Question 3

There are rigorous rapid-mixing theorems for genuinely random products, under spectral-gap hypotheses. They do not apply to the deterministic schedule \(M(1),M(2),\ldots\). Full group generation is a nonconfinement check, not an equidistribution theorem for the prefixes.

No existing general result appears to give the required \(1/p\)-scale singleton-target estimate for a bounded-degree nonautonomous polynomial Möbius cocycle of this form.

### Answer to Question 4

The closest literature concerns:

- subgroup generation/strong approximation: Nori;
- random-walk expansion: Bourgain-Gamburd;
- fixed-conductor trace functions: Katz;
- autonomous rational dynamics: Silverman, Chang-D'Andrea-Ostafe-Shparlinski-Sombra;
- exponential sums along fixed-map orbits: Ostafe-Shparlinski and collaborators.

Each addresses one useful component, but none directly supplies the deterministic shrinking-target theorem needed here.

### Answer to Question 5

The proposed \(P_r(x)\) has degree one in \(x\), so degree growth in the initial value is not available. Root counting in \(x\) cannot bound how many different times satisfy \(P_r(5)=0\).

The useful resultant construction is instead:

\[
b_r=b_{r+h}=0
\Longrightarrow N_h(r)=0,
\qquad \deg N_h=3(h-1),
\]

and for triple returns,

\[
p\mid\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_k).
\]

This is the correct algebraic route. Its present limitation is the growth of complexity with the moving gaps.

## 12. Recommended next step

The project should treat transfer matrices as an exact bridge to one of two stronger objects:

1. **Gap-factorial-moment route:** combine pairwise coprimality, root-strip control, and resultant budgets to prove averaged bounds for \((z_p)_k\) or an interval-resolved Poisson law.
2. **Cocycle-horizontal-sieve route:** construct a bounded-complexity marked two-point cohomological object and prove a horizontal large sieve for its same-characteristic Hasse zeros.

Testing whether small sets of Apéry matrices generate \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)\) remains useful as a diagnostic against subgroup confinement. It should not be mistaken for the load-bearing equidistribution theorem.

The closest currently rigorous route in the repository is therefore the gap-polynomial/resultant machinery, not a generic random-matrix-product theorem.
