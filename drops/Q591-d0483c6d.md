# Q591 — Universal zero bounds for Apéry solutions and projective orbit revisitation

## Summary

The computational observation about the gap Wronskian is extremely important, but the implication

\[
\text{every nonzero Apéry solution has }O(1)\text{ zeros mod }p
\]

is not a formal consequence of transfer-matrix nondegeneracy alone. It is a much stronger arithmetic statement about a non-autonomous projective dynamical system.

The right reformulation is:

\[
N(x)=\max_{c\in\mathbf F_p}\#\{n:R_n=c\},
\qquad R_n=f_n/g_n.
\]

Thus the problem is bounded fiber multiplicity of the Apéry projective orbit.

The gap Wronskian computation gives a strong local input: every adjacent pair of orbit points is distinct. It does not by itself control long returns.

---

## (A) Is every Apéry solution expected to have O(1) zeros?

Let

\[
X_n=(x_n,x_{n-1})^t
\]

and write the recurrence as

\[
X_{n+1}=M_nX_n,
\]

with

\[
M_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)}{(n+1)^3}&-\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For two independent solutions f,g, define

\[
R_n=f_n/g_n.
\]

Then

\[
x_n=\alpha f_n+\beta g_n=0
\]

iff

\[
R_n=-\beta/\alpha.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\max_xN(x)=\max_c\#R^{-1}(c).
\]

The statement needed for ZM₂ is exactly:

> The Apéry projective orbit has uniformly bounded fibers.

The computations suggest a constant around 4–5, but no general theorem currently gives this.

---

## (B) Why is the Apéry ratio much less colliding than a random sequence?

A random sequence of length p in \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\) has maximum occupancy roughly

\[
\frac{\log p}{\log\log p}.
\]

The Apéry orbit is constrained by the recurrence. The strongest known constraint is the discrete Wronskian.

For solutions f,g:

\[
f_ng_{n+1}-f_{n+1}g_n
=\frac{(-1)^n}{(n+1)^3}.
\]

Hence

\[
R_{n+1}-R_n\neq0.
\]

So the orbit is an algebraic walk on \(\mathbf P^1\), not independent sampling.

The extra hidden structure is likely:

1. the transfer matrices lie in a thin family inside \(GL_2\);
2. the recurrence has rigid differential-Galois group (expected \(SL_2\));
3. return equations are algebraic equations of controlled degree.

The third point is the promising one.

---

## (C) Can spectral gaps of individual M_n prove bounded returns?

Not directly.

The eigenvalues of a single \(M_n\) are almost irrelevant because the orbit is governed by products

\[
M_{n-1}\cdots M_0.
\]

Even if every matrix has distinct eigenvalues, products of varying matrices can return repeatedly.

A counterexample in general is easy: choose diagonal matrices alternately expanding and contracting. Every factor has a spectral gap, but the product orbit can be periodic.

The required statement is not a local spectral-gap theorem. It is a global non-autonomous monodromy theorem.

A useful replacement is:

For fixed r, zeros of the gap Wronskian satisfy

\[
W(r,s)=0.
\]

Since W(r,s) satisfies the Apéry recurrence in s, one can try to prove:

\[
\#\{s:W(r,s)=0\}\le C
\]

by a recurrence-theoretic argument.

This is much closer to the observed phenomenon.

---

## (D) Is there a GL_2(F_p) orbit theorem giving return bounds?

For arbitrary matrices:

\[
g_1,g_2,\ldots,g_N\in GL_2(\mathbf F_p),
\]

there is no nontrivial bound.

A sequence can be chosen so that

\[
g_k\cdots g_1x=x
\]

for every k.

Classical results on group actions, expansion, and product theorems (Helfgott, Bourgain-Gamburd, etc.) concern:

- random products;
- fixed generating sets;
- stationary distributions.

They do not apply automatically to the deterministic sequence

\[
M_0,M_1,\ldots,M_{p-2}.
\]

The needed theorem would be an Apéry-specific deterministic orbit theorem:

\[
\#\{n\le p:R_n=c\}\le C.
\]

---

## A more promising algebraic route: collision polynomials

A collision

\[
R_r=R_s
\]

is equivalent to

\[
f_rg_s-f_sg_r=0.
\]

Define

\[
W(r,s)=f_rg_s-f_sg_r.
\]

The computational discovery that W satisfies the same recurrence is significant.

For fixed r:

\[
W(r,r)=0,
\]

and

\[
W(r,r+1)=\frac{(-1)^r}{(r+1)^3}.
\]

Thus W is the unique solution with a nonzero first step.

The desired theorem becomes:

> A nonzero Apéry solution with initial zero and nonzero next value has at most C later zeros.

This is analogous to a zero-counting theorem for recurrence solutions.

Possible tools:

- p-adic differential equations;
- Cartier operator arguments;
- polynomial continuation of the recurrence;
- arithmetic dynamics on P¹.

---

## (E) Expected constant C

The data suggests:

\[
C\approx4\text{ or }5.
\]

There are several reasons this is plausible.

The recurrence is order two, so a collision imposes one projective condition. A generic algebraic orbit would have O(1) intersections with a fixed point before finite-field recurrence effects appear.

The observed maximum multiplicity being around 5 is consistent with a hidden degree bound.

However, one should not expect C=2. Reflection already creates paired zeros, and the Apéry zero set has the involution

\[
r\leftrightarrow p-1-r.
\]

Therefore even a minimal theorem must allow multiple hits.

A realistic target is:

\[
\boxed{N(x)\le 8\text{ for every nonzero Apéry solution mod }p}
\]

which would immediately imply

\[
|Z_p|=O(1)
\]

and hence ZM₂.

---

## Relation to P3.2

This route is stronger than HM₃/large-sieve approaches. If proved, it bypasses probabilistic independence entirely:

\[
|Z_p|\le C
\Longrightarrow
\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|\ll \pi(X).
\]

The gap-Wronskian computation identifies the exact algebraic object that must be controlled. The missing lemma is no longer "randomness of Apéry zeros" but:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Uniform zero bound for nontrivial solutions of the Apéry recurrence over }\mathbf F_p.
}
\]

The most promising proof direction is therefore not spectral gap, but a finite-field analogue of Sturm theory for the Apéry recurrence: a theorem that the projective ratio map associated to a rigid Picard-Fuchs equation has bounded fiber multiplicity.
