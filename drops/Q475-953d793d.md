# Q475: Palindromic symmetry and the mean of Apéry-like zero sets

## Executive verdict

The proposed “Sp\(_4\) versus SL\(_2\)” dichotomy is **not** the correct explanation of the data.

There are four main corrections.

1. **Odd \(|Z_p|\) does not imply that \(Z_p\) is non-palindromic.**  The involution
   \[
   r\longmapsto p-1-r
   \]
   has the fixed point \(m=(p-1)/2\). A palindromic zero set has odd cardinality precisely when the midpoint is a zero.

2. **All four sequences in the question satisfy a twisted reflection law.**  With the standard normalizations,
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   b_{p-1-r}&\equiv b_r,\\
   D_{p-1-r}&\equiv 64^{-r}D_r,\\
   a_{p-1-r}&\equiv (-1)^r a_r,\\
   f_{p-1-r}&\equiv (-8)^{-r}f_r
   \end{aligned}
   \qquad(\bmod p).
   \]
   Every multiplier is nonzero, so **all four zero sets are palindromic**.

3. **The Domb “only even” observation is false for the standard Domb numbers.**  Already
   \[
   Z_{11}^{\mathrm{Domb}}=\{5\},\qquad |Z_{11}^{\mathrm{Domb}}|=1.
   \]
   In addition, the repository’s existing helper `SUBMIT/3.2/scripts/q32_family_compare.py` has the wrong sign in the Domb recurrence and produces \(D_2=44\), rather than the true \(D_2=28\). The 667-prime Domb statistics must therefore be regenerated from a checked implementation.

4. **The means near \(1\) and \(1.5\) have a simple finite-field explanation.**  The noncentral reflection orbits contribute mean about \(1\). In the two rank-2 examples, the twist is a nonsquare, and the reflection law forces the midpoint to vanish for half of the primes. This adds \(1/2\), giving a predicted mean
   \[
   1+\frac12=\frac32.
   \]
   Thus the observed values \(0.91,1.01,1.41,1.47\) are much better explained by the **square class of the reflection twist** than by compact Haar trace densities.

This answer therefore supersedes the rank/Haar interpretation in Q473. The relevant universality classes, if a universality theorem exists, are indexed at least by the reflection/Kummer twist and by the finite-field zero mass—not merely by the rank or the abstract monodromy group.

## 2. General reflection theorem

The phenomenon is most transparent at the recurrence level.

### Theorem: twisted reflection for self-reciprocal three-term recurrences

Let \(u_0=1\), and suppose

\[
(n+1)^d u_{n+1}=P(n)u_n+\beta n^d u_{n-1},
\tag{2.1}
\]

where

\[
P(-n-1)=(-1)^dP(n).
\tag{2.2}
\]

Put

\[
c=-\beta.
\]

Let \(p\) be an odd prime with \(p\nmid c\). Then, for \(0\le r\le p-1\),

\[
\boxed{
 c^r u_{p-1-r}\equiv u_{p-1}u_r\pmod p.
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

If in addition \(u_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\), then

\[
\boxed{
 u_{p-1-r}\equiv c^{-r}u_r\pmod p.
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

In particular,

\[
u_r\equiv0\pmod p
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u_{p-1-r}\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{2.5}
\]

Thus the zero set is palindromic regardless of whether the **values** are literally equal.

### Proof

Define

\[
v_r=c^r u_{p-1-r}.
\]

Apply (2.1) at \(n=p-1-r\). Modulo \(p\), using (2.2), one gets

\[
r^d u_{p-r}
=P(r)u_{p-1-r}+\beta(r+1)^d u_{p-2-r}.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Since \(c=-\beta\), equation (2.6) implies

\[
(r+1)^d v_{r+1}=P(r)v_r+\beta r^d v_{r-1}.
\]

So \((v_r)\) satisfies the same recurrence as \((u_r)\).

At \(n=p-1\), equation (2.1) gives

\[
0\equiv P(-1)u_{p-1}+\beta(-1)^d u_{p-2}.
\]

By (2.2), \(P(-1)=(-1)^dP(0)\), and therefore

\[
c\,u_{p-2}=P(0)u_{p-1}=u_1u_{p-1}.
\]

Consequently

\[
v_0=u_{p-1}u_0,
\qquad
v_1=u_{p-1}u_1.
\]

Uniqueness of the forward recurrence modulo \(p\) gives

\[
v_r=u_{p-1}u_r,
\]

which is (2.3).

This proof is elementary and does not invoke a hypergeometric transformation, monodromy, or random matrices.

## 3. Applying the theorem to the four sequences

The four recurrences are as follows.

### \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry numbers

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}
=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}.
\]

Here

\[
d=3,\qquad \beta=-1,\qquad c=1.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.}
\tag{3.1}
\]

### Domb numbers

\[
(n+1)^3D_{n+1}
=2(2n+1)(5n^2+5n+2)D_n-64n^3D_{n-1}.
\]

Here

\[
d=3,\qquad \beta=-64,\qquad c=64.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{D_{p-1-r}\equiv64^{-r}D_r\pmod p.}
\tag{3.2}
\]

This is the precise Domb analogue of Apéry reflection. It is **twisted**, not literally equal in the standard normalization.

Since \(64=8^2\), define

\[
\widetilde D_r=8^{-r}D_r\pmod p.
\]

Fermat’s theorem then turns (3.2) into exact value palindromy:

\[
\boxed{\widetilde D_{p-1-r}=\widetilde D_r.}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Thus the difference between \(c=1\) and \(c=64\) is only a square gauge factor.

### \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry numbers

\[
(n+1)^2a_{n+1}
=(11n^2+11n+3)a_n+n^2a_{n-1}.
\]

Here

\[
d=2,\qquad \beta=1,\qquad c=-1.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{a_{p-1-r}\equiv(-1)^r a_r\pmod p.}
\tag{3.4}
\]

Literal equality fails in general, but the zero set is still palindromic.

### Franel numbers

\[
(n+1)^2f_{n+1}
=(7n^2+7n+2)f_n+8n^2f_{n-1}.
\]

Here

\[
d=2,\qquad \beta=8,\qquad c=-8.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{f_{p-1-r}\equiv(-8)^{-r}f_r\pmod p.}
\tag{3.5}
\]

Again, the zero set is palindromic.

### Why \(u_{p-1}\equiv1\) in all four cases

This was used in passing from (2.3) to (2.4).

* For both Apéry sequences, in the binomial sum at \(n=p-1\), every \(k\ge1\) term contains \(\binom{p-1+k}{k}\equiv0\pmod p\); only \(k=0\) survives.
* For Franel,
  \[
  f_{p-1}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(-1)^{3k}
  =\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(-1)^k=1.
  \]
* For Domb, let \(m=(p-1)/2\). For \(k<m\), the factor \(\binom{2p-2-2k}{p-1-k}\) is divisible by \(p\); for \(k>m\), \(\binom{2k}{k}\) is divisible by \(p\). Only \(k=m\) survives, and its value is
  \[
  \binom{p-1}{m}^4\equiv1\pmod p.
  \]

The complete comparison is therefore:

| sequence | \(c=-\beta\) | reflection | square class of \(c\) |
|---|---:|---|---|
| \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry | \(1\) | \(b_{p-1-r}=b_r\) | square |
| Domb | \(64\) | \(D_{p-1-r}=64^{-r}D_r\) | square |
| \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry | \(-1\) | \(a_{p-1-r}=(-1)^r a_r\) | nonsquare over \(\mathbb Q\) |
| Franel | \(-8\) | \(f_{p-1-r}=(-8)^{-r}f_r\) | nonsquare over \(\mathbb Q\) |

This table, not the claimed Sp\(_4\)/SL\(_2\) table, controls the observed parity and first moment.

## 4. The fixed point explains all odd cardinalities

Let

\[
m=\frac{p-1}{2}.
\]

The reflection involution has one fixed point, namely \(m\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{
|Z_p|\equiv \mathbf 1_{\{u_m\equiv0\pmod p\}}\pmod2.
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

So “odd \(|Z_p|\)” means **midpoint zero**, not “non-palindromic zero set.”

At the midpoint, (2.4) gives

\[
c^m u_m=u_m.
\]

By Euler’s criterion,

\[
c^m\equiv\left(\frac cp\right)\pmod p.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\left(\frac cp\right)=-1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u_{(p-1)/2}\equiv0\pmod p.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This gives exact residue-class predictions.

### \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry

Here \(c=1\), so the midpoint is never forced by reflection.

### Domb

Here \(c=64=8^2\), so the midpoint is never forced by reflection. It can nevertheless vanish for additional arithmetic reasons, as it does at \(p=11\).

### \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry

Here \(c=-1\). Therefore

\[
p\equiv3\pmod4
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a_{(p-1)/2}\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Thus every such prime has odd \(|Z_p|\), while the zero set remains perfectly palindromic.

### Franel

Here \(c=-8\), whose square class is \(-2\). Therefore

\[
p\equiv5,7\pmod8
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_{(p-1)/2}\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Again, half of the prime residue classes have a forced central zero.

These two forced-midpoint laws already explain why odd cardinalities occur frequently for the two rank-2 examples.

## 5. Answer to (A): Domb has a reflection formula, but it is not forced by Sp\(_4\)

The exact Domb relation is

\[
D_{p-1-r}\equiv64^{-r}D_r\pmod p.
\]

It can be obtained directly from the Domb recurrence by the general theorem above. In a hypergeometric presentation it is the same self-reciprocity that appears when the terminating \({}_4F_3\) solution is continued from \(n\) to \(-n-1\); the factor \(64^n\) is the unavoidable scale in that transformation.

It is **not** a consequence of the abstract group \(\mathrm{Sp}_4\) alone.

There are two distinct objects that must not be conflated:

1. The ordinary generating function of the \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry numbers is annihilated by the third-order operator
   \[
   \theta^3-z(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
   +z^2(\theta+1)^3.
   \]
   The Domb generating function likewise has a third-order operator
   \[
   \theta^3-2z(2\theta+1)(5\theta^2+5\theta+2)
   +64z^2(\theta+1)^3.
   \]
   These order-three equations are of symmetric-square/Clausen type.

2. The repository’s Kummer/Mellin construction for Apéry, in
   `problems/3.2/lemmaBprime_result.tex`, produces a generic rank-four balanced hypergeometric object
   \[
   \operatorname{Hyp}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon;
   A,A,A^{-1},A^{-1}),
   \]
   with a symplectic duality structure. At the balanced point \(t=1\), the middle-extension stalk loses one nearby-cycle direction and the effective factor becomes rank three.

Thus a rank-four symplectic sheaf can legitimately occur in the project, but it is not the same statement as saying that the coefficient generating function itself is a rank-four Picard–Fuchs system.

More importantly, the reflection \(r\mapsto p-1-r\) occurs because the Kummer character \(A=\omega^r\) is sent to its inverse \(A^{-1}=\omega^{p-1-r}\). The geometric involution supplies an identification between those two fibers. The alternating pairing is then a consequence of duality. **The involution/duality datum is stronger than the bare monodromy-group label.**

Rank two does not oppose this mechanism: \(\mathrm{Sp}_2\cong\mathrm{SL}_2\). A rank-two SL\(_2\) local system is already symplectic. Therefore “symplectic in rank four, nonsymplectic in rank two” cannot be the explanation.

## 6. Answer to (B): exact equality fails for \(\zeta(2)\), but zero-set palindromy holds

For \(a_n=\sum_k\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}\), the correct law is

\[
a_{p-1-r}\equiv(-1)^r a_r\pmod p.
\]

So \(a_{p-1-r}\equiv a_r\) is false in general, but

\[
a_{p-1-r}\equiv0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a_r\equiv0.
\]

Here are exact small-prime checks.

| \(p\) | \((a_0,\ldots,a_{p-1})\bmod p\) | \(Z_p\) |
|---:|---|---|
| 5 | \((1,3,4,2,1)\) | \(\varnothing\) |
| 7 | \((1,3,5,0,5,4,1)\) | \(\{3\}\) |
| 11 | \((1,3,8,4,8,0,8,7,8,8,1)\) | \(\{5\}\) |
| 13 | \((1,3,6,4,3,8,10,5,3,9,6,10,1)\) | \(\varnothing\) |

For example, at \(p=5\),

\[
a_3=2\not\equiv3=a_1,
\]

but

\[
a_3\equiv-a_1\pmod5,
\]

exactly as predicted.

At \(p=7\) and \(p=11\), the singleton zero is the midpoint. Those are not counterexamples to palindromy; a singleton at the fixed point is a palindromic set.

## 7. The Domb counterexample at \(p=11\)

The standard Domb values begin

\[
1,4,28,256,2716,31504,\ldots
\]

Modulo \(11\), the first half is

\[
D_0,\ldots,D_5\equiv1,4,6,3,10,0.
\]

Using

\[
D_{10-r}\equiv64^{-r}D_r\pmod{11}
\]

gives the full row

\[
(D_0,\ldots,D_{10})
\equiv(1,4,6,3,10,0,2,1,7,9,1)\pmod{11}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{Z_{11}^{\mathrm{Domb}}=\{5\}.}
\]

This has three consequences.

* The standard Domb zero set is palindromic.
* Its cardinality need not be even.
* “Only even” cannot be used as evidence for a special Sp\(_4\) mechanism.

The midpoint zero at \(p=11\) is **not forced** by the Legendre-symbol mechanism because \(64\) is a square. It is an additional arithmetic zero specific to the Domb sequence.

## 8. Answer to (C): the toric involution gives inverse-character duality, possibly with a rank-one twist

The repository already contains the correct geometric mechanism for the \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry coordinate.

In `problems/3.2/lemmaBprime_result.tex`, after the coordinate change,

\[
\Lambda=
\frac{uv(w-1)(1-uvw)}{w(u-1)(v-1)},
\]

and

\[
\tau(u,v,w)=\bigl(w,(uvw)^{-1},u\bigr),
\qquad
\Lambda\circ\tau=\Lambda^{-1}.
\]

If \(A=\omega^r\), then

\[
\tau^*\mathcal L_A\simeq\mathcal L_{A^{-1}}.
\]

Since

\[
A^{-1}=\omega^{-r}=\omega^{p-1-r},
\]

this is exactly the geometric origin of the reflection index.

On a locus on which \(\Lambda\) is nonzero and \(\tau\) is a bijection, the elementary calculation is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_x\Lambda(x)^{p-1-r}
&=\sum_x\Lambda(x)^{-r}\\
&=\sum_x\Lambda(\tau x)^r\\
&=\sum_x\Lambda(x)^r.
\end{aligned}
\]

That explains \(c=1\) in the Apéry case.

There are, however, two cautions.

1. A Laurent polynomial may vanish, and a rational involution may have deleted divisors. The equality \(\Lambda\circ\tau=\Lambda^{-1}\) is an identity on an open locus, not automatically a term-by-term identity on the entire raw torus.
2. The endpoint \(r=0\) needs a \(0^0\) correction. The project’s `lemmaA_result.tex` explicitly records this: the Kummer open-set sum at the trivial character differs from the prescribed raw zeroth moment by boundary terms.

This is why the sheaf/Jacobi or recurrence proof is safer than a formal manipulation that silently divides by zero.

### General twisted toric form

For another sequence, the natural relation can be

\[
\Lambda(\tau x)=\frac{c}{\Lambda(x)}.
\]

Then the same calculation gives

\[
S(A^{-1})=A(c)^{-1}S(A).
\]

After reduction, \(A(c)=c^r\), producing

\[
u_{p-1-r}=c^{-r}u_r.
\]

Thus the natural factor is not a constant \(\varepsilon=\pm1\). It is a **rank-one Kummer character in \(r\)**.

The four examples have

\[
c=1,64,-1,-8.
\]

A rescaling

\[
u_r\longmapsto t^{-r}u_r
\]

changes \(c\) by the square \(t^2\). Therefore the invariant datum is the square class of \(c\), not its particular numerical representative.

This also clarifies the logical direction:

```text
toric/recurrence self-reciprocity
        ↓
A ↔ A^{-1}, possibly with a rank-one Kummer twist
        ↓
Poincaré duality / self-dual pairing
        ↓
orthogonal or symplectic monodromy constraints
```

The abstract monodromy group by itself does not reconstruct the first line.

## 9. Answer to (D): rank-two examples do have palindromic symmetry

Yes. In fact the two rank-two sequences named in the question already have palindromic **zero sets**:

\[
Z_p(a)=p-1-Z_p(a),
\qquad
Z_p(f)=p-1-Z_p(f).
\]

Their odd cardinalities come from the fixed point.

There are three different notions that should be separated.

1. **Zero-set palindromy:**
   \[
   u_r=0\Longleftrightarrow u_{p-1-r}=0.
   \]
   All four examples have it.

2. **Twisted value palindromy:**
   \[
   u_{p-1-r}=c^{-r}u_r.
   \]
   All four examples have it.

3. **Untwisted value palindromy:**
   \[
   u_{p-1-r}=u_r.
   \]
   This holds in the given normalization for \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry. It holds for Domb after the square rescaling \(D_r\mapsto8^{-r}D_r\). For a nonsquare twist there is no global rational rescaling that removes it.

Consequently this is not a rank phenomenon. In the general Zagier quadratic recurrence

\[
(n+1)^2u_{n+1}
=(An^2+An+\lambda)u_n-Bn^2u_{n-1},
\]

the reflection constant is \(c=B\). Rank-two sequences with square \(B\) have untwisted palindromy after a square gauge; those with nonsquare \(B\) have the same palindromic zero sets but a nontrivial quadratic obstruction at the midpoint.

## 10. Answer to (E): the correct first-moment model is finite-field orbit counting, not compact Haar density

The densities

\[
f_{SU(2)}(0)=\frac1\pi,
\qquad
f_{USp(4)}(0)=\frac2\pi
\]

refer to the density of a **real normalized trace** near zero under compact Haar measure. The event in this project is different:

\[
u_r\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

This is an exact finite-field congruence. For a genuinely varying finite-field trace, the generic local heuristic is

\[
\Pr(u_r=0)\sim\frac{\kappa}{p},
\]

where \(\kappa\) is the finite-field zero-mass constant. Under the simplest uniform model, \(\kappa=1\). The real density \(f_K(0)\) does not enter.

Equivalently, for an algebraic monodromy group \(G\), the relevant quantity is of the form

\[
\frac{\#\{g\in G(\mathbb F_p):\operatorname{tr}(g)=0\}}
{\#G(\mathbb F_p)},
\]

which is generically \(\asymp1/p\) because trace zero is a codimension-one condition. It is not obtained by taking the value at zero of the real compact trace density.

### Reflection-orbit computation

The index set \(\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\) has

* one fixed point \(m=(p-1)/2\);
* one deterministic nonzero pair \(\{0,p-1\}\), because \(u_0=u_{p-1}=1\);
* \((p-3)/2\) remaining two-element orbits.

Assume each noncentral orbit representative is zero with probability \(1/p\). A zero orbit contributes two indices. Therefore

\[
\mathbb E\bigl[|Z_p|\text{ away from }m\bigr]
\approx
2\cdot\frac{p-3}{2}\cdot\frac1p
=rac{p-3}{p}
\longrightarrow1.
\tag{10.1}
\]

Pairing does not change the mean: it halves the number of independent trials and doubles the contribution of each success.

### Midpoint contribution

The midpoint contributes deterministically whenever \((c/p)=-1\). Hence, under the same generic model,

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E|Z_p|\longrightarrow1+\delta(c),
}
\tag{10.2}
\]

where

\[
\delta(c)=\operatorname{density}\left\{p:\left(\frac cp\right)=-1\right\}.
\]

For a fixed nonzero rational \(c\), ignoring finitely many bad primes,

\[
\delta(c)=
\begin{cases}
0,&c\text{ is a square in }\mathbb Q,\\[2mm]
\frac12,&c\text{ is a nonsquare in }\mathbb Q.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus the four predictions are

| sequence | \(c\) | predicted generic mean |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry | \(1\) | \(1\) |
| Domb | \(64\) | \(1\) |
| \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry | \(-1\) | \(3/2\) |
| Franel | \(-8\) | \(3/2\) |

This matches the displayed data strikingly well:

\[
0.91,\quad1.01,\quad1.41,\quad1.47.
\]

Indeed, after subtracting the forced midpoint density \(\approx1/2\), the rank-two means become

\[
1.41-0.5\approx0.91,
\qquad
1.47-0.5\approx0.97,
\]

which line up with the two alleged “rank-four” base means. This is much stronger evidence for the twist model than for a Haar-density model.

### Distributional prediction

If noncentral zero orbits are approximately independent, then their number is approximately Poisson with mean \(1/2\). Therefore:

* for square \(c\), apart from additional midpoint zeros,
  \[
  |Z_p|\approx2\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2);
  \]
* for nonsquare \(c\), over varying primes,
  \[
  |Z_p|\approx
  \mathbf1_{\{(c/p)=-1\}}
  +2\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).
  \]

The first formula is exactly the empirical law previously observed for the \(\zeta(3)\) Apéry sequence. The second predicts a roughly 50–50 mixture of even and odd translates for \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry and Franel.

There can also be **accidental midpoint zeros** when \((c/p)=+1\). Domb at \(p=11\) is an example. Their density is a separate arithmetic question and must be measured rather than assumed to be zero.

## 11. Answer to (F): no universality by rank or monodromy group alone

The evidence supports a more refined conjectural template, not

```text
Sp4  -> mean 1
SL2  -> mean 1.4 or 1.5.
```

A plausible generic formula is

\[
\boxed{
\lim_{X\to\infty}
\frac1{\pi(X)}\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|
=
\kappa_{\mathrm{pair}}
+\delta(c)
+\delta_{\mathrm{acc}},
}
\tag{11.1}
\]

where

* \(\kappa_{\mathrm{pair}}\) is the finite-field zero-mass constant on noncentral reflection orbits; generically one expects \(\kappa_{\mathrm{pair}}=1\);
* \(\delta(c)\) is the forced-midpoint density from the square class of the reflection twist;
* \(\delta_{\mathrm{acc}}\) is the density of additional midpoint zeros in residue classes where reflection does not force them.

Big geometric monodromy may help prove equidistribution and force \(\kappa_{\mathrm{pair}}=1\) in favorable families. But rank or monodromy alone does not determine all three terms.

The constant can change because of:

* a different square class of \(c\);
* extra deterministic zeros or exceptional fibers;
* reducibility, CM, or finite monodromy;
* multiple geometric components of the trace-zero divisor;
* a nonuniform finite-field trace distribution;
* extra automorphisms that identify more than reflection pairs;
* positive-density accidental midpoint zeros.

Therefore the strongest defensible statement is:

> Under a generic finite-field equidistribution hypothesis, self-reciprocal Apéry-like recurrences with square reflection twist should have mean \(1\), while those with nonsquare twist should have mean \(3/2\). This is a universality by **reflection square class**, not by rank.

The four current examples fit this statement, but they do not establish it universally.

## 12. Recommended recomputation and decisive tests

### 12.1 Fix and validate Domb first

Use both the binomial definition and the recurrence, and require agreement. At minimum:

```python
DOMB_PREFIX = [1, 4, 28, 256, 2716, 31504]
assert generated_domb[:6] == DOMB_PREFIX
```

Then check the exact reflection identity:

```python
for p in primes:
    values = domb_mod_p(p)
    for r in range(p):
        assert (pow(64, r, p) * values[p - 1 - r] - values[r]) % p == 0
```

At \(p=11\), require

```python
assert zero_set_domb(11) == {5}
```

### 12.2 Test the four exact twists, not just parity

```text
Apery zeta(3):  c = 1
Domb:           c = 64
Apery zeta(2):  c = -1
Franel:         c = -8
```

For every tested \((p,r)\), verify

\[
c^r u_{p-1-r}\equiv u_r\pmod p.
\]

This is a much stronger regression test than checking whether \(|Z_p|\) is even.

### 12.3 Split the statistic into midpoint and paired parts

Record

\[
M_p=\mathbf1_{\{u_{(p-1)/2}=0\}},
\qquad
P_p=\frac{|Z_p|-M_p}{2}.
\]

Then \(P_p\) is the number of noncentral zero pairs. The corrected model predicts

\[
\mathbb E P_p\to\frac12
\]

for all four examples, while

\[
\mathbb E M_p\to
\begin{cases}
0,&c\text{ square, barring accidental zeros},\\
1/2,&c\text{ nonsquare, plus any accidental zeros}.
\end{cases}
\]

This decomposition should make the alleged rank effect disappear.

### 12.4 Stratify by the quadratic character

For \(\zeta(2)\), compare primes \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) and \(p\equiv3\pmod4\).

For Franel, compare \(p\equiv1,3\pmod8\) against \(p\equiv5,7\pmod8\).

The exact predictions are:

```text
Apery zeta(2): midpoint forced for p = 3 mod 4.
Franel:        midpoint forced for p = 5 or 7 mod 8.
```

### 12.5 Break the rank/twist correlation

To test universality, add examples selected by reflection constant rather than by rank:

* a rank-two Zagier recurrence with square \(B\);
* a cubic recurrence with nonsquare \(c\), if available;
* sequences with finite or CM monodromy as controls.

If the present explanation is correct, the means will follow the square class of \(c\), not the order of the differential equation.

## 13. Direct answers, condensed

### (A)

Domb satisfies

\[
D_{p-1-r}\equiv64^{-r}D_r\pmod p.
\]

After replacing \(D_r\) by \(8^{-r}D_r\), it becomes literally palindromic. The mechanism is recurrence/Kummer self-reciprocity. It is compatible with symplectic duality but is not forced by the abstract group \(\mathrm{Sp}_4\).

### (B)

For \(\zeta(2)\) Apéry,

\[
a_{p-1-r}\not\equiv a_r
\]

in general; instead

\[
a_{p-1-r}\equiv(-1)^r a_r.
\]

Small-prime computations above verify this. The zero set remains palindromic, and the midpoint is forced to vanish for \(p\equiv3\pmod4\).

### (C)

The toric involution sends \(A\) to \(A^{-1}\), hence \(r\) to \(p-1-r\). In general it may introduce a rank-one Kummer factor \(A(c)^{-1}=c^{-r}\). The natural multiplier is therefore not a constant \(\varepsilon=\pm1\), but an \(r\)-dependent character. The square class of \(c\) is the invariant datum.

### (D)

Yes: the two displayed rank-two sequences themselves have palindromic zero sets. Odd cardinality records a central fixed-point zero. This is not a rank phenomenon.

### (E)

Compact Haar trace densities are the wrong statistic for exact mod-\(p\) zeros. The finite-field orbit model predicts a base mean \(1\), plus a forced midpoint contribution of \(1/2\) when the reflection twist is nonsquare. Hence the predictions \(1,1,3/2,3/2\), matching the data.

### (F)

There is no reason for all Sp\(_4\) sequences to have mean exactly \(1\), or all SL\(_2\) sequences to have one common mean. A plausible generic universality class is determined by finite-field zero mass, reflection orbit structure, and the square class of the Kummer twist. Sequence-specific midpoint arithmetic can still modify the constant.
