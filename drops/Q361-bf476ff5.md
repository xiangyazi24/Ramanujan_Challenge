# Direct sieve bounds for prime factors of Apéry numbers

## Executive verdict

There is **no generic Selberg-sieve, Turán–Kubilius, or D-finite theorem** that can give the desired pointwise bound for Apéry numbers merely from the recurrence or the binomial-sum representation. The class of polynomial-coefficient recurrences is far too broad: the central binomial coefficients are P-recursive, have the same exponential-size scale as the Apéry numbers, and already possess about \(n/\log n\) forced distinct prime divisors.

The closest sequence-specific result I found is Luca–Shparlinski, *Arithmetic properties of Apéry numbers* (2008). It proves, for a density-one set of indices, lower bounds on \(\omega(b_n)\) and on the largest prime factor. It does **not** prove an upper bound or an Erdős–Kac theorem.

The seven proposed routes separate as follows.

| Route | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Generic sieve for P-recursive sequences | Impossible at the desired strength without extra arithmetic hypotheses |
| Classical Turán–Kubilius | Not directly applicable to \(n\mapsto\omega(b_n)\) |
| Factorization of the binomial summands | Does not control prime factors of their sum |
| Iterating the recurrence modulo \(p\) | Controls repeated hits of one prime, not many singleton primes dividing one \(b_n\) |
| Erdős–Kac analogy | Plausible heuristic; no known theorem for Apéry values |
| ABC applied to the recurrence | Does not isolate \(\operatorname{rad}(b_n)\); \(\omega(b_n)=o(n)\) is already unconditional |
| Primitive divisor theorem | No known Apéry analogue; in any case it gives lower, not upper, information |

For the current Problem 3.2 architecture, the useful distinction is:

- recurrence/gap-polynomial machinery controls a prime that hits **two or more indices**;
- \(\omega(b_n)\) at a fixed \(n\) is dominated by primes that may each hit only that one index.

That singleton-atom problem is exactly why the existing `atom_tail_section.tex` cannot be replaced by a standard sieve citation.

## 0. Baseline bounds and the exact target

Let

\[
 \omega_{>T}(m)=\#\{p>T:p\mid m\}.
\]

Since the product of these primes is at least \(T^{\omega_{>T}(m)}\), one always has

\[
 T^{\omega_{>T}(m)}\leq \operatorname{rad}(m)\leq |m|,
 \qquad
 \omega_{>T}(m)\leq rac{\log |m|}{\log T}.
 	ag{0.1}
\]

For the Apéry numbers,

\[
 b_n\sim c\,(17+12\sqrt2)^n n^{-3/2},
\]

so, with

\[
 \kappa=\log(17+12\sqrt2)=3.52549\ldots,
\]

we get

\[
 \omega_{>T}(b_n)\leq
 rac{\kappa n+O(\log n)}{\log T}.
 	ag{0.2}
\]

Thus, for \(n\leq N\) and \(T=\sqrt N\),

\[
 \omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_n)\leq
 (2\kappa+o(1))rac{N}{\log N}.
 	ag{0.3}
\]

For the lcm/GCD application, primes larger than \(N\) are irrelevant. The more precise quantity is therefore

\[
 \omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(b_n)
 =\#\{p:\sqrt N<p\leq N,\ p\mid b_n\}.
\]

It also satisfies the prime-supply bound

\[
 \omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(b_n)\leq
 \pi(N)-\pi(\sqrt N)\sim rac{N}{\log N}.
 	ag{0.4}
\]

The desired pointwise result must improve (0.4) to little-oh. Neither the size of \(b_n\) nor generic recurrence theory does that.

There is, however, a stronger unconditional statement for the **total** number of prime factors. If \(\omega(m)=k\), then \(m\) is at least the product of the first \(k\) primes, whose logarithm is \((1+o(1))k\log k\). Hence

\[
 \omega(m)\leq (1+o(1))rac{\log m}{\log\log m}.
\]

Consequently

\[
 oxed{\ \omega(b_n)\leq(\kappa+o(1))rac{n}{\log n}=o(n).\ }
 	ag{0.5}
\]

So Question 6's target \(\omega(b_n)=o(n)\) needs neither ABC nor any Apéry-specific input. The difficult scale is the much sharper empirical prediction \(\omega(b_n)\asymp\log n\), or the critical pointwise tail \(o(N/\log N)\).

### A caution about the computation

Trial division only up to \(10^6\) does not determine \(\omega(b_n)\) unless every residual cofactor was also primality-tested or fully factored. Otherwise the recorded value is a lower bound for the true \(\omega(b_n)\). The heuristic mean is nevertheless natural: a random integer of size \(b_n\) has expected prime-factor count

\[
 \log\log b_n=\log n+\log\kappa+o(1).
\]

Thus a coefficient near \(1\) in front of \(\log n\) is the random-integer prediction.

## 1. Is there a sieve upper bound for a polynomial-coefficient recurrence?

### No, not from P-recursiveness alone

A decisive counterexample is

\[
 a_n=inom{2n}{n}.
\]

It satisfies the first-order polynomial recurrence

\[
 (n+1)a_{n+1}=2(2n+1)a_n
\]

and has exponential size \(\log a_n\sim n\log4\). Yet every prime \(p\) with

\[
 n<p\leq2n
\]

divides \(inom{2n}{n}\). Therefore

\[
 \omega_{>\sqrt n}\!\left(inom{2n}{n}ight)\geq
 \pi(2n)-\pi(n)\simrac{n}{\log n}.
 	ag{1.1}
\]

So even among positive hypergeometric/P-recursive sequences of exponential height, the critical \(n/\log n\) scale can be attained. No theorem based only on “linear recurrence with polynomial coefficients” can imply \(o(n/\log n)\), much less \(O(\log n)\).

The even simpler sequence \(a_n=n!\), satisfying \(a_{n+1}=(n+1)a_n\), shows the same failure without the exponential-height normalization.

### What a sieve theorem would actually require

For a multiset

\[
 \mathcal A_N=\{a_1,\ldots,a_N\},
\]

one introduces

\[
 A_d(N)=\#\{n\leq N:d\mid a_n\}.
\]

A Granville–Soundararajan type sieve/Erdős–Kac framework needs information of the form

```text
A_d(N) = N g(d) + r_d
```

for squarefree \(d\), where:

1. \(g(d)\) is approximately multiplicative;
2. \(g(p)\) has the expected size, often \(ho(p)/p\);
3. the remainders \(r_d\) have a strong average bound over products of many primes;
4. higher correlations \(A_{p_1\cdots p_k}(N)\) remain controlled in a growing range.

P-recursiveness supplies none of these statements automatically. They are the arithmetic theorem one has to prove.

### The directly relevant Apéry paper

The closest result is:

> Florian Luca and Igor E. Shparlinski, “Arithmetic properties of Apéry numbers,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 78 (2008), 545–562, DOI `10.1112/jlms/jdn031`.

They prove that, for a set of indices \(n\) of asymptotic density \(1\),

\[
 \omega(b_n)>c_0\log\log\log n,
 \qquad
 P^+(b_n)>c_0(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2},
 	ag{1.2}
\]

where \(P^+(m)\) is the largest prime factor. Their method extends to certain second-order polynomial recurrences with Lucas-type congruences.

This is important confirmation that the recurrence plus Lucas property has genuine “anatomy” content. But the direction is opposite to Q361: (1.2) is a density-one **lower** bound, not a pointwise upper bound for \(\omega(b_n)\).

## 2. Can Turán–Kubilius be applied to \(f(n)=\omega(b_n)\)?

### Not in its classical form

The function \(\omega(m)\) is strongly additive in the variable \(m\):

\[
 \omega(uv)=\omega(u)+\omega(v)\quad	ext{when }(u,v)=1.
\]

But the composed function

\[
 n\longmapsto\omega(b_n)
\]

is not an additive arithmetic function of \(n\). There is no relation such as

\[
 \omega(b_{mn})=\omega(b_m)+\omega(b_n),
\]

and the Apéry sequence is not a divisibility sequence. Classical Turán–Kubilius averages an additive function over all integers \(m\leq x\); it does not automatically remain valid on the exponentially sparse subsequence \(m=b_n\).

### The correct custom analogue

Truncate the prime factors and write

\[
 S_y(n)=\omega_{\leq y}(b_n)
       =\sum_{p\leq y}X_p(n),
 \qquad
 X_p(n)=\mathbf1_{p\mid b_n}.
\]

Then the first two moments are exactly

\[
 \sum_{n\leq N}S_y(n)=\sum_{p\leq y}A_p(N),
 	ag{2.1}
\]

and

\[
 \sum_{n\leq N}S_y(n)^2
 =\sum_{p\leq y}A_p(N)
  +2\sum_{p<q\leq y}A_{pq}(N).
 	ag{2.2}
\]

Thus an Apéry-specific Turán–Kubilius inequality would follow from estimates for

\[
 A_p(N)=\#\{n\leq N:p\mid b_n\},
 \qquad
 A_{pq}(N)=\#\{n\leq N:pq\mid b_n\}.
\]

Higher moments require \(A_{p_1\cdots p_k}(N)\).

This is precisely a divisibility-correlation problem. It is not a formal consequence of additivity. The existing project machinery—zero sets \(Z_p\), CRT moments, HM\(_2\), and the atom-tail analysis—is best viewed as a custom Turán–Kubilius/large-sieve program for these indicators.

Even a sharp variance theorem would normally give an average or density-one statement. It would not by itself prove a pointwise bound for **every** \(n\), which is the all-\(n\) bottleneck of Problem 3.2.

## 3. Can the binomial-sum factorization bound \(\omega(b_n)\)?

### Termwise factorization does not survive addition

Each summand

\[
 T_{n,k}=inom nk^2inom{n+k}k^2
\]

has prime factors at most \(n+k\leq2n\). Nevertheless their sum can acquire entirely new and much larger primes. Already

\[
 b_2=1+36+36=73.
\]

Thus every summand has prime factors at most \(4\), while the sum is the prime \(73\).

There is no useful general inequality bounding

\[
 \omega\!\left(\sum_k T_{n,k}ight)
\]

in terms of the prime factorizations of the \(T_{n,k}\). Congruential cancellation is the whole issue.

Granville-style anatomy results are effective when divisibility is local and multiplicative—for example products of linear factors, polynomial values, factorial ratios, or families for which one can count roots modulo squarefree moduli. They do not turn the factorization of summands into a factorization of the sum.

### What the sum formula does give

The useful exploitation of the binomial sum is instead to convert it into a diagonal/constant-term representation. Modulo \(p\), this gives a Cartier coefficient description of \(b_r\). The project already uses this in `problems/3.2/atom_tail_section.tex` to study

\[
 Z_p=\{0\leq r<p:p\mid b_r\}.
\]

That translation can support root counts, reflection, and correlations among indices. It is much stronger than termwise factorization, but it still does not directly bound the number of distinct primes dividing one fixed integer \(b_n\).

### Central binomial and Catalan numbers are warnings, not analogies

The central binomial coefficient satisfies (1.1). Likewise, for the Catalan number

\[
 C_n=rac1{n+1}inom{2n}{n},
\]

every prime \(n+1<p\leq2n\) divides \(C_n\), so

\[
 \omega(C_n)\geq\pi(2n)-\pi(n+1)\simrac{n}{\log n}.
\]

A binomial formula can therefore force **many** prime factors rather than random-integer behavior.

## 4. Can the recurrence constrain how many primes divide one \(b_n\)?

The recurrence is

\[
 (n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1},
 \qquad
 P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

For a prime \(p
mid n(n+1)\), it gives an invertible two-dimensional transfer matrix modulo \(p\). If \(p\mid b_n\), then

\[
 (n+1)^3b_{n+1}\equiv -n^3b_{n-1}\pmod p.
 	ag{4.1}
\]

Iterating (4.1) backward to the initial state does not create a second independent condition on \(p\). It simply reconstructs the original statement \(b_n\equiv0\pmod p\) in transfer-matrix coordinates.

### Where iteration is genuinely powerful

It becomes powerful when the **same prime** divides two terms. If

\[
 p\mid b_r,\qquad p\mid b_{r+h},
\]

then the intervening transfer block yields the gap-polynomial condition

\[
 N_h(r)\equiv0\pmod p,
 \qquad \deg N_h=3(h-1).
 	ag{4.2}
\]

This gives:

- no consecutive zeros for \(p>3\);
- root-count bounds for repeated hits;
- codegree and collision estimates;
- resultant constraints for three or more returns.

These are exactly the mechanisms developed in the repository.

### Why this does not answer Q361

Suppose distinct primes \(p_1,\ldots,p_K\) all divide the same \(b_n\). Each prime may occur nowhere else in the relevant index range. Then (4.2) never activates. The only common integer known to be divisible by all \(p_i\) is \(b_n\) itself, and its logarithmic height is \(\Theta(n)\). This recovers only (0.2).

A recurrence-only proof of a stronger pointwise bound would need a new compression statement of the following kind:

```text
Construct a nonzero integer C_n such that
  every relevant prime p | b_n also divides C_n,
but
  log |C_n| = o(n).
```

The recurrence presently supplies no such carrier. Its small-height certificates arise only after two or more zero conditions for the same prime are imposed.

At primes \(p\leq n\), singular recurrence indices require Lucas/block identities rather than a single invertible transfer product. This changes the encoding, but it does not create cross-prime coupling for singleton divisors.

## 5. Erdős–Kac theorems for structured families

### Yes, but only when strong local equidistribution is available

There are many Erdős–Kac theorems on structured sparse families. Examples include:

- shifted primes, such as the distribution of \(\omega(p-1)\);
- entries of integral matrices in expanding families in \(\mathrm{SL}_d(\mathbb Z)\);
- integers with dense divisors, including practical-number-type families;
- numerous polynomial-value and sifted-orbit settings.

A modern general framework is Granville–Soundararajan, “Sieving and the Erdős–Kac theorem,” arXiv `math/0606039`. Daniel El-Baz proves an Erdős–Kac analogue for entries of special-linear matrices using that framework and homogeneous dynamics (arXiv `1811.01919`). Tenenbaum–Weingartner prove an Erdős–Kac theorem for integers with dense divisors, *Quarterly Journal of Mathematics* 75 (2024), 161–195, DOI `10.1093/qmath/haae002`.

The common feature is not merely “structure”; it is a theorem giving the required local divisor densities and their correlations.

### The examples in the question

1. **Central binomial coefficients:** not Erdős–Kac on the random-integer scale. Their deterministic prime interval gives \(\omega\gg n/\log n\), much larger than \(\log n\).
2. **Catalan numbers:** the same obstruction remains after dividing by \(n+1\).
3. **Partition values \(p(n)\):** I found no primary-source Erdős–Kac theorem for \(\omega(p(n))\).
4. **Apéry numbers:** the Luca–Shparlinski density-one lower bounds appear to be the strongest directly relevant anatomy theorem; they do not establish a Gaussian law.
5. **Classical linear recurrences such as Fibonacci:** primitive-divisor and greatest-prime-factor theorems are abundant, but they are not an Erdős–Kac law for \(\omega(F_n)\).

So an Erdős–Kac theorem for \(b_n\) would be a substantial new result. The random-integer prediction is

\[
 rac{\omega(b_n)-\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\Longrightarrow N(0,1),
\]

up to the constant shift \(\log\kappa\). Proving it would require asymptotics for all fixed moments of the indicators \(\mathbf1_{p\mid b_n}\), with a prime range growing with \(n\). The currently proved second-moment information is far short of that—but it is in the correct conceptual direction.

## 6. What does ABC give?

Apply ABC, after dividing out the common gcd, to

\[
 A=(n+1)^3b_{n+1},\qquad
 B=n^3b_{n-1},\qquad
 C=P(n)b_n,\qquad A+B=C.
\]

ABC supplies a lower bound for the radical of the **entire triple** relative to its height. Schematically,

\[
 \operatorname{rad}\!igl(n(n+1)P(n)b_{n-1}b_nb_{n+1}igr)
 \geq \exp(c_arepsilon n)
\]

up to gcd and \(arepsilon\)-losses.

There are two obstructions.

1. The radicals of \(b_{n-1}\) and \(b_{n+1}\) can absorb the lower bound. ABC does not isolate \(\operatorname{rad}(b_n)\).
2. Even an isolated **lower** bound for \(\operatorname{rad}(b_n)\) does not give an upper bound for \(\omega(b_n)\). A large radical can be one enormous prime or many moderate primes.

ABC is often useful for proving that high powers cannot dominate, or for obtaining primitive divisors in autonomous dynamical sequences. For example, Gratton–Nguyen–Tucker prove an ABC-conditional primitive-divisor theorem for rational dynamical orbits, *Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society* 45 (2013), 1194–1208, DOI `10.1112/blms/bdt049`. The Apéry recurrence is nonautonomous and is not covered by that theorem.

Most importantly, the weak conclusion asked in Question 6 is already unconditional by (0.5):

\[
 oxed{\omega(b_n)=o(n).}
\]

ABC does not appear to improve this to the empirically relevant \(O(\log n)\), nor to the pointwise critical-tail statement

\[
 \omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(b_n)=o(N/\log N).
\]

## 7. Primitive prime divisors

### No known Apéry theorem

The Bilu–Hanrot–Voutier theorem applies to Lucas and Lehmer sequences, whose terms come from fixed algebraic characteristic roots and possess strong divisibility properties. They prove that every term beyond index \(30\) has a primitive divisor:

> Yuri Bilu, Guillaume Hanrot, Paul Voutier, “Existence of primitive divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers,” *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik* 539 (2001), 75–122, DOI `10.1515/crll.2001.080`.

The Apéry recurrence has polynomially varying coefficients and the sequence is not a divisibility sequence. I found no proved analogue asserting a primitive divisor for every sufficiently large Apéry number.

### The proposed monotonicity implication is false

Even if every \(b_n\) had a primitive prime divisor, it would not imply

\[
 \omega(b_n)\geq\omega(b_{n-1})+1.
\]

Prime factors of \(b_{n-1}\) need not remain factors of \(b_n\). Indeed,

\[
 b_1=5,\qquad b_2=73,
\]

and \(73\) is primitive, but

\[
 \omega(b_1)=\omega(b_2)=1.
\]

A primitive-divisor theorem would instead imply growth of the union of all prime divisors:

\[
 \omega\!\left(\prod_{m\leq N}b_might)\geq N-O(1).
 	ag{7.1}
\]

That is a lower bound and does not control the number of primes in a single term.

### A useful Apéry-specific observation

The Lucas congruence gives, for the base-\(p\) expansion \(n=\sum_i n_ip^i\),

\[
 b_n\equiv\prod_i b_{n_i}\pmod p.
\]

If \(p\leq n\) and \(p\mid b_n\), then \(p\mid b_{n_i}\) for some digit \(n_i<p\leq n\). Hence \(p\) already divides an earlier Apéry number. Therefore

\[
 oxed{	ext{every primitive prime divisor of }b_n	ext{ must exceed }n.}
 	ag{7.2}
\]

Thus a primitive-divisor theorem would prove the existence of at least one large prime in every sufficiently large term. It would again be lower-tail information, not an upper bound for \(\omega_{>\sqrt n}(b_n)\).

## What a viable direct-sieve theorem would look like

There are two materially different goals.

### A. Average or density-one control

For \(p\) in a prime window, prove uniform local-density formulas

\[
 A_d(N)=\#\{n\leq N:d\mid b_n\}
       =N g(d)+r_d
\]

for squarefree \(d\), with summed remainder bounds over \(d\) having several prime factors. This can yield first moments, variance estimates, and perhaps an Erdős–Kac theorem. It cannot by itself remove all exceptional indices.

For the relevant large-prime window, order switching gives

\[
 \sum_{n\leq N}\omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(b_n)
 =\sum_{\sqrt N<p\leq N}\#\{n\leq N:p\mid b_n\},
\]

which is naturally expressed through the zero sets \(Z_p\) and Lucas blocks. This is the correct place for Selberg/large-sieve ideas.

### B. Pointwise all-\(n\) control

To prove the required bound for every \(n\), one needs additional rigidity beyond moments. Two possible forms are:

1. a low-height carrier \(C_n\) divisible by every relevant singleton prime;
2. an Apéry-specific orbit theorem forcing many primes dividing \(b_n\) to create repeated-zero/collision data that the gap-polynomial machinery can charge.

The current recurrence identities provide neither for isolated prime factors. This is why the repository's `atom_tail_section.tex` explicitly uses the trivial height estimate

\[
 \omega_X(M)\leqrac{\log|M|}{\log X}
\]

for integer carriers and then spends its main effort producing collision carriers of controlled height. A generic “anatomy of integers” theorem does not close the singleton gap.

## Recommended conclusion for Problem 3.2

The direct-\(\omega(b_n)\) route should not be treated as an available black-box sieve avenue. The honest status is:

- **Unconditional:** \(\omega(b_n)=O(n/\log n)=o(n)\).
- **Generic pointwise large-prime bound:** only the size/prime-supply scale \(O(N/\log N)\).
- **Density-one Apéry anatomy:** lower bounds of Luca–Shparlinski.
- **No known:** pointwise \(O(\log n)\), Erdős–Kac law, or Apéry primitive-divisor theorem.
- **Recurrence strength:** repeated-return/collision control only.

The highest-return sieve question is therefore not “can Turán–Kubilius be cited?” but the concrete sequence-specific problem

\[
 A_{p_1\cdots p_k}(N)\quad	ext{for distinct primes }p_i
\]

in the exact moving windows used by the proof. That can strengthen density-one and moment results. The all-\(n\) theorem still requires a deterministic singleton-atom mechanism or a smaller-height carrier.
