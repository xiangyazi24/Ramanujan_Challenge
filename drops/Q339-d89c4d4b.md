# Verdict

The proposed implication has two sharply different parts:

1. **If one could prove a genuinely uniform \(O(1)\) variance bound on an interval of length \(N\), then the pointwise conclusion \(\max B_{\mathrm{low}}\ll \sqrt N\) would follow immediately.** This part is correct, up to a harmless endpoint adjustment in the Chebyshev argument.
2. **CRT and the average zero-mass hypothesis \(\sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll\pi(x)\) do not prove that variance bound.** The desired estimate
   \[
   \sum_{p\ne q}R_{pq}=O(N)
   \quad\text{or}\quad o(N)
   \]
   is itself a strong short-interval, cross-prime anti-concentration theorem. It can fail by a factor about \(N/\log^2 N\) even when every \(z_p\le2\), the sets \(Z_p\) have the Apéry reflection symmetry, and no \(Z_p\) contains consecutive residues.

Thus the CRT-Chebyshev route is a valid **conditional reduction**, but not an elementary completion of the proof. It replaces \((\mathrm{HM})_3\) or the Sparse Bridge input by a localized second-moment theorem which is already strong enough to rule out every isolated bad integer.

The existing repository result \((\mathrm{HM})_2\) does not supply this theorem. In `problems/3.2/pairpalm_result.tex`, it averages primes \(p\asymp X\) over an interval of length \(X^2\). The proposed argument needs essentially the same Poisson-scale second moment over an interval of length only \(X\). That is an extra factor-\(X\) saving.

# 1. First correct the finite-interval bookkeeping

Fix for the moment a set of primes \(\mathcal P\subseteq(\sqrt N,N]\), and define

\[
Y_N(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}X_p(n),
\qquad
X_p(n)=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p},
\qquad 1\le n\le N.
\]

This is not literally the variable-window quantity \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\), because the latter also imposes \(p\le n\) and \(p>\sqrt n\). One can handle that by working on dyadic intervals \(N<n\le2N\), inserting the cutoff \(p\le n\), or simply dominating by a fixed prime range. None of these boundary choices affects the obstruction below; at the endpoint \(n=N\), the counterexample is already exactly in the required window.

Put

\[
\rho_p=\frac{z_p}{p},
\qquad
\lambda_N=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\rho_p,
\qquad
S_N=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}z_p.
\]

## Marginals

For one residue class modulo \(p\), the number of representatives in \([1,N]\) is \(N/p+O(1)\). Therefore

\[
A_p(N):=\#\{n\le N:n\bmod p\in Z_p\}
       =N\frac{z_p}{p}+E_p,
\qquad |E_p|\le z_p.
\]

Consequently

\[
\mathbb E X_p=\frac{z_p}{p}+O\!\left(\frac{z_p}{N}\right).
\]

The error is **not** generally \(O(1/p^2)\). Since \(p>\sqrt N\), one has \(1/p^2<1/N\), so \(O(1/p^2)\) is actually smaller than the unavoidable endpoint error for a single residue.

If

\[
\sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll\pi(x),
\]

then \(S_N\ll N/\log N\), hence

\[
\frac1N\sum_pE_p=O(1/\log N)=o(1).
\]

Partial summation also gives \(\lambda_N=O(1)\) over \((\sqrt N,N]\).

## Pairs

For \(p\ne q\), let

\[
C_{pq}(N)
 =\#\{n\le N:n\bmod p\in Z_p,\ n\bmod q\in Z_q\}.
\]

CRT identifies \(z_pz_q\) residue classes modulo \(pq\), so

\[
C_{pq}(N)=N\frac{z_pz_q}{pq}+R_{pq}(N),
\qquad |R_{pq}(N)|\le z_pz_q.
\]

When \(pq>N\), every individual CRT class contributes either zero or one representative to \([1,N]\). Its probability under uniform \(n\le N\) is therefore either \(0\) or \(1/N\), not \(1/(pq)\) plus an error \(O(1/(pq))\). The comparison with \(1/(pq)\) is precisely the discrepancy term \(R_{pq}/N\).

So the displayed decomposition in the question is fine after \(R_{pq}\) is introduced, but CRT itself gives only the pointwise bound \(|R_{pq}|\le z_pz_q\). It gives no cancellation when the errors are summed over pairs.

# 2. Exact variance identity

Let

\[
e_N=\frac1N\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}E_p,
\qquad
R_N^{(2)}=\sum_{\substack{p,q\in\mathcal P\\p\ne q}}R_{pq}(N),
\]

where the pair sum is ordered. Then

\[
\mathbb E Y_N=\lambda_N+e_N
\]

and

\[
\mathbb E (Y_N)_2
 =\sum_{p\ne q}\rho_p\rho_q+\frac{R_N^{(2)}}N
 =\lambda_N^2-\sum_p\rho_p^2+\frac{R_N^{(2)}}N.
\]

Using \(Y^2=Y+(Y)_2\), one obtains the exact formula

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(Y_N)
 =\lambda_N-\sum_p\rho_p^2
  +\frac{R_N^{(2)}}N
  +e_N(1-2\lambda_N)-e_N^2.}
\]

Under \(S_N\ll N/\log N\) and \(\lambda_N=O(1)\), the last two terms are \(o(1)\). In the actual Apéry setting, the uniform bound \(z_p=O(p^{2/3})\) from `problems/3.2/proof.tex` also gives

\[
\max_{p>\sqrt N}\rho_p=o(1),
\qquad
\sum_p\rho_p^2
 \le \bigl(\max_p\rho_p\bigr)\lambda_N=o(1).
\]

Therefore the proposed implication is correct:

\[
R_N^{(2)}=o(N)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Var}(Y_N)=\lambda_N+o(1)=O(1).
\]

For the pointwise conclusion, however, one does not need the absolute estimate \(|R_N^{(2)}|=o(N)\), nor even a Poisson asymptotic. A one-sided upper bound

\[
R_N^{(2)}\ll N^{1+o(1)}
\]

would give

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Y_N)\ll N^{o(1)},
\]

which is already enough for \(Y_N(n)\le N^{1/2+o(1)}\).

# 3. If the variance were \(O(1)\), the maximum bound would indeed follow

Suppose

\[
\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}|Y_N(n)-\overline Y_N|^2\le C
\]

uniformly in \(N\). Then for every individual \(n\le N\),

\[
|Y_N(n)-\overline Y_N|^2
 \le \sum_{m\le N}|Y_N(m)-\overline Y_N|^2
 \le CN.
\]

Hence

\[
\max_{n\le N}Y_N(n)\le \overline Y_N+\sqrt{CN}=O(\sqrt N).
\]

This is just the deterministic \(L^2\)-to-\(L^\infty\) inequality on a finite set; Chebyshev is not really needed.

In the formulation in the question, setting the Chebyshev upper bound equal to \(1\) only proves that there is at most one exceptional integer. To prove there are none, take a slightly larger threshold, or use the direct inequality above. This changes only the absolute constant.

Thus step (c) in the proposed chain is sound. The entire issue is step (b).

# 4. A counterexample: average local mass does not control the total CRT error

The failure is not merely hypothetical. There are fixed families of sparse residue sets satisfying several of the known local Apéry constraints but having huge diagonal spikes.

Choose a rapidly increasing sequence \(N_j\) with

\[
N_{j+1}>N_j^3,
\]

so the intervals \((N_j/2,N_j]\) are disjoint. For every odd prime

\[
p\in (N_j/2,N_j]
\]

except at most two harmless endpoint primes, put

\[
r_p=N_j-p=N_j\bmod p
\]

and define

\[
Z_p=\{r_p,\ p-1-r_p\},
\]

with a duplicate collapsed if \(r_p=(p-1)/2\). Put \(Z_p=\varnothing\) for all other primes. Omit the at most two primes for which one of the displayed residues is \(0\).

This family has:

- \(z_p\le2\) for every prime;
- \(\sum_{p\le x}z_p\le2\pi(x)\);
- reflection symmetry \(r\in Z_p\Longleftrightarrow p-1-r\in Z_p\);
- no consecutive residues in \(Z_p\), because the two reflected residues have even difference for odd \(p\);
- \(0\notin Z_p\).

Nevertheless, at \(n=N_j\), every prime \(p\in(N_j/2,N_j]\) retained above satisfies

\[
N_j\bmod p=N_j-p=r_p\in Z_p.
\]

Therefore

\[
B_{\mathrm{low}}(N_j)
 \ge \pi(N_j)-\pi(N_j/2)-O(1)
 \asymp \frac{N_j}{\log N_j}.
\]

So \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(N_j)\) is as large as the forbidden scale, despite the strongest possible average bound \(z_p\le2\).

The same example makes the pair error explicit. Restrict to primes \(p,q\in(N_j/2,N_j]\). Then \(pq>N_j\), and \(n=N_j\) is a representative of the CRT class determined by the primary residues \(r_p,r_q\). Hence

\[
C_{pq}(N_j)\ge1.
\]

Since \(z_pz_q\le4\) and \(pq>N_j^2/4\),

\[
N_j\frac{z_pz_q}{pq}\le\frac{16}{N_j}.
\]

Thus

\[
R_{pq}(N_j)\ge1-\frac{16}{N_j}
\]

for every ordered pair of distinct retained primes in that block. Summing gives

\[
R_{N_j}^{(2)}
 \gg \left(\frac{N_j}{\log N_j}\right)^2,
\]

which is not merely non-\(o(N_j)\); it exceeds \(N_j\) by a factor \(N_j/\log^2N_j\).

This counterexample is not claimed to arise from the Apéry recurrence. Its role is decisive but limited:

> No argument using only the cardinalities \(z_p\), CRT, reflection, and no-consecutive-zero information can prove the desired variance bound. One must use a genuine cross-prime property of the actual Apéry sets \(Z_p\).

# 5. Relation to the proved repository \((\mathrm{HM})_2\)

The distinction in interval length explains exactly why the existing second moment does not close the problem.

For a dyadic prime block

\[
\mathcal P_X=\{p:X<p\le2X\},
\qquad
K_X(m)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}1_{m\bmod p\in Z_p},
\qquad
\lambda_X=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}\frac{z_p}{p},
\]

`problems/3.2/pairpalm_result.tex` takes

\[
M=X^2
\]

and proves the sharpened form

\[
\sum_{0\le m<M}(K_X(m))_2\le4M\lambda_X^2.
\]

Indeed, for \(p\ne q\) in \((X,2X]\), one has \(pq>X^2=M\), so every chosen residue pair has at most one representative below \(M\). Therefore

\[
\sum_{m<M}(K_X(m))_2
 \le \left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}z_p\right)^2
 \le (2X\lambda_X)^2
 =4X^2\lambda_X^2.
\]

This is the natural deterministic CRT scale. The interval has length \(X^2\), matching the size of a two-prime modulus.

For the all-\(n\) problem at integers \(n\asymp X\), the relevant interval length is only

\[
M\asymp X.
\]

Applying the same CRT counting gives only

\[
\sum_{m\in I}(K_X(m))_2
 \le \left(\sum_pz_p\right)^2
 \ll X^2\lambda_X^2,
\]

whereas a Poisson-scale short-interval estimate would be

\[
\sum_{m\in I}(K_X(m))_2
 \ll |I|\lambda_X^2
 \asymp X\lambda_X^2.
\]

The missing saving is a full factor \(X\).

If \(z_p\) has bounded average, then \(\lambda_X\asymp1/\log X\) heuristically. The two scales are therefore

\[
\text{proved deterministic bound: }
 \frac{X^2}{\log^2X},
\qquad
\text{desired Poisson bound: }
 \frac{X}{\log^2X}.
\]

A single aligned row with

\[
K_X(m)\asymp X/\log X
\]

contributes \(X^2/\log^2X\) by itself and saturates the proved \((\mathrm{HM})_2\) budget. This is why the \(X^2\)-length second moment allows exactly the isolated spike that the all-\(n\) theorem must exclude.

A precise name for the new required statement would be a **short-interval HM\(_2\)** or **quenched pair-dispersion estimate**, for example

\[
\boxed{
\sup_{|I|\asymp X}
\sum_{m\in I}
\left|
 K_X(m)-\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}\frac{z_p}{p}
\right|^2
\ll X^{1+o(1)}.}
\]

Such an estimate would indeed bypass \((\mathrm{HM})_3\), because it directly rules out spikes by \(L^2\)-to-\(L^\infty\). But it is not a consequence of the proved \((\mathrm{HM})_2\); it is a substantially stronger localization theorem.

This agrees with the diagnosis in `problems/3.2/UNDERSTANDING.md`: the remaining issue is a quenched diagonal anti-concentration statement, not one-prime zero sparsity.

# 6. What the standard large sieve actually gives

The correct large-sieve orientation is the **dual** inequality.

For each prime \(p\), define the centered periodic function

\[
f_p(n)=1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}-\frac{z_p}{p}.
\]

With normalized Fourier coefficients

\[
\widehat f_p(a)
 =\frac1p\sum_{r\bmod p}f_p(r)e_p(-ar),
\]

one has \(\widehat f_p(0)=0\) and

\[
f_p(n)=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\widehat f_p(a)e_p(an).
\]

Parseval gives

\[
\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}|\widehat f_p(a)|^2
 =\frac{z_p}{p}\left(1-\frac{z_p}{p}\right)
 \le\frac{z_p}{p}.
\]

The fractions \(a/p\), with \(p\le Q\) prime and \(1\le a<p\), are distinct reduced fractions and are separated by at least \(1/Q^2\). The dual large sieve therefore yields

\[
\sum_{n\le N}
\left|\sum_{p\le Q}f_p(n)\right|^2
\ll (N+Q^2)
\sum_{p\le Q}\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}|\widehat f_p(a)|^2.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}
\left|\sum_{p\le Q}f_p(n)\right|^2
\ll (N+Q^2)\sum_{p\le Q}\frac{z_p}{p}.}
\]

For the range in the question, \(Q=N\) and

\[
\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac{z_p}{p}=O(1).
\]

The result is therefore only

\[
\sum_{n\le N}|\cdots|^2\ll N^2,
\]

or an average variance bound \(O(N)\), not \(O(1)\). The resulting pointwise estimate is \(O(N)\), which is useless for the target.

More revealingly, for a dyadic prime block \(p\asymp X\), the large sieve gives

\[
\sum_{n\le N}
\left|\sum_{X<p\le2X}f_p(n)\right|^2
\ll (N+X^2)\lambda_X.
\]

- If \(X\le\sqrt N\), the \(N\)-term dominates and one gets a useful variance-scale estimate.
- If \(X>\sqrt N\), the \(X^2\)-term dominates.

The quantity \(B_{\mathrm{low}}\) lies precisely on the wrong side of this square-root barrier. Its prime moduli all satisfy \(X>\sqrt N\).

Taking \(a_n=1\) in the primal large sieve estimates the already elementary distribution of the interval \([1,N]\) modulo individual primes. It does not insert the phases of \(Z_p\) in the way needed to control the sum over \(p\). Once the indicators are Fourier-expanded and the relevant weights are included, one returns to the dual estimate above and its \(Q^2\) barrier.

Restricting the moduli to primes can save logarithms in some large-sieve variants, but it cannot supply the missing factor \(X\). The aligned singleton/reflection counterexample proves that no inequality valid for arbitrary sparse \(Z_p\) can give the desired \(O(N)\) mean square.

Even strong one-prime Fourier magnitude bounds are not enough by themselves. A singleton residue set has perfectly flat nonzero Fourier magnitudes, yet singleton sets can be phase-aligned across primes to create the counterexample. The missing information is cross-prime phase/discrepancy control.

# 7. Sieve-theoretic references and the correct analogy

The closest standard framework is the **Turán sieve**, or more generally a probabilistic/abstract sieve with local events. In that framework one writes a counting function as a sum of local indicators, expands its second moment, and obtains:

- local density terms;
- pair-density terms;
- explicit two-modulus remainder terms.

In the present notation, those pair remainders are exactly \(R_{pq}\). A Turán-sieve theorem does not make them small automatically; their control is an input.

Useful references are:

1. A. C. Cojocaru and M. R. Murty, *An Introduction to Sieve Methods and Their Applications*, Chapter 4, “The Turán sieve.”
2. E. Kowalski, *The Large Sieve and its Applications*, especially Chapter 2 on the large-sieve principle and Chapter 6 on probabilistic sieves.
3. P. X. Gallagher, “The large sieve,” *Mathematika* 14 (1967), 14–20.
4. H. L. Montgomery and R. C. Vaughan, “The large sieve,” *Mathematika* 20 (1973), 119–134.
5. P. D. T. A. Elliott, “A local Turán–Kubilius inequality,” *Acta Arithmetica* 49 (1987), 127–139, for comparison with genuinely additive functions.

The Turán–Kubilius analogy is suggestive but not transferable without a new theorem. For an additive function built from divisibility indicators \(1_{p\mid n}\), the local classes are all the zero class, and multiplicativity imposes strong compatibility. In particular, if \(p,q>\sqrt N\), then no positive \(n\le N\) can be divisible by both \(p\) and \(q\). The large-prime covariance is therefore automatically nonpositive.

For shifted sparse sets \(Z_p\), the opposite extreme is possible: every \(Z_p\) may contain the residue of the same integer \(N\). There is no multiplicative exclusivity. That is exactly what the counterexample exploits.

Gallagher's **larger sieve** is also not the required tool. It bounds the size of a set of integers that occupies unusually few residue classes modulo many primes. Here the problem is a possible concentration of many prime-indexed sparse target sets at one integer. The aligned-residue construction again shows that occupancy information alone cannot control the variance.

# 8. At what scale does the CRT error become noticeable?

There are two different answers.

## Pair-by-pair scale

For primes \(p,q\asymp X\) and an interval of length \(M\), the expected number of representatives of one CRT class is

\[
\frac{M}{pq}\asymp\frac{M}{X^2}.
\]

The endpoint error \(O(1)\) per class is relatively small only when

\[
M\gg X^2.
\]

The transition is therefore

\[
M\asymp pq\asymp X^2.
\]

In the question, \(M=N\) and \(p,q>\sqrt N\), so \(pq>N=M\). Thus the CRT error is already dominant for every pair in the proposed range.

- Near \(p,q\asymp\sqrt N\), one is at the transition \(pq\asymp N\).
- Near \(p,q\asymp N\), the expected count per class is only \(\asymp1/N\), while the actual count is \(0\) or \(1\).

So the empirical Poisson variance is not occurring because each pair is individually well equidistributed. It is occurring because a huge collection of order-one pair discrepancies appears to cancel in aggregate.

## Aggregate random-model scale

If the CRT representatives behaved like independent random points, then for bounded \(z_p\)

\[
\operatorname{Var}(R_N^{(2)})
 \approx N\sum_{p\ne q}\frac{z_pz_q}{pq}
 \asymp N\lambda_N^2.
\]

This predicts

\[
R_N^{(2)}\text{ of size about }\sqrt N\,\lambda_N,
\qquad
\frac{R_N^{(2)}}N\text{ of size about }\frac{\lambda_N}{\sqrt N}.
\]

For \(\lambda_N=O(1)\), the normalized correction is heuristically \(N^{-1/2}\). At \(N=10^5\), that scale is roughly \(3\times10^{-3}\), comparable to ordinary finite-sample fluctuations in an empirical variance. Thus the observed agreement with a Poisson variance near \(0.5\) is quite compatible with the random model.

This calculation is heuristic: the pair errors share primes and integers and are far from independent. It nevertheless explains why there need not be a later scale at which a random-model CRT correction suddenly becomes visible. Under genuine randomness, the normalized correction should shrink.

In the worst case, by contrast,

\[
R_N^{(2)}\asymp\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}
\]

can occur at arbitrarily large scales. Therefore there is no deterministic crossover scale. The alternatives are:

- random-like aggregate cancellation, producing a vanishing correction;
- arithmetic alignment, producing isolated giant spikes.

The computation through \(10^5\) is evidence for the first behavior of the Apéry sets, but does not prove that the second can never occur.

# 9. The exact theorem that would make the route work

A clean sufficient statement is the following short-interval second-moment estimate, uniformly in \(N\):

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}
\left|
\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}
\left(1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}-\frac{z_p}{p}\right)
\right|^2
\ll N^{1+o(1)}.}
\]

For the literal \(B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)\), one may formulate the same estimate on dyadic integer intervals and include the cutoffs \(p\le n\) and \(p>\sqrt n\), or decompose the primes into dyadic blocks. Polylogarithmic losses are harmless.

Together with

\[
\sum_{p\le x}z_p\ll\pi(x),
\]

this would imply

\[
\max_{n\le N}B_{\mathrm{low}}(n)
 \le N^{1/2+o(1)}
 =o(N/\log N),
\]

and hence close the all-\(n\) GCD problem through the known reduction.

Equivalently, after the harmless marginal corrections, it asks for

\[
\sum_{p\ne q}R_{pq}(N)\ll N^{1+o(1)}.
\]

This is a legitimate alternative target. But it should be recorded as a new **short-interval pair-dispersion theorem**, not as “pairwise independence from CRT.” It is precisely a cross-prime statement about the positions of the Apéry zero sets.

# Final answers to the four questions

1. **Can one prove \(\left|\sum R_{pq}\right|=o(N)\) from CRT and \(\sum z_p\ll\pi(x)\)?** No. The assertion is false for general sparse families, even with \(z_p\le2\), reflection symmetry, no consecutive residues, and \(0\notin Z_p\). For the actual Apéry sets it is an open cross-prime discrepancy statement. If it were proved, then the variance argument would work.

2. **Can the standard large sieve give the variance?** Not in the required range. The dual large sieve gives
   \[
   \sum_{n\le N}|\sum_pf_p(n)|^2
   \ll (N+Q^2)\sum_pz_p/p.
   \]
   With \(Q=N\), this is \(O(N^2)\), not \(O(N)\). The obstruction is the classical \(Q^2\) barrier, and \(B_{\mathrm{low}}\) lives entirely above \(Q=\sqrt N\).

3. **What is the correct sieve reference?** The exact framework is the Turán sieve/probabilistic sieve: the second moment is reduced to local densities plus two-modulus remainder terms. Turán–Kubilius applies to additive functions because divisibility supplies special multiplicative compatibility; it does not automatically apply to arbitrary shifted sparse residue sets.

4. **When does the CRT error become noticeable?** Pairwise, immediately when \(pq>N\), which is exactly the proposed range. The observed Poisson variance means aggregate cancellation is occurring empirically. A random model predicts a normalized error of order \(N^{-1/2}\), about \(0.003\) at \(N=10^5\); a worst-case aligned family can instead produce an error of order \(N/\log^2N\) in the variance at arbitrarily large scales.
