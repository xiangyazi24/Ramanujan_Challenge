# Z_p = first-break nonordinary locus: analysis of the claim

## Executive summary

The revised claim is essentially the correct one, but the original equivalence with full nonordinarity is too strong. The trace modulo p only detects the existence of a slope-zero Frobenius eigenvalue. Therefore the correct geometric condition is **first-break ordinarity**, not full ordinarity.

The key point is:

$$
b_r \equiv -\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi_{p,r}) \pmod p
$$

and modulo p the trace sees only slope-zero eigenvalues. It does not detect a defect occurring at the second Hodge break.

---

# (A) Trace versus Mellin coefficient

The identification is correct only after fixing normalization.

The Mellin coefficient

$$
b_r(p)=\sum_t Ha_p(t)t^{-r}$$

is a trace function of the Kummer twist of the Apéry sheaf. By the Grothendieck trace formula it is the Frobenius trace of the twisted cohomology, up to the usual sign and Tate normalization:

$$
b_r(p)=\pm p^m \operatorname{Tr}(\Phi_{p,r}|H^i_{\mathrm{cris}}).
$$

For the normalized middle cohomology crystal one chooses the Tate twist so that the weight-zero part is integral. Under this normalization the congruence relevant to zeros is:

$$
b_r(p)\equiv -\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi_{p,r})\pmod p.
$$

A missing Tate twist would shift all slopes, so one must use the Gauss-normalized crystal, not the raw geometric Frobenius on H^2.

---

# (B) The gap: full nonordinary versus first-break nonordinary

The original proof has a genuine gap.

For Hodge slopes

$$0,1,2,$$

the Hodge polygon has two nontrivial breaks. A Newton polygon above it can fail to coincide in two different ways.

It is possible to have Newton slopes

$$
(0,s,3-s),\qquad s>1,
$$

while the first slope remains zero.

Then there is still a unit-root eigenvalue:

$$v_p(\alpha)=0.$$

Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi)=\alpha+p\beta+p^2\gamma\equiv \alpha\not\equiv0\pmod p.
$$

So second-break nonordinarity does NOT create a zero of b_r.

Hence:

$$
\text{full nonordinary}\Rightarrow b_r=0
$$

is true only when nonordinary means failure at the first break. General nonordinary is too broad.

---

# (C) Correct equivalence

The corrected statement is:

$$
 b_r\equiv0\pmod p
 \iff
 \text{the twisted crystal has no slope-zero Frobenius eigenvalue}
$$

or equivalently:

$$
Z_p=E_{p}^{sl,1}
$$

where $E_p^{sl,1}$ denotes the first-break nonordinary locus.

The second break is invisible to the mod-p trace.

This is an important sharpening: AFP is not a theorem about complete ordinarity. It is a theorem about **horizontal first-break ordinarity**.

---

# (D) Existing ordinarity results

Generic ordinarity results do not immediately give the needed statement.

## Adolphson-Sperber

Adolphson-Sperber theory controls generic Newton polygons of hypergeometric and exponential sums. It proves equality with the Hodge polygon generically under nondegeneracy assumptions.

However the Apéry problem requires a horizontal statement:

$$
\sum_{p\le X}\#\{r:\text{first slope}>0\}\ll \pi(X).
$$

This is much stronger than generic ordinarity.

## Wan

Wan's work on Dwork families and slope variation is closer. It studies p-adic variation of Newton polygons and ordinary loci.

But a uniform bound over all tame Kummer characters is still an additional input.

## Kedlaya

Kedlaya's algorithms compute Newton polygons effectively. They can verify examples but do not supply the required asymptotic theorem.

The missing theorem is therefore a horizontal ordinarity theorem for the Apéry hypergeometric family.

---

# (E) Unit-root formula route

This is probably the most promising concrete route.

For an ordinary twist, the unit root satisfies a Dwork-type congruence:

$$
\alpha_r\equiv H_p(r)\pmod p.
$$

Therefore:

$$
H_p(r)\ne0
\Longrightarrow
\text{first slope}=0
\Longrightarrow
b_r\ne0\pmod p.
$$

Thus one wants to prove:

$$
\#\{r:H_p(r)=0\}=O(1)
$$

or on average over p:

$$
\sum_{p\le X}\#\{r:H_p(r)=0\}\ll\pi(X).
$$

This converts AFP into an explicit hypergeometric zero-counting problem.

The corrected chain is:

\iff

$$

The target is therefore not complete Hodge-Newton coincidence, but control of the first Newton slope.

This removes the apparent contradiction caused by possible slope patterns such as $(0,s,3-s)$: those are invisible to $b_r\bmod p$ and should not be included in the exceptional set.
