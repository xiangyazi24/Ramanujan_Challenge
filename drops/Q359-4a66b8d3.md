# Q359 — Chen–Stein, Poisson heuristics, and the exact second-moment target

## Executive verdict

The proposed Chen–Stein shortcut does **not** work as stated.

1. The indicators \(X_p\) do not have a natural sparse dependency graph. They are all functions of the same random integer \(n\). The only unconditional exact dependency graph is the complete graph, which gives a useless Chen–Stein bound.

2. Declaring the neighborhoods empty is invalid. In the usual dependency-graph formulation, an empty graph means **joint mutual independence**, not pairwise near-independence. If one instead uses singleton neighborhoods without exact independence, the omitted Chen–Stein term \(b_3\) measures the remaining global dependence.

3. The CRT estimate in the question is at the wrong scale. A normalized error \(O(1/N)\) per prime pair sums to \(O(N/\log^2 N)\), whereas the normalized second factorial moment must be \(O(1)\). In the unnormalized energy this is \(O(N^2/\log^2 N)\), exactly the bad scale one is trying to beat.

4. The second base-\(p\) digit is not a harmless perturbation. A low root \(a\in Z_p\) infects an interval of length about \(p\), not merely one residue class. It creates three additional pair channels—suffix/leading, leading/suffix, and leading/leading—which are not controlled by the suffix/suffix CRT argument.

5. Even a genuine total-variation Poisson limit would **not by itself imply** \(\sum B_N(n)^2=O(N)\). Total variation does not control an unbounded second moment without uniform integrability or a sufficiently strong quantitative tail estimate. An anchored star changes the empirical law at only one point, hence by \(O(1/N)\) in total variation, but contributes \(\asymp N^2/\log^2 N\) to the energy.

6. For P3.2, Poisson approximation is therefore stronger than necessary and, in its usual total-variation form, still not sufficient. The right direct target is the first two factorial moments:

\[
\sum_{p}|E_p|=O(N),
\qquad
\sum_{p<q}|E_p\cap E_q|=O(N),
\]

where \(E_p=\{n\le N:p\mid b_n\}\). After Lucas, the second estimate splits into four explicit digit channels. This is the weakest natural condition and exactly distinguishes the Apéry heuristic from the anchored star.

Thus:

> **Chen–Stein can be used only after proving an Apéry-specific Kubilius/transference or conditional-dependence theorem. Such a theorem is at least as difficult as the required two-prime incidence estimate. For closing P3.2, prove the second moment directly rather than first proving a Poisson law.**

\Pr(Y\ge3)&=0.0231\ldots,
\end{aligned}

not \(62.7\%,23\%,10\%,4\%\). Also

\[
\mathbb E Y^2=\operatorname{Var}(Y)+(\mathbb EY)^2=0.6+0.6^2=0.96.

If instead

\[
\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=1.63
\]

and \(\operatorname{Var}(B_N)/\mathbb E B_N=1\), then the mean \(m\) must satisfy

\[
m+m^2=1.63,
\]

so

\[
m=0.871\ldots,
\]

not \(0.6\). Equivalently, mean \(0.6\) and second moment \(1.63\) would give variance/mean about \(2.12\), not \(1\).

This may simply mean that the histogram, second-moment table, and quoted \(\lambda\) came from different prime windows or different versions of `B_low`. It should be fixed before using a fitted Poisson parameter.

There are two additional repository-state cautions.

None of these corrections damages the basic Poisson heuristic, but they matter for the exact theorem one tries to prove.

## 1. Put the probability space and the target in the correct form

Because the prime window depends on \(N\), write

\[
B_N(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}X_p(n),
\qquad
\mathcal P_N:=\{p\text{ prime}:\sqrt N<p\le N\},
\]

where

\[
X_p(n)=\mathbf 1_{p\mid b_n}.
\]

It is convenient to sample \(n\) uniformly from

\[
\Omega_N:=\{0,1,\ldots,N\}.
\]

Since \(b_0=1\), the added point \(n=0\) contributes zero, so the numerator of every moment is exactly the original sum over \(1\le n\le N\). Put

\[
E_p:=\{n\in\Omega_N:p\mid b_n\},
\qquad
\mu_p:=\frac{|E_p|}{N+1},
\qquad
\mu_{p,q}:=\frac{|E_p\cap E_q|}{N+1}.
\]

Then

\[
\lambda_N:=\mathbb E B_N=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}\mu_p
\]

and the exact second-moment identity is

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E B_N^2
 =\lambda_N+2\sum_{\substack{p<q\\p,q\in\mathcal P_N}}\mu_{p,q}.
}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
 =\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}|E_p|
 +2\sum_{\substack{p<q\\p,q\in\mathcal P_N}}|E_p\cap E_q|.
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Thus

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N)
\]

is equivalent to the two nonnegative estimates

\[
\sum_p|E_p|=O(N),
\qquad
\sum_{p<q}|E_p\cap E_q|=O(N).
\tag{1.3}
\]

This is already the complete answer at second order. No Poisson theorem is needed.

A useful covariance version is the following. If \(\lambda_N=O(1)\), then it is sufficient to prove

\[
\sum_{p<q}
\bigl(\mu_{p,q}-\mu_p\mu_q\bigr)_+=O(1),
\tag{1.4}
\]

because

\[
\sum_{p<q}\mu_p\mu_q\le\frac12\lambda_N^2.
\]

Notice that (1.4) asks only for bounded **aggregate positive correlation**. It does not require every pair to be individually close to independent.

For a Poisson second-factorial-moment asymptotic one would want the stronger statement

\[
\lambda_N\to\lambda,
\qquad
\sum_p\mu_p^2\to0,
\qquad
\sum_{p\ne q}(\mu_{p,q}-\mu_p\mu_q)\to0,
\tag{1.5}
\]

which gives

\[
\mathbb E(B_N)_2\to\lambda^2.
\]

Even (1.5) controls only the first two factorial moments; it does not by itself prove a full Poisson law.

## 2. Why the standard dependency-graph Chen–Stein theorem does not apply

The Arratia–Goldstein–Gordon formulation has, schematically,

\[
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(B_N,\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_N))
\ \lesssim\ b_1+b_2+b_3,
\tag{2.1}
\]

where for chosen neighborhoods \(\mathcal N_p\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
b_1&=\sum_p\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_p}\mu_p\mu_q,\\
b_2&=\sum_p\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal N_p\\q\ne p}}\mu_{p,q},\\
b_3&=\sum_p
\mathbb E\left|
 \mathbb E\left[X_p-\mu_p\mid
 \sigma(X_q:q\notin\mathcal N_p)
 \right]
\right|.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.2}
\]

In a genuine dependency graph, \(X_p\) is jointly independent of all variables outside \(\mathcal N_p\), so \(b_3=0\). That is why dependency-graph statements are often quoted with only \(b_1+b_2\).

Here every \(X_p\) is a deterministic function of the same random integer \(n\). There is no prime-local source of randomness. Consequently:

- the only unconditional exact dependency graph is the complete graph;
- with the complete graph, \(b_1\) is of order \(\lambda_N^2\) and \(b_2\) is the whole second factorial moment, so the bound does not tend to zero;
- taking \(\mathcal N_p=\{p\}\) does not make \(b_3\) vanish. It merely moves all the arithmetic difficulty into \(b_3\).

Therefore there is no natural sparse dependency graph unless one first proves a strong conditional-independence or coupling theorem. That theorem would be an Apéry-specific analogue of the Kubilius model and is not supplied by Lucas or CRT alone.

### Empty neighborhoods would mean genuine mutual independence

Under the standard convention a neighborhood contains its own vertex, so “empty neighborhoods” really means \(\mathcal N_p=\{p\}\), with no other vertices. This is legitimate only when the entire family is mutually independent. In that genuinely independent case, Le Cam/Chen–Stein gives

\[
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
 \left(\sum_pX_p,\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_N)\right)
 \le C\sum_p\mu_p^2,
\tag{2.3}
\]

and \(\sum_p\mu_p^2\to0\) would suffice.

But “all pairs are approximately independent” is not the same as mutual independence, and it is not enough for Poisson convergence.

### Exact pairwise independence can still be very far from Poisson

Let \(m\) be prime, let \(U,V\) be independent uniform elements of \(\mathbf F_m\), and for each \(t\in\mathbf F_m\) define

\[
X_t=\mathbf 1_{Ut+V=0}.
\]

Then

\[
\Pr(X_t=1)=\frac1m
\]

and every distinct pair \(X_s,X_t\) is exactly independent, because the two linear equations have the unique solution \(U=V=0\). Hence

\[
\sum_t\Pr(X_t=1)=1,
\qquad
\sum_t\Pr(X_t=1)^2=\frac1m\to0.
\]

Nevertheless, for \(W_m=\sum_tX_t\),

\[
W_m=
\begin{cases}
1,&U\ne0,\\
0,&U=0,\ V\ne0,\\
m,&U=V=0.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus \(W_m\to1\) in probability, not \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\). In fact

\[
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(W_m,\operatorname{Poisson}(1))
\ge \left|\Pr(W_m=1)-e^{-1}\right|
\to1-e^{-1}.
\]

This example has rare marginals, vanishing \(\sum\mu_p^2\), and exact pairwise independence. The failure is entirely in higher-order dependence. It is precisely why one cannot set the dependency neighborhoods to be empty from pairwise information.

## 3. A qualitative Poisson limit would still not imply the required energy

This is a separate logical obstruction.

Let \(Y_N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\), and modify it with probability \(1/N\) by replacing its value with

\[
M_N:=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{\log N}\right\rfloor.
\]

Call the result \(W_N\). Then

\[
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(W_N,Y_N)\le\frac1N\to0
\]

and even

\[
\mathbb EW_N=\lambda+O(1/\log N)\to\lambda.
\]

But

\[
\mathbb EW_N^2
\ge \frac{M_N^2}{N}
\asymp\frac{N}{\log^2 N}\to\infty.
\]

This is exactly the anchored-star pathology in probabilistic form. If one point \(n_0\) is hit by

\[
L_N\asymp\frac{N}{\log N}
\]

prime columns, then changing the empirical distribution at that one point changes total variation by at most \(1/N\), while adding

\[
L_N^2\asymp\frac{N^2}{\log^2 N}
\]

to the unnormalized energy.

Therefore

\[
B_N\xrightarrow{\mathrm{TV}}\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)
\]

does **not** imply

\[
\sup_N\mathbb E B_N^2<\infty.
\]

One additionally needs uniform integrability, an exponential-tail bound, a moment-sensitive Stein estimate, or a total-variation error so tiny that it survives multiplication by the square of the support. Since

\[
B_N(n)\le \#\mathcal P_N\asymp\frac{N}{\log N},
\]

a bare TV estimate \(\delta_N\) controls the second-moment error only at the scale

\[
\delta_N\frac{N^2}{\log^2 N}.
\]

To obtain an \(O(1)\) normalized second moment from that argument alone would require roughly

\[
\delta_N\ll\frac{\log^2 N}{N^2},
\]

far stronger than an ordinary qualitative Poisson approximation.

For P3.2, this is another reason to attack (1.2) directly.

## 4. The suffix/suffix CRT calculation: the claimed error is not small enough

Split the Lucas event into

\[
E_p^{(0)}:=\{n\le N:n\bmod p\in Z_p\}
\]

and

\[
E_p^{(1)}:=\{n\le N:\lfloor n/p\rfloor\in Z_p\}.
\]

The suffix/suffix intersection is \(E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(0)}\).

For \(p\ne q\) in \(\mathcal P_N\), one has \(pq>N\). For each \((a,b)\in Z_p\times Z_q\), let

\[
c_{p,q}(a,b)\in\{0,1,\ldots,pq-1\}
\]

be its CRT representative. Then exactly

\[
A_{p,q}^{00}
:=|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(0)}|
=\sum_{a\in Z_p}\sum_{b\in Z_q}
\mathbf 1_{c_{p,q}(a,b)\le N}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

The random-CRT main term is

\[
\frac{(N+1)z_pz_q}{pq}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

If

\[
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}\frac{z_p}{p}=O(1),
\tag{4.3}
\]

then the total main term is already of the desired size:

\[
\sum_{p<q}\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}
\le\frac N2
\left(\sum_p\frac{z_p}{p}\right)^2
=O(N).
\tag{4.4}
\]

The problem is the discrepancy.

For one pair, the trivial bound is

\[
\left|
A_{p,q}^{00}-\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}
\right|
\le z_pz_q.
\tag{4.5}
\]

After division by \(N\), this is the \(z_pz_q/N\) error quoted in the question. But summing over all prime pairs gives, when the average \(z_p\) is bounded,

\[
\frac1N\sum_{p<q}z_pz_q
\asymp
\frac1N\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right)^2
=\frac{N}{\log^2 N}.
\tag{4.6}
\]

This is not an acceptable error for the normalized second moment. The required comparison is

\[
\sum_{p<q}\mu_{p,q}=O(1),
\]

not merely \(o(N)\). In unnormalized form, (4.6) corresponds to

\[
O\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2 N}\right),
\]

which is exactly the anchored-star scale.

So the sentence

> “the total error is \(O(N/\log^2N)=o(N)\), hence acceptable”

uses the wrong normalization. One needs an **aggregate** estimate such as

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}A_{p,q}^{00}=O(N),
}
\tag{4.7}
\]

or, relative to the random main term, a one-sided discrepancy bound

\[
\sum_{p<q}
\left(
A_{p,q}^{00}-\frac{Nz_pz_q}{pq}
\right)_+
=O(N).
\tag{4.8}
\]

The fact that each CRT class has at most one representative does not prove (4.7). It merely turns the problem into the sparse incidence count (4.1).

There is also a conceptual point. For \(p,q\asymp N\) and singleton zero sets, the joint probability is either \(0\) or about \(1/N\), while the independent benchmark is about \(1/N^2\). Thus individual pairwise approximation is not even the right shape. The desired phenomenon is that only about the correct sparse proportion of prime pairs have a low CRT representative. It is a global dispersion statement, not uniform closeness for every pair.

## 5. The leading digit: exact one-prime formula and why it is a new obstacle

For \(p\in\mathcal P_N\) and \(0\le n\le N\), write

\[
n=ap+r,
\qquad
0\le r<p.
\]

Since \(p>\sqrt N\), one has \(a<p\), and Lucas gives

\[
p\mid b_n
\iff
a\in Z_p\quad\text{or}\quad r\in Z_p.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Put

\[
Q_p:=\left\lfloor\frac Np\right\rfloor,
\qquad
s_p:=N-Q_pp,
\qquad 0\le s_p<p.
\]

Define

\[
\begin{aligned}
t_p&:=|Z_p\cap\{0,1,\ldots,Q_p-1\}|,\\
u_p&:=|Z_p\cap\{0,1,\ldots,s_p\}|,\\
\delta_p&:=\mathbf 1_{Q_p\in Z_p}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Then the suffix event has exact size

\[
|E_p^{(0)}|=Q_pz_p+u_p,
\tag{5.2}
\]

the leading-digit event has exact size

\[
|E_p^{(1)}|=pt_p+(s_p+1)\delta_p,
\tag{5.3}
\]

and their overlap has exact size

\[
|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_p^{(1)}|=z_pt_p+u_p\delta_p.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
|E_p|
=Q_pz_p+u_p+(p-z_p)t_p
 +\delta_p\bigl((s_p+1)-u_p\bigr).
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

This is the precise version of the complete-block formula in the question.

The main lesson is that a low root contributes with weight \(p\). A simple upper bound is

\[
|E_p|
\le \frac{Nz_p}{p}+z_p
   +p\,|Z_p\cap[0,Q_p]|.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Therefore a bounded suffix intensity

\[
\sum_p\frac{z_p}{p}=O(1)
\]

does not by itself even prove \(\mathbb EB_N=O(1)\). One also needs the weighted low-root estimate

\[
\boxed{
\frac1N
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}
 p\,|Z_p\cap[0,\lfloor N/p\rfloor]|
=O(1).
}
\tag{5.7}
\]

This is already an Apéry prime-divisor problem at the smaller indices \(a\le\sqrt N\), since

\[
a\in Z_p\iff p\mid b_a.
\]

Equivalently, the leading-digit mass can be written as the weighted divisor incidence

\[
\sum_{p}|E_p^{(1)}|
=
\sum_{a\le\sqrt N}
\sum_{\substack{p\in\mathcal P_N\\p\mid b_a}}
\left|[ap,(a+1)p-1]\cap[0,N]\right|.
\tag{5.8}
\]

This is not a standard independent-residue sum.

### A useful unconditional restriction on the leading-digit channel

A leading-digit hit cannot occur for the very largest primes. Indeed, if

\[
a\le N/p,
\qquad
p\mid b_a,
\]

then \(p\le b_a\). The elementary exponential bound

\[
b_a\le(a+1)64^a
\]

gives

\[
\log p\le a\log64+\log(a+1)
\le\frac{N\log64}{p}+O(\log N).
\]

More directly, if \(p>C N/\log N\), then \(a<N/p<(\log N)/C\), and for sufficiently large fixed \(C\) the bound above gives \(b_a<p\), a contradiction. Hence

\[
\boxed{
E_p^{(1)}=\varnothing
\quad\text{for }p\gg N/\log N.
}
\tag{5.9}
\]

Thus the topmost prime range is suffix-only. This is a genuine simplification, but it leaves the large interval

\[
\sqrt N<p\ll N/\log N,
\]

where the leading digit remains a serious issue.

## 6. The exact four-channel criterion

Set

\[
X_p^{(0)}:=\mathbf 1_{E_p^{(0)}},
\qquad
X_p^{(1)}:=\mathbf 1_{E_p^{(1)}},
\qquad
Y_p:=X_p^{(0)}+X_p^{(1)}.
\]

Since \(X_p=\mathbf 1_{E_p^{(0)}\cup E_p^{(1)}}\), pointwise

\[
X_p\le Y_p\le2X_p.
\]

Writing

\[
Y_N(n):=\sum_pY_p(n),
\]

we get

\[
B_N(n)\le Y_N(n)\le2B_N(n),
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N)
\iff
\sum_{n\le N}Y_N(n)^2=O(N).
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Expanding the latter gives the exact nonnegative decomposition

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{n\le N}Y_N(n)^2
={}&
\sum_p\Bigl(
 |E_p^{(0)}|+|E_p^{(1)}|
 +2|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_p^{(1)}|
\Bigr)\\
&+2\sum_{p<q}\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
 |E_p^{(i)}\cap E_q^{(j)}|.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.2}
\]

All terms are nonnegative. Consequently the following is an exact necessary-and-sufficient criterion up to the harmless factor \(4\) introduced by replacing an OR by a sum.

### Four-channel energy criterion

One has

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N)
\]

if and only if

\[
\sum_p\bigl(|E_p^{(0)}|+|E_p^{(1)}|\bigr)=O(N)
\tag{6.3}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}
\left(
 |E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(0)}|
+|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(1)}|
+|E_p^{(1)}\cap E_q^{(0)}|
+|E_p^{(1)}\cap E_q^{(1)}|
\right)
=O(N).
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

The four terms are:

1. **suffix/suffix** — the CRT incidence (4.1);
2. **suffix/leading** — a residue class modulo \(p\) intersecting a \(q\)-block;
3. **leading/suffix** — the symmetric mixed channel;
4. **leading/leading** — overlap of two root-indexed intervals.

The mixed channel has the explicit form

\[
\begin{aligned}
|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(1)}|
={}&
\sum_{r\in Z_p}
\sum_{\substack{a\in Z_q\\aq\le N}}
\#\Bigl(
 [aq,\min((a+1)q-1,N)]
 \cap(r+p\mathbf Z)
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.5}
\]

Unlike the suffix/suffix channel, the interval can contain several representatives of a residue class when \(q>p\).

The leading/leading channel is

\[
\begin{aligned}
|E_p^{(1)}\cap E_q^{(1)}|
={}&
\sum_{\substack{a\in Z_p\\ap\le N}}
\sum_{\substack{b\in Z_q\\bq\le N}}
\left|
 [ap,(a+1)p-1]
 \cap[bq,(b+1)q-1]
 \cap[0,N]
\right|.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.6}
\]

An individual overlap in (6.6) can have length comparable to \(\min(p,q)\). Thus the digit-1/digit-1 term is qualitatively different from a CRT class with at most one representative.

Equations (6.3)–(6.4) are the clean answer to “how should the second digit be handled?” Split it off and prove a four-channel incidence theorem. There is no standard independence lemma that removes these terms.

## 7. The weakest condition on the family \(Z_p\)

There are two useful answers, one exact and one in decorrelation language.

### Exact answer

Define

\[
E_p(Z_p)
=
\left\{
0\le n\le N:
 n\bmod p\in Z_p
 \text{ or }
 \lfloor n/p\rfloor\in Z_p
\right\}.
\]

Then the weakest possible condition is simply

\[
\boxed{
\sum_p|E_p(Z_p)|
+2\sum_{p<q}|E_p(Z_p)\cap E_q(Z_q)|
=O(N).
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

This is necessary and sufficient by (1.2). The atomized, structurally usable version is (6.3)–(6.4).

### Decorrelation answer

Let \(\mu_p=|E_p|/(N+1)\) and \(\mu_{p,q}=|E_p\cap E_q|/(N+1)\). A convenient sufficient condition is

\[
\sum_p\mu_p=O(1)
\tag{7.2}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}
(\mu_{p,q}-\mu_p\mu_q)_+=O(1).
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

This says that the total positive covariance mass is bounded. It is much weaker than pointwise approximate independence and is exactly calibrated to the energy.

### No primewise property can be sufficient

The distinguishing property cannot be only a condition imposed separately on each \(Z_p\), such as:

- \(|Z_p|=O(1)\);
- reflection symmetry;
- no consecutive roots;
- interval discrepancy within one \(\mathbf F_p\);
- squarefreeness of an associated polynomial;
- bounded internal additive energy.

The anchored star

\[
Z_p^*=\{N\bmod p\}
\]

has \(|Z_p^*|=1\), yet \(N\in E_p(Z_p^*)\) for every \(p\in\mathcal P_N\). Therefore

\[
B_N(N)=\#\mathcal P_N\asymp\frac{N}{\log N}
\]

and

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
\ge B_N(N)^2
\asymp\frac{N^2}{\log^2 N}.
\tag{7.4}
\]

The bad feature is **horizontal alignment across characteristics**: many different prime columns pass through the same integer anchor. Any successful condition must therefore involve the family \((Z_p)_p\) jointly and the moving CRT/block incidences. This is exactly the star-falsified “anti-anchoring” or cross-prime dispersion input already isolated in the project.

A stronger but simpler sufficient condition is

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)=O(N),
\qquad
\max_{n\le N}B_N(n)=O(1),
\]

because then

\[
\sum_nB_N(n)^2
\le \max_nB_N(n)\sum_nB_N(n)=O(N).
\]

For closing P3.2 one does not need the maximum to be \(O(1)\). If

\[
\sum_nB_N(n)=N^{1+o(1)},
\qquad
\max_nB_N(n)=N^{o(1)},
\]

then

\[
\sum_nB_N(n)^2=N^{1+o(1)},
\]

which is already far below \(N^2/\log^2N\). This connects directly with the project’s atom-tail/maximum-load avenue.

## 8. Number-theoretic precedents and the precise limitation of the analogy

There are strong precedents for Poisson approximation in probabilistic number theory, but they do not automatically transfer here.

### The classical Kubilius model

For a uniformly random integer \(m\le x\), small-prime divisibility indicators are modeled by independent Bernoulli variables with parameters \(1/p\). Sieve estimates or the Kubilius model supply the required transference from the actual divisibility vector to the independent model. Once that transference is proved, Le Cam or Chen–Stein gives Poisson laws for suitable prime sets.

Kevin Ford’s paper *Joint Poisson distribution of prime factors in sets* proves quantitative joint Poisson approximation for sets of “not too large” primes. His later paper *Poisson Approximation of Prime Divisors of Shifted Primes* develops a Kubilius-type model for shifted primes, but the core work is precisely a new total-variation/sieve transference theorem.

This is the correct precedent conceptually:

> First prove that the arithmetic incidence vector is close to an independent Bernoulli model; then apply Poisson approximation.

For Apéry numbers, the first step is presently missing. Lucas gives the form of the incidence vector but not the transference estimate.

### In the exact window \((\sqrt N,N]\), ordinary integers are not Poisson

There is an especially relevant warning. For an ordinary integer \(m\le N\),

\[
\omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(m)\in\{0,1\},
\]

because two primes greater than \(\sqrt N\) have product greater than \(N\). Moreover,

\[
\mathbb E\omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(m)
=\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac{\lfloor N/p\rfloor}{N}
\to\log2.
\]

Hence

\[
\omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(m)
\Longrightarrow \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\log2),
\]

not \(\operatorname{Poisson}(\log2)\).

Thus the fact that \(pq>N\) does not support independence; for ordinary divisibility it produces the strongest possible negative dependence. Apéry values are much larger than \(N\), so multiple such primes may divide \(b_n\), but their joint distribution has to be proved from Apéry-specific structure.

### The natural Poisson route is factorial moments, not a dependency graph

If one ultimately wants a Poisson theorem, the clean arithmetic formulation is Brun’s/factorial-moment method. For every fixed \(k\), prove

\[
\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\in\mathcal P_N\\\text{distinct}}}
\frac1{N+1}
\left|
E_{p_1}\cap\cdots\cap E_{p_k}
\right|
\longrightarrow \lambda^k.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Together with suitable tightness/uniform-integrability control, this yields a Poisson law. For the energy, however, only \(k=1,2\) and boundedness—not asymptotic equality—are needed. Therefore proving all of (8.1) would be substantial overkill for P3.2.

## 9. Can one prove a direct \(B_N(n)\le C\) statement for generic \(n\)?

There are three different interpretations.

### Fixed \(n\), with \(N\to\infty\)

This is trivial and irrelevant: since \(b_n\) is fixed, eventually every prime in \((\sqrt N,N]\) exceeds \(b_n\), so \(B_N(n)=0\).

### Uniformly for all \(n\le N\)

The elementary product bound gives only

\[
\prod_{\substack{\sqrt N<p\le N\\p\mid b_n}}p\le b_n.
\]

Using

\[
\log b_n=\kappa n+O(\log n),
\qquad
\kappa=\log(17+12\sqrt2),
\]

one obtains

\[
\boxed{
B_N(n)\le
\frac{2\log b_n}{\log N}
\le
(2\kappa+o(1))\frac{n}{\log N}.
}
\tag{9.1}
\]

For \(n\asymp N\), this is \(O(N/\log N)\), not \(O(1)\). No unconditional direct constant bound is currently available.

### Outside an exceptional set

If the desired energy estimate were known,

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2\le CN,
\]

then Markov gives

\[
\#\{n\le N:B_N(n)>T\}
\le\frac{CN}{T^2}.
\tag{9.2}
\]

Thus for every function \(T(N)\to\infty\), one would have

\[
B_N(n)\le T(N)
\]

for a density-one set of \(n\). But (9.2) does not give a fixed constant on a density-one set.

In fact a nondegenerate Poisson heuristic predicts that no fixed constant works on a density-one set. If

\[
B_N\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda),
\qquad \lambda>0,
\]

then for each fixed \(C\),

\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}
\frac1N\#\{n\le N:B_N(n)>C\}
=\Pr(\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)>C)>0.
\]

What Poisson predicts is instead:

- for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a constant \(C_\varepsilon\) such that at least \((1-\varepsilon)N\) indices satisfy \(B_N(n)\le C_\varepsilon\);
- the maximum should grow slowly, heuristically around \(\log N/\log\log N\) under an independent-sample model, not remain bounded.

So a universal generic constant is neither necessary nor the natural Poisson prediction.

## 10. Direct answers to the six questions

### 1. Can Chen–Stein be applied, and what is the natural dependency graph?

Only after proving an additional Apéry-specific dependence theorem. The natural exact dependency graph is complete, because all \(X_p\) are functions of the same \(n\), and no joint independence outside sparse neighborhoods is known. The complete-graph Chen–Stein bound is useless. A nonlocal Stein coupling or a Kubilius-type transference theorem is possible in principle, but proving it would require control of the same cross-prime incidences that appear in the second moment.

### 2. Do empty dependency neighborhoods immediately imply Poisson convergence?

Only under genuine mutual independence. Pairwise near-independence is insufficient, and the omitted \(b_3\) term records the remaining dependence. Even exact pairwise independence plus \(\sum\mu_p^2\to0\) does not imply Poisson, as the finite-field example in Section 2 shows. Moreover, standard total-variation Poisson convergence alone would not imply the energy bound without uniform integrability.

### 3. Does the second digit break near-independence?

Yes. It changes both the marginal and pair problems. A low root creates an interval of length about \(p\), so one needs the weighted low-root estimate (5.7). At second order it produces the three additional channels in (6.4), and the leading/leading intersection can contain \(\asymp\min(p,q)\) integers. The standard treatment is to split into the two digit events and estimate all four channels explicitly; there is no automatic independence theorem.

### 4. Are there number-theoretic precedents?

Yes: the Kubilius model, Brun’s method, Chen–Stein for prime-factor indicators, Ford’s joint Poisson theorem for prime factors in suitable sets, and Ford’s shifted-prime transference theorem. But the key input in each case is an arithmetic approximation of the actual divisor vector by an independent model. In the literal large-prime window \((\sqrt N,N]\), ordinary integer divisibility is Bernoulli rather than Poisson, so this window is precisely where naive Kubilius independence can fail.

### 5. What is the weakest condition on \(Z_p\) sufficient for \(\sum B_N^2=O(N)\)?

The exact answer is (7.1), equivalently the one-prime condition (6.3) plus the four-channel pair condition (6.4). In near-independence language, bounded mean together with bounded aggregate positive covariance (7.3) suffices. No condition on the individual sets \(Z_p\) alone can work; one needs a horizontal, cross-prime anti-anchoring condition.

### 6. Can one prove \(B_N(n)\le C\) for generic \(n\)?

Not presently, and a fixed \(C\) on a density-one set is contrary to a nondegenerate Poisson limit. The unconditional pointwise bound is (9.1). An \(O(N)\) energy bound would give the density estimate (9.2), hence \(B_N(n)\le T(N)\) for almost all \(n\) for any slowly growing \(T(N)\), but not a fixed constant.

## 11. Recommended P3.2 target

Do not make Poisson approximation the next theorem. Isolate the following two statements.

```text
LPD1(N):
  sum_{sqrt(N) < p <= N} #{n <= N : p divides b_n} = O(N).
```

After the exact Lucas split, this includes the nontrivial weighted low-root term (5.7).

```text
LPD2_ij(N), i,j in {0,1}:
  sum_{sqrt(N) < p < q <= N}
    #{n <= N : digit-channel i hits at p
             and digit-channel j hits at q}
  = O(N).
```

Summing the four \(LPD2_{ij}\) statements and using (6.2) gives

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N).
\]

For the original GCD theorem, even this is much stronger than necessary. It is enough to prove

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
=o\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right),
\]

so any fixed power saving over the anchored-star scale would close the argument. A realistic progression is therefore:

1. prove the one-prime weighted low-root bound;
2. prove a suffix/suffix aggregate CRT-dispersion estimate;
3. treat the two mixed channels by sorting the small leading digit \(\lfloor n/p\rfloor\);
4. treat leading/leading as an interval-overlap problem for large prime divisors of the smaller Apéry values;
5. only after these are available, investigate whether higher factorial moments give a genuine Poisson theorem.

The empirical Poisson law is valuable evidence for these incidence estimates, but Chen–Stein does not bypass them.
