# Expansion in the Apéry cocycle: exact verdict

## Executive conclusion

The proposed chain is **not complete**, and the failure is not merely that one technical hypothesis of Bourgain--Gamburd or Varjú remains unchecked. There are several logically independent breaks.

```text
fixed generators generate a large group
        does not imply
one prescribed non-autonomous word equidistributes;

marginal equidistribution of v_n
        does not imply
few intersections with the moving target R(n);

few roots of b_{n+1}-b_n in F_p
        does not imply
finite-field stationary phase with bounded conductor;

|S_c| << sqrt(p) for every c != 0
        implies only
|Z_p| << sqrt(p), not |Z_p| = O(1).
```

There is nevertheless one useful positive statement. After correcting from `SL_2` to projective linear groups, the first two nonsingular transfer matrices already generate a Zariski-dense subgroup of `PGL_2(Q)`. Therefore the **random walk obtained by choosing these fixed matrices and their inverses randomly** is covered, outside finitely many primes, by superstrong approximation/expansion theory. That random walk is not the Apéry orbit.

The repository state at public `main` commit

```text
f914983116049a10ced0844172d7e7f99a0eb238
```

already isolates the same non-autonomous obstruction in

- `problems/3.2/campaign3_questions/q11_fammech.txt`,
- `drops/Q6543-3b0405d8.md`, and
- `drops/Q7231-dbbed013.md`.

The present calculation sharpens that audit and answers (a)--(e) directly.

## 1. The exact projective critical-point equation

Write

\[
P(n)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

The Apéry recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}.
\]

Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
(n+1)^3(b_{n+1}-b_n)
 &=\bigl(P(n)-(n+1)^3\bigr)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}\\
 &=D(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1},
\end{aligned}
\]

where

\[
D(n)=33n^3+48n^2+24n+4.
\]

For \(1\le n\le p-2\), the factor \((n+1)^3\) is a unit, so

\[
b_{n+1}=b_n
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
D(n)b_n=n^3b_{n-1}.
\]

The correct formulation does not divide by either \(b_{n-1}\) or \(D(n)\). Put

\[
v_n=[b_n:b_{n-1}]\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p),
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{crit}}(n)=[n^3:D(n)].
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
 b_{n+1}=b_n
 \iff
 v_n=R_{\mathrm{crit}}(n)
}
\qquad(1\le n\le p-2).
\]

The no-consecutive-zeros identity ensures that \([b_n:b_{n-1}]\) is always a projective point for \(p>3\).

The projective update is represented by

\[
\widetilde T_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)&-n^3\\
(n+1)^3&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_{n+1}=\widetilde T_n v_n.
\]

Equivalently, after scaling by \((n+1)^{-3}\), this is the matrix in the question.

Two endpoint singularities are important:

\[
\det \widetilde T_n=n^3(n+1)^3.
\]

Thus \(\widetilde T_0\) is singular, and modulo \(p\) the step \(n=p-1\) is also singular. The invertible projective cocycle exists on the nonwrapping range \(1\le n\le p-2\), not around a complete cyclic clock.

## 2. Question (a): do the first matrices generate \(SL_2(\mathbf F_p)\)?

### 2.1 As written, no

For the normalized matrix

\[
T_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(n)/(n+1)^3&-n^3/(n+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]

one has

\[
\det T_n=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^3.
\]

Therefore:

1. \(T_0\) is singular;
2. for \(n\ge1\), \(T_n\) is generally in \(GL_2\), not \(SL_2\);
3. its projective class may lie in either coset of
   \(PSL_2(\mathbf F_p)\subset PGL_2(\mathbf F_p)\), according to the square class of its determinant.

So the literal statement

\[
\langle T_0,T_1,\ldots,T_k\rangle=SL_2(\mathbf F_p)
\]

is not well-formed.

The corrected finite-group question is

\[
G_{p,k}:=\langle [T_1],\ldots,[T_k]\rangle
\le PGL_2(\mathbf F_p),
\]

and one should ask whether

\[
G_{p,k}\supseteq PSL_2(\mathbf F_p).
\]

Then \(G_{p,k}\) is either \(PSL_2(\mathbf F_p)\) or \(PGL_2(\mathbf F_p)\).

### 2.2 A positive calculation: \([T_1]\) and \([T_2]\) are already Zariski-dense over \(\mathbf Q\)

Scale the first two matrices projectively to

\[
A_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
117&-1\\
8&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
535&-8\\
27&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

A direct multiplication gives

\[
A_2A_1^{-1}
\sim
U:=
\begin{pmatrix}
64&-401\\
0&27
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The eigenvalue ratio of \(U\) is \(64/27\), so \(U\) has infinite order over characteristic zero. Its two fixed points on \(\mathbf P^1\) are

\[
\infty,
\qquad
\frac{401}{37}.
\]

But

\[
A_1(\infty)=\frac{117}{8}
\notin
\left\{\infty,\frac{401}{37}\right\},
\]

and \(401/37\) is not fixed by \(A_1\), since

\[
8\left(\frac{401}{37}\right)^2
-117\left(\frac{401}{37}\right)+1
=-\frac{448152}{1369}\ne0.
\]

Consequently \(\langle[A_1],[A_2]\rangle\) is

- not finite, because it contains \(U\) of infinite order;
- not contained in a Borel, because there is no common fixed point;
- not contained in the normalizer of a torus, because \(A_1\) does not preserve the fixed pair of \(U\).

These exhaust the proper infinite algebraic subgroups of \(PGL_2\) in characteristic zero. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\langle[T_1],[T_2]\rangle
\text{ is Zariski-dense in }PGL_2.
}
\]

By strong/superstrong approximation, its reductions contain
\(PSL_2(\mathbf F_p)\) for all but finitely many primes. Depending on the determinant square classes, the image is then \(PSL_2\) or \(PGL_2\).

What this does **not** prove is the exact statement “for every prime \(p\ge7\).” Proving that version requires determining the finite exceptional set and checking it individually. The repository's small-prime generation experiments are evidence, not an all-prime proof.

Most importantly, even a proof of exact generation for every \(p\ge7\) would not imply equidistribution of the Apéry orbit. Generation is not the missing step.

## 3. Question (b): is there a uniform spectral gap?

There are two different operators here, and they have opposite answers.

### 3.1 The artificial Cayley random-walk operator: yes, outside finitely many primes

For the fixed symmetric set

\[
S=\{[T_1],[T_1]^{-1},[T_2],[T_2]^{-1}\},
\]

define

\[
(\mathcal A_pf)(x)=\frac14\sum_{s\in S}f(s^{-1}x),
\qquad x\in\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p).
\]

The Zariski-density calculation above places this fixed-generator random walk in the Bourgain--Gamburd / Bourgain--Varjú / Salehi Golsefidy--Varjú framework. After excluding finitely many primes, \(\mathcal A_p\) has a spectral gap bounded below independently of \(p\), both on the group and on the quotient \(\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)\).

This says that a product

\[
s_m\cdots s_1,
\qquad s_i\text{ chosen randomly from }S,
\]

has an almost-uniform endpoint after \(O(\log p)\) steps.

### 3.2 The actual deterministic Apéry transfer operator: no

The Apéry orbit uses the prescribed ordered product

\[
T_{n-1}T_{n-2}\cdots T_1,
\]

with the time-dependent matrix \(T_j\) forced at time \(j\). There is no random choice among a fixed set.

On an invertible time interval, the natural skew-product map is

\[
F(n,x)=(n+1,T_nx).
\]

Its Koopman/transfer operator is composition by a deterministic bijection:

\[
(\mathcal U f)(n,x)=f(F^{-1}(n,x)).
\]

Therefore

\[
\|\mathcal U f\|_2=\|f\|_2.
\]

Every singular value is \(1\); on the mean-zero space there is no contraction. In the usual spectral-gap normalization,

\[
\boxed{\text{the relevant deterministic operator has gap }0.}
\]

The singular steps at \(n=0,-1\pmod p\) do not repair this. They instead prevent one from defining an invertible full-cycle monodromy, exactly as noted in the repository's transfer-word audit.

One could average the matrices over time,

\[
\frac1{p-2}\sum_{n=1}^{p-2}\rho(T_n),
\]

or randomize the order in which they are applied. Such an average may have a gap, but it is a different stochastic process. It does not control the single ordered word \(T_{n-1}\cdots T_1\).

A simple general warning is enough: an expander generating set permits prescribed words such as

\[
s,s^{-1},s,s^{-1},\ldots
\]

whose deterministic partial products remain in a two-point set. Expansion concerns the distribution over many random words, not every individual word.

## 4. Question (c): does Varjú's 2012 theorem apply?

No.

The relevant 2012 Varjú theorem is of the following form:

> Fix a finite symmetric subset \(S\) of an arithmetic linear group, and assume that the subgroup generated by \(S\) is Zariski-dense. Then the Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients with respect to the reductions of this same fixed \(S\) form an expander family under the stated modulus hypotheses.

Likewise, Bourgain--Varjú treats convolution powers associated with a fixed finite generating set.

These are the hypotheses of a random walk:

\[
\mu^{*m},
\qquad
\mu=\frac1{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}\delta_s.
\]

They do not treat a deterministic non-autonomous product

\[
T_{m-1}(u+m-1)\cdots T_1(u+1)
\]

whose factor at time \(j\) is prescribed by a polynomial function of the clock.

I could not identify a Varjú theorem asserting that polynomially varying **deterministic** matrix products equidistribute merely from non-degeneracy of their entries. The statement in the question appears to conflate congruence expansion for a fixed generating set with a theorem about ordered polynomial cocycles.

A genuinely relevant new estimate would look more like

\[
G_\ell(m)=T_{m+\ell-1}\cdots T_m,
\]

\[
\boxed{
\sup_{\rho\ne1}
\left\|
\frac1p\sum_{m\in\mathbf F_p}\rho(G_\ell(m))
\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
\le p^{-\eta}
}
\]

for a useful range of block lengths \(\ell\), uniformly over nontrivial representations \(\rho\). This is an ordered-word flattening theorem for the cocycle. It is not a consequence of the cited expansion results, and even this averaged-in-\(m\) statement would still require an additional argument to control the single orbit starting at the Apéry initial vector.

## 5. What the empirical “64% distinct” statistic actually says

A length-\(p\) sequence of independent uniform samples from a set of approximately \(p\) points visits approximately

\[
(1-e^{-1})p
\]

distinct points. Thus the observed \(64\%\) is a strong indication that the multiplicity profile resembles a random occupancy model.

It is not the usual conclusion called equidistribution in an expander theorem.

A spectral-gap theorem says that the **law of a random endpoint** approaches uniform measure. The occupancy statistic concerns the whole path, and the maximum multiplicity concerns high-order return correlations along that path. Even for a genuinely mixing random chain, the number of visits to a specified point has a distribution with unbounded support. A fixed universal maximum such as \(5\) is not a consequence of Poisson behavior.

Thus the empirical statement is valuable, but the correct theoretical target is a family of collision estimates, not merely a one-step or endpoint spectral gap.

## 6. Question (d): does equidistribution imply only \(O(1)\) solutions of \(v_n=R(n)\)?

No. Marginal equidistribution and moving-target incidence are different properties.

### 6.1 Perfect marginal equidistribution can coexist with \(p\) moving-target hits

Take

\[
v_n=n\in\mathbf F_p,
\qquad
R(n)=n.
\]

Then every value of \(v_n\) occurs exactly once: this is perfect marginal equidistribution and maximum multiplicity \(1\). Nevertheless

\[
\#\{n:v_n=R(n)\}=p.
\]

More generally, if \(R\) is a separable rational map of degree \(d\), the sequence

\[
v_n=R(n)
\]

has fibers of size at most \(d\) away from exceptional points, yet it intersects the graph of \(R\) at every time.

Therefore neither

- many distinct values of \(v_n\), nor
- a bounded multiplicity for each fixed value of \(v_n\)

controls the correlation between \(v_n\) and the clock \(n\).

### 6.2 A Poisson heuristic does not yield a deterministic universal constant

If the events \(v_n=R(n)\) were independent with probability \(1/p\), their total would be approximately Poisson with mean \(1\). A Poisson random variable has unbounded support. For every fixed \(D\), the probability of exceeding \(D\) is small but positive.

Thus the heuristic predicts a tight distribution, not a theorem of the form

\[
\#\{n:v_n=R(n)\}\le D
\quad\text{for every prime }p.
\]

### 6.3 The required estimate is a graph-correlation theorem

One needs direct control of the orbit graph

\[
\Gamma_v=\{(n,v_n):1\le n\le p-2\}
\subset \mathbf F_p\times\mathbf P^1(\mathbf F_p)
\]

against

\[
\Gamma_R=\{(n,R(n)):n\in\mathbf F_p\}.
\]

For the critical target, the required statement is

\[
\boxed{
\#(\Gamma_v\cap\Gamma_{R_{\mathrm{crit}}})=O(1).
}
\]

This is a two-coordinate incidence/rigidity theorem. It does not follow from the projection of \(\Gamma_v\) onto the second coordinate being uniform.

If \(\Gamma_v\) were known to lie on a fixed algebraic curve of bounded degree different from \(\Gamma_R\), Bézout would give such a result. The direct transfer word does not supply a bounded-degree curve: its algebraic degree and puncture divisor grow with the word length, as documented in `drops/Q7231-dbbed013.md`.

## 7. Question (e), first additional break: bounded discrete critical points do not imply stationary phase

The observed condition is

\[
\#\{n:b_{n+1}=b_n\}=O(1).
\]

This counts zeros of the **finite difference** of the value sequence. Standard finite-field stationary phase for a polynomial or rational phase is controlled by the geometric ramification/conductor of the associated Artin--Schreier sheaf, equivalently by critical points over the algebraic closure with their multiplicities and behavior at poles. It is not controlled merely by the number of \(\mathbf F_p\)-zeros of one finite difference.

There is an elementary counterexample tailored exactly to interpolation.

For odd \(p\), define a function on the standard representatives \(0,1,\ldots,p-1\) by

\[
f(n)=
\begin{cases}
0,&n\text{ even},\\
1,&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\]

There is a unique interpolation polynomial \(F_p(X)\in\mathbf F_p[X]\) of degree at most \(p-1\) with these values. Consecutive values alternate, so

\[
\#\{0\le n\le p-2:f(n+1)=f(n)\}=0
\]

(and including the cyclic wrap creates only one equality). Yet for the standard additive character \(e_p(t)=e^{2\pi it/p}\),

\[
\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}e_p(f(n))
=\frac{p+1}{2}+\frac{p-1}{2}e_p(1),
\]

whose magnitude is at least

\[
p\cos(\pi/p)\asymp p.
\]

Thus even **zero** discrete critical points are compatible with a character sum of linear size.

A second standard warning concerns formal derivatives. The polynomial

\[
F(X)=X^{p-1}
\]

has only one distinct critical point in \(\mathbf F_p\), since

\[
F'(X)=-X^{p-2},
\]

but

\[
\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(cF(x))
=1+(p-1)e_p(c),
\]

again of size \(\asymp p\). The missing datum is the enormous critical multiplicity/conductor.

Therefore a bounded count of roots of \(b_{n+1}-b_n\) cannot by itself justify

\[
|S_c|=O(\sqrt p).
\]

One would need a uniform bounded-conductor geometric realization of the phase, or a new discrete oscillation theorem exploiting much more than the zero set of the first difference.

## 8. Question (e), second additional break: square-root sums do not imply bounded zeros

Let

\[
S_c=\sum_{n\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(cb_n)
\]

and

\[
Z_p=\{n\in\mathbf F_p:b_n=0\}.
\]

Fourier inversion gives the exact identity

\[
|Z_p|
=\frac1p\sum_{c\in\mathbf F_p}S_c
=1+\frac1p\sum_{c\ne0}S_c.
\]

If one proves only

\[
|S_c|\le C\sqrt p
\qquad(c\ne0),
\]

then the triangle inequality yields

\[
|Z_p|
\le1+C\frac{p-1}{\sqrt p}
=O(\sqrt p).
\]

So the asserted implication

\[
|S_c|=O(\sqrt p)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|Z_p|=O(1)
\]

is false.

The same point appears through Plancherel. If

\[
N_a=\#\{n:b_n=a\},
\]

then

\[
\sum_{a\in\mathbf F_p}N_a^2
=\frac1p\sum_{c\in\mathbf F_p}|S_c|^2.
\]

A uniform square-root bound gives only

\[
\sum_aN_a^2=O(p),
\]

which permits an individual fiber of size \(O(\sqrt p)\).

To obtain \(|Z_p|=O(1)\), one needs one of the following genuinely stronger inputs:

1. a direct bounded-fiber theorem for the value \(0\);
2. cancellation in the sum over \(c\), much stronger than the pointwise bound;
3. bounded algebraic degree of the zero fiber in a uniform model;
4. sufficiently strong all-order collision estimates.

There is also no finite-field Rolle theorem connecting the two desired conclusions. The alternating example above has about \(p/2\) zeros and no equal adjacent values. Hence bounded critical points do not directly bound the zero fiber either.

## 9. The exact status of the expansion strategy

The strongest correct statement obtainable from the first matrices is:

> The fixed projective generators \([T_1],[T_2]\) are Zariski-dense, and the random Cayley walks generated by them form an expander family outside finitely many primes.

This is useful as a heuristic explanation for why random words in the same ambient group look uniform. It proves none of the following:

\[
\frac1p\sum_{n=1}^{p-2}\delta_{v_n}
\approx \text{uniform measure},
\]

\[
\#\{n:v_n=R(n)\}=O(1),
\]

or

\[
|Z_p|=O(1).
\]

To turn expansion into a theorem about the Apéry sequence, a new result must use the arithmetic order of the matrices. A plausible first target is an averaged block-flattening estimate for

\[
G_\ell(m)=T_{m+\ell-1}\cdots T_m.
\]

But the actual critical-point theorem requires the still stronger moving-target statement

\[
\left|
\sum_{n=1}^{p-2}
\left(
\mathbf 1_{\{v_n=R_{\mathrm{crit}}(n)\}}
-\frac1{p+1}
\right)
\right|=O(1).
\]

Ordinary square-root discrepancy would give only \(O(\sqrt p)\), not a constant. Thus even a successful deterministic mixing theorem at the usual expander/Deligne scale would not automatically prove the empirical bound \(\le5\).

The bounded-critical-point phenomenon, if true uniformly, is therefore more rigid than generic expansion. It likely requires an Apéry-specific algebraic identity, a bounded-complexity two-coordinate realization, or a direct recurrence/resultant argument.

## 10. Direct answers to (a)--(e)

### (a)

**No as stated.** \(T_0\) is singular and the other \(T_n\) are not generally in \(SL_2\). The corrected projective group generated by \([T_1],[T_2]\) is Zariski-dense in \(PGL_2\), so its reductions contain \(PSL_2(\mathbf F_p)\) for all but finitely many primes. The exact assertion for every \(p\ge7\) still needs a finite-exception calculation.

### (b)

**Yes for the artificial random Cayley walk; no for the relevant deterministic cocycle.** The Cayley averaging operator attached to a fixed symmetric generating set has a uniform gap outside finitely many primes. The deterministic skew-product operator is unitary/permutational on its invertible domain and has spectral gap zero.

### (c)

**No.** Varjú's expansion theorem concerns congruence quotients with a fixed finite symmetric generating set and convolution/random words. It does not cover the polynomially time-varying prescribed Apéry product.

### (d)

**No.** Marginal equidistribution, even with multiplicity one, does not control intersection with a moving graph. A direct joint graph-correlation theorem is required. The Poisson heuristic predicts tightness but not a universal deterministic bound.

### (e)

**No.** The argument is incomplete at four separate transitions:

1. fixed-generator expansion does not mix the ordered cocycle;
2. equidistribution of \(v_n\) does not imply few solutions of \(v_n=R(n)\);
3. bounded \(\mathbf F_p\)-roots of the finite difference do not imply stationary-phase square-root cancellation;
4. square-root character sums imply at most \(|Z_p|=O(\sqrt p)\), not \(O(1)\).

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\text{Apéry cocycle expansion + the proposed stationary-phase step
is not a complete proof strategy.}
}
\]

The genuinely new one-prime theorem would have to be an ordered non-autonomous moving-target rigidity theorem, followed by a separate bounded-fiber argument for \(b_n=0\).
