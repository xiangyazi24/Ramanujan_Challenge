# 3. What counts as a genuine second equation

A polynomial `F_p(X)` is **not** automatically a second equation merely because it vanishes at some computed target vectors.

There are three different notions which must not be conflated.

## 3.1 Candidate-universal relation

A polynomial may vanish on every toric packet vector, target or not.  Such a relation belongs to the ambient toric ideal

\[
J_{1,p}^{\rm tor}.
\]

It lowers the ambient presentation but supplies no target codimension.

## 3.2 Structural target relation

This is the relevant notion for a carrier theorem.  A polynomial `F_p` is structurally target-valid if its vanishing follows from the currently proved toric/edge identities plus `b_r=0`, with all proved unit pivots inverted.  Equivalently, its class vanishes on every specialization of the saturated formal target fibre.

A genuine second equation would require

\[
[F_p]\notin(\ell_p)
\]

in the saturated quotient by the universal relations.

## 3.3 Actual-section-only relation

A new Apéry theorem could in principle prove

\[
p\mid b_r
\Longrightarrow
F_p(X(r-1))=0
\]

without `F_p` vanishing on every formal point of `ell_p=0`.  This would be genuinely new arithmetic.  The current toric saturation theorem does not rule it out, because it is deliberately stronger to ask for validity on the whole formal target fibre.

The question in Q3067 is exactly whether degree `2` or `3` escapes category 3.2.  It does not.

# 5. Characteristic-dependent degree-2/3 theorem

## Theorem 5.1 — low-degree nonlinear hyperplane no-go

Fix a relevant prime `p>3` outside the finite basis/pivot exceptional set.  Let

\[
F_p\in\mathbf F_p[X_1,\ldots,X_6]
\]

have total degree

\[
\deg F_p\le3.
\]

The coefficients of `F_p` may depend arbitrarily on `p`.

Assume `F_p` is structurally target-valid: after imposing the universal toric relations and the proved edge unit localizations, its value is zero for every formal specialization satisfying

\[
\ell_p=0.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
F_p\equiv\ell_pG_p
\pmod{J_{1,p}^{\rm tor}}
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

in the saturated ring, for some polynomial `G_p` with

\[
\deg G_p\le2.
\]

In particular, if `F_p` is quadratic, `G_p` may be taken linear; if `F_p` is cubic, `G_p` may be taken quadratic.

### Proof

Use the saturated coordinates `(t,u_1,...,u_5)` from (4.2).  Let

\[
\widetilde F_p(t,u_1,\ldots,u_5)
\]

be the image of `F_p` in this chart.  Structural target validity says

\[
\widetilde F_p(0,u_1,\ldots,u_5)=0
\tag{5.2}
\]

for every

\[
(u_1,\ldots,u_5)\in\mathbf F_p^5.
\]

The restriction in (5.2) has total degree at most `3`, hence degree at most `3<p` in each variable.  A polynomial over `F_p` of degree `<p` in each variable that vanishes on all of `F_p^5` is the zero polynomial.  This follows by induction on the number of variables from the one-variable root bound.

Therefore

\[
\widetilde F_p(0,u)=0
\]

as a polynomial identity, not just as a function.  Hence

\[
t\mid\widetilde F_p.
\]

Using `ell_p=epsilon_p t` with `epsilon_p` a unit gives

\[
\widetilde F_p=\ell_p\widetilde G_p.
\]

Undoing the saturated coordinate change gives (5.1).  The degree bound on `G_p` follows by division by the linear form.  QED.

## Stronger algebraic version

If one invokes the principal-ideal equality (4.1) directly, the conclusion does not require the degree bound at all for polynomial identities in the saturated coordinate ring.  The `degree < p` argument is useful for the more permissive interpretation where one only knows that a characteristic-dependent polynomial **function** vanishes on every `F_p`-point of the formal target fibre.

Thus degree `2` and `3` are safely below both boundaries for every `p>3`.

For the P3.2 interior asymptotic problem, these fixed primes are irrelevant and can be removed once and for all.

# 8. Hostile check: actual target points versus the formal target hyperplane

This distinction is load-bearing.

Suppose a prime `p` has only `k` actual Apéry target indices in the packet, giving points

\[

A finite scan can search for a quadratic or cubic polynomial vanishing at these points.  Such a polynomial may exist for completely dimension-theoretic reasons.

For six affine variables, the dimensions of polynomial spaces are

\[
\dim\{\deg\le2\}=\binom{8}{2}=28,
\qquad
\dim\{\deg\le3\}=\binom{9}{3}=84.
\tag{8.1}

After restricting to one hyperplane, there are five free variables, so

\[
\dim\{\deg\le2\text{ on }H\}=\binom{7}{2}=21,

Hence a nullspace discovered from a small finite target sample is expected and has no theorem-level meaning.  Even a full enumeration for one prime would prove only a statement in that one finite characteristic.

A useful second equation must be specified **before** looking at which `r` are roots, or be derived from an independent Apéry identity.  It must then be proved to vanish on every actual target in its stated range.
