# P3.2 direct attack: the balanced \({}_4F_3(1)\) does transform, but not to a product; the useful output is a Racah contiguity/continuant

## Verdict

There are three conclusions.

1. **The classical Whipple transformation for terminating balanced \({}_4F_3(1)\) applies exactly to this kernel.** It does **not** require the series itself to be well-poised. Applied correctly, it gives another terminating balanced \({}_4F_3\), not a gamma/Pochhammer product.

2. A particularly useful specialization is
   \[
   \boxed{
   K(r,s)=\binom{r+s}{r}^{2}
   {}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-r,-r,-s,-s\\1,-r-s,-r-s\end{matrix};1\right)
   }
   \tag{W1}
   \]
   and therefore
   \[
   \boxed{
   K(r,s)=\binom{r+s}{r}^{2}
   \sum_{k=0}^{r}
   \left(\frac{\binom rk\binom sk}{\binom{r+s}{k}}\right)^2.
   }
   \tag{W2}
   \]
   In the first-half finite-field range \(0\le r<s\le (p-1)/2\), the prefactor and all displayed denominators are units mod \(p\). Thus Whipple removes no obstruction: nonvanishing is equivalent to nonvanishing of the residual square-sum.

3. **The useful classical identity is instead the Racah three-term difference equation in \(s\).** It gives an exact factorization of every off-diagonal value, conditional on \(b_r=0\), as \(b_{r-1}\) times one explicit fixed-gap continuant. Combining this with the Apéry gap continuant already in `problems/3.2/gap_kernel_green.tex` reduces a hypothetical counterexample to a common zero of two explicit continuants. This immediately gives a clean proof for gap \(2\) (and an exact finite check also closes gap \(3\)).

So the answer to the product question is **no classical Whipple/Bailey/Dougall product**. But the answer to the contiguous-relation question is **yes, and it is substantially stronger than the current Q2336 drop records**.

## 0. First correction: the binomial sum in the question has the wrong sign

The hypergeometric formula is

\[
K(r,s)={}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-r,r+1,-s,s+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};1\right).
\]

For one pair,

\[
\frac{(-r)_k(r+1)_k}{(k!)^2}
=(-1)^k\binom rk\binom{r+k}{k}.
\]

The \(s\)-pair contributes a second \((-1)^k\), so the signs cancel. Hence

\[
\boxed{
K(r,s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(r,s)}
\binom rk\binom{r+k}{k}
\binom sk\binom{s+k}{k}.
}
\tag{0.1}
\]

There is **no** \((-1)^k\) in the final binomial sum. This is also forced by the already-proved adjacent identity: for example \(K(2,1)=13=(b_2+b_1)/6\), whereas inserting an alternating sign would give the wrong value.

I use the correctly signed kernel below.

### Correction A: balanced Whipple transformation vs. well-poised summation

Q2336 says, too broadly, that the Whipple route does not apply because the series is not well-poised. The correct distinction is:

- a **balanced \({}_4F_3\) transformation** applies;
- a **product summation** such as Dougall/Rogers-Dougall needs the stronger very-well-poised structure (or another special summation condition).

DLMF 16.4.14 is exactly the applicable balanced \({}_4F_3(1)\) transformation.

### Correction B: do not invoke Racah orthogonality at \(r=s\)

Q2336 lists \(r=s\) as a “norm value from Racah orthogonality”. That is not correct for this specialization. DLMF 18.26.3 identifies

\[
{}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-n,n+\alpha+\beta+1,-y,y+\gamma+\delta+1\\
\alpha+1,\beta+\delta+1,\gamma+1\end{matrix};1\right)
\]

with Racah form, but the standard finite orthogonality family requires one of the lower parameters to be \(-N\). At \(\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\delta=0\), all three lower parameters are \(+1\), so we are **outside that finite orthogonality regime**.

The diagonal here is simply

\[
K(r,r)=b_r,
\]

the Apéry number. It is not a classical product/norm evaluation.

Thus “Racah-form/bispectral specialization” is the safe terminology; standard Racah orthogonality should not be used here.

## 2. Exact classification in the tables

Write

\[
F_{r,s}:={}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-r,r+1,-s,s+1\\1,1,1\end{matrix};1\right).
\]

The upper-parameter sum is

\[
-r+(r+1)-s+(s+1)=2,
\]

and the lower-parameter sum is \(3\). Therefore the series is 1-balanced (Saalschützian) in the terminology of DLMF §16.4(i).

It is **not well-poised**. DLMF 16.4.1 says that for a \({}_{q+1}F_q\) one must be able to order parameters so that

\[
a_1+b_1=\cdots=a_q+b_q=a_{q+1}+1.
\]

Here every lower parameter is \(1\), so well-poisedness would force all four upper parameters to be equal. The set

\[
\{-r,r+1,-s,s+1\}
\]

is not of that form in the nontrivial range.

This is why the product sums in the Dougall/Rogers-Dougall part of the tables do not directly apply. DLMF 16.4.9 and 16.4.10 are very-well-poised \({}_5F_4\) and \({}_7F_6\) sums, not a summation theorem for a generic balanced \({}_4F_3\).

The relevant classical table entries are instead:

- DLMF 16.4.14: the terminating balanced \({}_4F_3(1)\) transformation;
- DLMF 16.4.15: Whipple’s very-well-poised \({}_7F_6\leftrightarrow{}_4F_3\) transformation;
- DLMF 18.26.3: Racah \({}_4F_3\) form;
- DLMF 18.26.4_2: Racah duality (our symmetry \(K(r,s)=K(s,r)\) is already manifest directly).

DLMF §16.4 explicitly points to Andrews–Askey–Roy, *Special Functions* (1999), Chapters 2–3, and to Bailey’s classical treatment; Gasper–Rahman §2.10 gives the corresponding Sears balanced \({}_4\phi_3\) transformation, whose \(q\to1\) limit is this Whipple transformation.

## 3. The applicable Whipple transformation, specialized exactly

DLMF 16.4.14 states, under

\[
a+b+c-n+1=d+e+f,
\]

that

\[
{}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-n,a,b,c\\d,e,f\end{matrix};1\right)
=
\frac{(e-a)_n(f-a)_n}{(e)_n(f)_n}
{}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-n,a,d-b,d-c\\d,a-e-n+1,a-f-n+1\end{matrix};1\right).
\tag{3.1}
\]

Take

\[
n=r,\qquad a=-s,\qquad b=r+1,\qquad c=s+1,\qquad d=e=f=1.
\]

The balance condition is

\[
-s+(r+1)+(s+1)-r+1=3.
\]

The prefactor is

\[
\frac{(s+1)_r^2}{(r!)^2}=\binom{r+s}{r}^2,
\]

and the transformed series is

\[
{}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-r,-s,-r,-s\\1,-r-s,-r-s\end{matrix};1\right).
\]

This proves (W1).

For its \(k\)-th term,

\[
\frac{(-r)_k(-s)_k}{(-r-s)_k\,k!}
=(-1)^k\frac{\binom rk\binom sk}{\binom{r+s}{k}},
\]

so squaring gives (W2).

There is a second useful version: choose \(a=s+1\). One obtains

\[
\boxed{
K(r,s)=\binom sr^2
{}_4F_3\!\left(\begin{matrix}-r,-r,s+1,s+1\\1,s-r+1,s-r+1\end{matrix};1\right)
}
\tag{W3}
\]

for \(r\le s\). This exposes the gap \(h=s-r\) in the lower parameters, but the residual series still has \(r+1\) terms. It is not a product evaluation.

### Finite-field consequence

If

\[
0\le r<s\le \frac{p-1}{2},
\]

then \(r+s\le p-2\). Consequently every factorial occurring in (W2) is below \(p\), so

\[
\binom{r+s}{r}\ne0\pmod p,
\qquad
\binom{r+s}{k}\ne0\pmod p.
\]

Therefore

\[
K(r,s)=0\pmod p
\iff
\sum_{k=0}^{r}
\left(\frac{\binom rk\binom sk}{\binom{r+s}{k}}\right)^2=0\pmod p.
\tag{3.2}
\]

This is a genuine transformation, but it does **not** prove nonvanishing: finite-field sums of many squares can cancel.

## 4. Why there is no useful `F(r) G(s) × unit` coming from these tables

The table search does not produce a factorization

\[
K(r,s)=F(r)G(s)H(r,s)
\]

with \(H\) an explicitly guaranteed unit in the desired range.

What Whipple actually produces is

\[
K(r,s)=\underbrace{\binom{r+s}{r}^2}_{\text{unit in the first-half range}}
\times
\underbrace{{}_4F_3(\text{new balanced parameters};1)}_{\text{entire remaining obstruction}}.
\]

Dougall-type product evaluations live on stronger very-well-poised loci. Our parameters do not lie on those loci. Embedding the series into a Whipple \({}_7F_6\) and then trying to apply Dougall runs into singular integral-parameter specializations; the limiting identity returns a residual balanced series rather than a nonzero product.

There is also a simple sanity check against an all-parameter gamma/Pochhammer product: setting \(s=r\) would turn such an identity into a single gamma/Pochhammer product for

\[
b_r={}_4F_3(-r,-r,r+1,r+1;1,1,1;1),
\]

which is precisely the non-hypergeometric Apéry sequence, not a classical product-summation family.

I would therefore mark the **product-summation avenue closed** unless one adds genuinely Apéry-specific arithmetic input beyond the standard transformation tables.

## 5. The correct contiguous relation: the zero-parameter Racah difference equation

The hypergeometric kernel is the formal zero-parameter Racah specialization

\[
K(r,s)=R_r(s(s+1);0,0,0,0).
\]

Although \((0,0,0,0)\) is outside the standard *finite orthogonality* parameter range, the hypergeometric polynomial identity and its difference equation specialize perfectly well (and can also be checked directly from the terminating sum).

For fixed \(r\), put

\[
y_s:=K(r,s),\qquad \lambda_r=r(r+1).
\]

The standard Racah coefficients at \(\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\delta=0\) are

\[
B(s)=\frac{(s+1)^3}{2(2s+1)},
\qquad
D(s)=\frac{s^3}{2(2s+1)}.
\]

The difference equation is

\[
B(s)(y_{s+1}-y_s)+D(s)(y_{s-1}-y_s)=\lambda_r y_s.
\]

Multiplying by \(2(2s+1)\) gives the completely integral form

\[
\boxed{
(s+1)^3\bigl(K(r,s+1)-K(r,s)\bigr)
+s^3\bigl(K(r,s-1)-K(r,s)\bigr)
=2(2s+1)r(r+1)K(r,s).
}
\tag{R1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
(s+1)^3K(r,s+1)+s^3K(r,s-1)
=(2s+1)\bigl(s(s+1)+1+2r(r+1)\bigr)K(r,s).
}
\tag{R2}
\]

By symmetry the identical equation holds with \(r,s\) interchanged.

This is the classical contiguous relation that is actually useful here.

### Adjacent value recovered immediately

At \(s=r\), if \(b_r=K(r,r)=0\), (R2) gives

\[
(r+1)^3K(r,r+1)+r^3K(r,r-1)=0.
\]

Using the proved identity

\[
K(r,r-1)=\frac{b_{r-1}}6,
\]

we get

\[
\boxed{
K(r,r+1)=-\frac{r^3}{(r+1)^3}\frac{b_{r-1}}6\ne0.
}
\tag{R3}
\]

So the Racah contiguous equation contains both adjacent directions at once.

## 6. Exact fixed-gap factorization when \(b_r=0\)

This is, in my view, the strongest output of the hypergeometric attack.

Let

\[
s=r+h,\qquad h\ge1,
\]

and define

\[
Q_m(r):=(2r+2m+1)
\bigl(3r^2+2rm+m^2+3r+m+1\bigr).
\tag{6.1}
\]

This is just the coefficient in (R2) at \(s=r+m\):

\[
Q_m(r)=(2s+1)\bigl(s(s+1)+1+2r(r+1)\bigr).
\]

Define the **Racah gap continuant** \(\mathcal R_m(r)\) by

\[
\mathcal R_0(r)=1,
\qquad
\mathcal R_1(r)=Q_1(r),
\]

and, for \(m\ge2\),

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_m(r)=Q_m(r)\mathcal R_{m-1}(r)-(r+m)^6\mathcal R_{m-2}(r).
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Now assume \(b_r=0\). Starting with

\[
K(r,r)=0,
\qquad
K(r,r-1)=\frac{b_{r-1}}6,
\]

and iterating (R2), one obtains by induction

\[
\boxed{
K(r,r+h)
=-\frac{r^3b_{r-1}}{6\,(r+1)_h^3}\,
\mathcal R_{h-1}(r).
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Here

\[
(r+1)_h=(r+1)(r+2)\cdots(r+h).
\]

Thus, in the nonwrapping range \(0<r<r+h<p\), with \(p>3\), the known no-consecutive-zero fact implies \(b_{r-1}\ne0\), all denominator factors are units, and

\[
\boxed{
K(r,r+h)=0\pmod p
\iff
\mathcal R_{h-1}(r)=0\pmod p
\qquad (b_r=0).
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

This is much sharper than a generic “there is a contiguous relation”: it isolates the exact extra polynomial/continuant whose vanishing would be required for a counterexample.

## 7. Combine with the existing Apéry gap continuant

The checked-in project file `problems/3.2/gap_kernel_green.tex` already proves, with its notation \(N_h(r)\),

\[
\frac{N_h(r)}{\prod_{j=1}^{h}(r+j)^3}
=b_r c_{r+h}-c_r b_{r+h}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Its recurrence is

\[
N_1(r)=1,
\qquad
N_2(r)=P(r+1),
\]

\[
N_{h+1}(r)=P(r+h)N_h(r)-(r+h)^6N_{h-1}(r),
\tag{7.2}
\]

where

\[
P(t)=(2t+1)(17t^2+17t+5).
\]

Therefore a zero pair

\[
b_r=b_{r+h}=0
\]

forces

\[
N_h(r)=0\pmod p.
\tag{7.3}
\]

A counterexample to the desired direct theorem would consequently force the **simultaneous** congruences

\[
\boxed{
N_h(r)=0,
\qquad
\mathcal R_{h-1}(r)=0
\pmod p.
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

This is the precise bridge between the hypergeometric/Racah attack and the project’s existing gap-kernel algebra.

For each fixed \(h\), if the characteristic-zero resultant

\[
\operatorname{Res}_r\bigl(N_h(r),\mathcal R_{h-1}(r)\bigr)
\]

is nonzero after removing the reflection-mate factor when it occurs, then only the finitely many prime divisors of that integer can support a counterexample at that gap. This does not solve growing \(h\), but it is an exact finite-gap theorem schema.

## 8. New immediate corollary: gap \(h=2\) is completely closed

Take \(s=r+2\). From the Apéry zero-pair condition,

\[
N_2(r)=P(r+1)=0\pmod p.
\]

Explicitly,

\[
N_2(r)
=(2r+3)(17r^2+51r+39).
\tag{8.1}
\]

On the Racah side,

\[
\mathcal R_1(r)=Q_1(r)
=(2r+3)(3r^2+5r+3).
\tag{8.2}
\]

The common factor \(2r+3\) is exactly the even-gap reflection-mate factor: \(2r+3\equiv0\pmod p\) means \(r+2\equiv p-1-r\pmod p\).

For a **nonmate first-half pair**, \(r+2\le(p-1)/2\), this factor is a unit. We are reduced to

\[
17r^2+51r+39=0,
\qquad
3r^2+5r+3=0
\pmod p.
\]

Their characteristic-zero resultant is

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_r(17r^2+51r+39,\;3r^2+5r+3)=1500
=2^2\cdot3\cdot5^3.
}
\tag{8.3}
\]

Hence for every prime \(p\ge7\) the two equations cannot hold simultaneously. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
 b_r=b_{r+2}=0,\quad r+2\le\frac{p-1}{2}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 K(r,r+2)\ne0
 \qquad(p\ge7).
}
\tag{8.4}
\]

So the Racah contiguous relation extends the already-proved adjacent case to **gap 2** with a two-quadratic resultant calculation.

For completeness, the next pair of continuants is

+259059r^2+198156r+62531,

and

\mathcal R_2(r)=

+1715r^2+984r+251.

They are coprime over \(\mathbf Q\), and the exact resultant is

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_r(N_3,\mathcal R_2)
=-2^{18}5^7\,7\,61^2\,73^3.
}
\tag{9.1}

Thus only \(p\in\{5,7,61,73\}\) need inspection (for odd \(p\ge5\)). Exact residue checking in the first-half range gives no zero-pair counterexample. The only common polynomial root in the relevant range among the nontrivial exceptional cases is

b_8\equiv12\pmod{61},

so it is not a pair of Apéry zeros. Hence the same direct method also closes gap \(3\).

I regard this section as a useful exact finite check, not as evidence that the growing-gap resultants will stay small; they need not.

## 10. Why there is no contiguous identity using only \(K(r,r)\) and \(K(s,s)\)

The classical contiguous structure is second order in one spectral variable. For fixed \(r\), (R2) determines the row \(s\mapsto K(r,s)\) from **two adjacent initial values**, for example

\[
K(r,r),\qquad K(r,r-1).
\]

The value

\[
K(s,s)=b_s
\]

belongs to a *different row* (different Racah eigenvalue), so it is not the missing second initial datum for the fixed-\(r\) recurrence.

Accordingly, the standard contiguous tables do not give a relation of the schematic form

\[
K(r,s)=U(r,s)K(r,r)+V(r,s)K(s,s)
\]

with \(U,V\) universally invertible. If such a two-diagonal formula existed, then when both diagonals vanished it would force \(K(r,s)=0\), exactly the opposite of the target; so it could not be the desired nonvanishing mechanism anyway.

The correct local data are instead

\[
K(r,r)=b_r,
\qquad
K(r,r-1)=\frac{b_r+b_{r-1}}6,
\]

and after setting \(b_r=0\), the entire row is the single continuant (6.3).

## 11. Recommended next attack

The hypergeometric-table search is now essentially exhausted. I would freeze the following exact reduction:

> For \(s=r+h\) in the first-half nonmate range, a counterexample
> \[
> b_r=b_s=0,\qquad K(r,s)=0
> \]
> forces a common mod-\(p\) root of the two characteristic-zero continuants
> \[
> N_h(r),\qquad \mathcal R_{h-1}(r).
> \]

This gives two concrete ways forward:

1. **Fixed-gap resultant theorem.** Determine
   \[
   G_h=\gcd_{\mathbf Z[r]}(N_h,\mathcal R_{h-1})
   \]
   exactly (the even-gap reflection factor should be separated), and bound/factor
   \[
   \operatorname{Res}(N_h/G_h,\mathcal R_{h-1}/G_h).
   \]
   Gaps \(1,2,3\) are now closed by this method.

2. **A two-recurrence discrete Wronskian.** The Apéry gap continuant and the Racah continuant have the same \(-(r+m)^6\) off-diagonal coefficient but different middle coefficients:
   \[
   P(r+m)
   \quad\text{versus}\quad
   Q_m(r).
   \]
   That shared determinant structure is much more rigid than a generic pair of polynomials. A Bezout/WZ identity between the two continuants is the natural all-gap target.

This is more promising than another pass through Whipple/Dougall tables: the classical transformation theory has already exposed all it can, while the **difference equation produces exactly the arithmetic common-zero problem relevant to P3.2**.

## Final answer

- **Whipple transformation?** Yes: DLMF 16.4.14 applies exactly.
- **Product evaluation?** No: it transforms to another balanced \({}_4F_3\), e.g. (W1)/(W2); the residual series is not a guaranteed unit.
- **Dougall/very-well-poised summation?** Not applicable to this parameter locus in a nonsingular way that yields a product.
- **Contiguous relation?** Yes: the exact Racah equation (R1)/(R2).
- **Does it reduce to the diagonal values \(b_r,b_s\) alone?** No. It needs two adjacent values in one row.
- **Does it materially advance the direct attack?** Yes: under \(b_r=0\), (6.3) factors the off-diagonal kernel through one explicit Racah gap continuant; together with the existing Apéry gap continuant it turns a counterexample into a common-root/resultant problem. This proves the nonmate gap-2 case outright and, after a finite exceptional-prime check, gap 3 as well.

The key identity to carry forward is therefore not a product summation but

\[
\boxed{
K(r,r+h)
=-\frac{r^3b_{r-1}}{6(r+1)_h^3}\mathcal R_{h-1}(r)
\quad\text{when }b_r=0.
}
\]

That is the hypergeometric/Racah seam I would use for the next P3.2 attack.
