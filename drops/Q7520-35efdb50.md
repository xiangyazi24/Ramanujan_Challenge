# Route selection: the Cartier–Hurwitz primitive of the Apéry Hasse square root

## Verdict

I would put the next serious effort into **one route only**:

> use the Apéry-specific mod-​\(p\) square-root differential equation to turn a marked coefficient zero \(b_r\equiv0\pmod p\) into an exact tame rational cover of \(\mathbf P^1\); use the Kummer order
> \[
> d=\frac{p-1}{\gcd(r,p-1)}
> \]
> as a quantitative *indecomposability/imprimitivity parameter* for that cover; then prove a defining-characteristic moving-character large sieve for the resulting rank-two hypergeometric/Hurwitz objects.

This is not a generic holonomic or monodromy proposal.  The first part can already be pushed rigorously, and it gives a new theorem that appears to close a caveat explicitly left open in the current repository: the normalized Apéry Hasse square root \(B_p\) is **squarefree and coprime to the conifold discriminant for every \(p\ge5\)**.  From it one obtains an exact target-preserving primitive whose complete ramification passport is known.  Most importantly for the present all-index problem, any nontrivial imprimitive outer factor has degree dividing
\[
\gcd(r,p-1)=\frac{p-1}{d}.
\]
For \(d>2\) that outer degree is necessarily odd, and a nontrivial decomposition forces at least \(d\) distinct Apéry-square-root critical points to share one critical value.

That is genuine large-\(d\) rigidity.  It is complementary to the already proved small-\(d\) mass estimate, rather than another equivalent reformulation of \(p\mid b_r\).

There is also an important negative audit inside the same route: the tempting \(\asymp p\) family of critical-point equations is **rank one** as a divisibility certificate.  Its ordinary resultant is just a power of \(b_r\).  Thus one must not feed those critical points individually into an ordinary resultant/Bezout argument.  The remaining irreducible step is a horizontal, defining-characteristic operator-norm large sieve; I state below a precise version which would imply \(W_n=o(n)\) immediately when combined with the established small-Kummer-order estimate.

# 1. The Apéry Hasse square root

For a prime \(p\ge5\), put
\[
N=p-1,
\qquad
A_p(T)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}b_mT^m\in\mathbf F_p[T],
\qquad
\Delta(T)=T^2-34T+1.
\]
The project already records the Caruso–Fürnsinn–Vargas-Montoya–Zudilin factorization
\[
\boxed{
 A_p(T)=\Delta(T)^{\varepsilon_p}B_p(T)^2,
}
\tag{1}
\]
where
\[
\varepsilon_p=\frac{1-\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)}2\in\{0,1\},
\qquad
B_p(0)=1,
\qquad
M_p:=\deg B_p=\frac{N-2\varepsilon_p}{2}.
\tag{2}
\]
The same repository file gives universal characteristic-zero square roots
\[
\mathcal A(T)=C_+(T)^2=\Delta(T)C_-(T)^2
\tag{3}
\]
and shows that \(B_p\) is the indicated truncation of \(C_+\) or \(C_-\).  Write \(\vartheta=T\,d/dT\).  The two square roots satisfy
\[
L_+C_+=0,
\qquad
L_-C_-=0,
\tag{4}
\]
with
\[
L_+=4\vartheta^2
 -2T(68\vartheta^2+34\vartheta+5)
 +T^2(2\vartheta+1)^2,
\tag{5}
\]
\[
L_-=4\vartheta^2
 -2T(68\vartheta^2+102\vartheta+39)
 +T^2(2\vartheta+3)^2.
\tag{6}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{
L_+=4\Delta\vartheta^2+4T(T-17)\vartheta+T(T-10),
}
\tag{7}
\]
\[
\boxed{
L_-=4\Delta\vartheta^2+12T(T-17)\vartheta+3T(3T-26).
}
\tag{8}
\]

The current `hasse_franel_descent.tex` explicitly says that squarefreeness of \(B_p\) and coprimality with \(\Delta\) had only been checked computationally.  They are in fact consequences of (1)--(8).

## Theorem 1 — exact square-root ODE, squarefreeness, and conifold avoidance

For every prime \(p\ge5\):

1. if \(\varepsilon_p=0\), then
   \[
   \boxed{L_+B_p=0\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_p[T];}
   \tag{9}
   \]
2. if \(\varepsilon_p=1\), then
   \[
   \boxed{L_-B_p=0\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_p[T];}
   \tag{10}
   \]
3. \(B_p\) is coprime to \(T\Delta(T)\);
4. \(B_p\) is squarefree over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\).

Hence \(B_p\) has exactly \(M_p=(p-1-2\varepsilon_p)/2\) distinct geometric roots, none equal to \(0\) or to a conifold point.

### Proof

Write
\[
C_+(T)=\sum_{m\ge0}c_mT^m,
\qquad
C_-(T)=\sum_{m\ge0}d_mT^m.
\]
The coefficient recurrences recorded in the project are
\[
4(m+1)^2c_{m+1}
=2(68m^2+34m+5)c_m-(2m-1)^2c_{m-1},
\tag{11}
\]
\[
4(m+1)^2d_{m+1}
=2(68m^2+102m+39)d_m-(2m+1)^2d_{m-1}.
\tag{12}
\]

### Square branch

Assume \(\varepsilon_p=0\), so \(M_p=N/2=(p-1)/2\) and
\[
B_p=\sum_{m=0}^{M_p}\bar c_mT^m.
\]
Compare the coefficient of \(T^{M_p+1}\) in the characteristic-zero identity \(\mathcal A=C_+^2\) with the same coefficient in the exact mod-\(p\) identity \(A_p=B_p^2\).  Since
\[
M_p+1=\frac{p+1}{2}<p,
\]
the coefficient of \(\mathcal A\) is still the actual Apéry coefficient reduced modulo \(p\).  All convolution terms agree except the two endpoint terms involving \(c_{M_p+1}\).  Therefore
\[
2\bar c_{M_p+1}=0,
\]
so
\[
\bar c_{M_p+1}=0.
\tag{13}
\]
Every coefficient of \(L_+B_p\) through degree \(M_p+1\) now vanishes by the recurrence (11).  The only possible degree-\(M_p+2\) term is
\[
(2M_p+1)^2\bar c_{M_p}T^{M_p+2}
=p^2\bar c_{M_p}T^{M_p+2}=0.
\]
Thus (9) holds exactly, not merely up to the truncation order.

### Defect branch

Assume \(\varepsilon_p=1\), so \(M_p=(p-3)/2\) and
\[
B_p=\sum_{m=0}^{M_p}\bar d_mT^m.
\]
Because \(\Delta(0)=1\), division by \(\Delta\) is valid in \(\mathbf F_p[[T]]\).  The identities
\[
\mathcal A/\Delta=C_-^2,
\qquad
A_p/\Delta=B_p^2
\]
agree through degree \(p-1\).  Comparing degree
\[
M_p+1=\frac{p-1}{2}
\]
again leaves only the two endpoint terms and gives
\[
\bar d_{M_p+1}=0.
\tag{14}
\]
The recurrence (12) kills all coefficients of \(L_-B_p\) through degree \(M_p+1\), while the last possible term is
\[
(2M_p+3)^2\bar d_{M_p}T^{M_p+2}
=p^2\bar d_{M_p}T^{M_p+2}=0.
\]
This proves (10).

### No repeated root away from the conifold

Let \(\alpha\ne0\) with \(\Delta(\alpha)\ne0\), and suppose
\[
B_p(T)=(T-\alpha)^m u(T),
\qquad
m\ge2,
\qquad
u:=u(\alpha)\ne0.
\]
Because \(m<p\), the coefficient
\[
4\Delta(\alpha)\alpha^2m(m-1)\nu
\]
of \((T-\alpha)^{m-2}\) in \(4\Delta\vartheta^2B_p\) is nonzero.  Every other term in either (7) or (8) has order at least \(m-1\).  This contradicts \(L_\pm B_p=0\).  Therefore every root away from \(T\Delta=0\) is simple.

Also \(B_p(0)=1\), so \(0\) is not a root.

### No root on \(\Delta=0\)

The discriminant of \(\Delta\) is \(1152\), hence \(\Delta\) has two distinct roots over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\) for every \(p\ge5\).  If \(\Delta(\alpha)=0\), then \(\alpha\ne0\), and
\[
\alpha\ne17
\]
because
\[
\Delta(17)=-288
\]
has no prime factor \(\ge5\).
Moreover
\[
\Delta'(\alpha)=2(\alpha-17).
\tag{15}
\]
Suppose again
\[
B_p=(T-\alpha)^m u,
\qquad m\ge1,
\qquad u(\alpha)\ne0.
\]
In the square branch, the coefficient of \((T-\alpha)^{m-1}\) in (7) is
\[
4\alpha^2(\alpha-17)m(2m-1)u(\alpha).
\tag{16}
\]
Here
\[
1\le m\le\frac{p-1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1\le2m-1\le p-2,
\]
so (16) is nonzero.

In the defect branch, the corresponding coefficient from (8) is
\[
4\alpha^2(\alpha-17)m(2m+1)u(\alpha).
\tag{17}
\]
Now
\[
1\le m\le\frac{p-3}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
3\le2m+1\le p-2,
\]
so (17) is nonzero as well.  Both contradict \(L_\pm B_p=0\).

Thus \(\gcd(B_p,\Delta)=1\), and the preceding argument then makes every root simple.  This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

# 2. A target-preserving nonhomogeneous primitive

The preceding theorem becomes useful for the **coefficient** problem only after keeping the marked residue in the operator.  This is where an ordinary evaluation-root argument would miss the target.

Let
\[
1\le r\le p-2.
\]
Define
\[
\boxed{
Q_{p,r}(T)
:=\sum_{\substack{0\le m\le p-1\\m\ne r}}
\frac{b_m}{m-r}T^m
\in\mathbf F_p[T].
}
\tag{18}
\]
All denominators are nonzero in \(\mathbf F_p\).  Then, coefficient by coefficient,
\[
\boxed{
(\vartheta-r)Q_{p,r}
=A_p-b_rT^r.
}
\tag{19}
\]
For the all-index target \(r=n\bmod p\), one may equally write \(\vartheta-n\), since \(n\equiv r\pmod p\).  Thus
\[
\boxed{
 p\mid b_r
 \iff
 (\vartheta-n)Q_{p,r}=A_p
 =\Delta^{\varepsilon_p}B_p^2.
}
\tag{20}
\]
This is a genuinely target-preserving nonhomogeneous identity: the one-dimensional cokernel of \(\vartheta-n\) on the degree-\(<p\) polynomial space is exactly the marked monomial \(T^r\), and \(b_r\) is its obstruction class.

It is still not enough by itself.  The gain comes from reading (20) as a ramification statement.

Put
\[
\boxed{
H_{p,r}(T):=T^{-r}Q_{p,r}(T)\in\mathbf F_p(T).
}
\tag{21}
\]
Differentiating (19) gives the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
H_{p,r}'(T)
=T^{-r-1}\bigl(A_p(T)-b_rT^r\bigr).
}
\tag{22}
\]
Hence at a bad residue,
\[
\boxed{
 b_r=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 H_{p,r}'(T)=T^{-r-1}\Delta(T)^{\varepsilon_p}B_p(T)^2.
}
\tag{23}
\]

For every bad prime in the range relevant to \(W_n\), the endpoints are automatically absent: \(b_0=1\) and \(b_{p-1}\equiv b_0=1\pmod p\).  Thus (18)--(23) apply to every prime actually counted by \(W_n\).

## Theorem 2 — exact tame Cartier–Hurwitz passport

Assume \(p\ge5\), \(1\le r\le p-2\), and \(b_r\equiv0\pmod p\).  Then the rational map
\[
H_{p,r}:\mathbf P^1\longrightarrow\mathbf P^1
\]
has degree \(N=p-1\), is tamely ramified, and has the following complete ramification data.

1. Its only poles are \(0\) and \(\infty\), with ramification indices
   \[
   e_0=r,
   \qquad
   e_\infty=p-1-r.
   \tag{24}
   \]
2. Every root of \(B_p\) is a distinct finite ramification point of index \(3\).
3. If \(\varepsilon_p=1\), the two roots of \(\Delta\) are distinct finite ramification points of index \(2\).  If \(\varepsilon_p=0\), there are no index-\(2\) finite ramification points.
4. There is no other ramification.

### Proof

The coefficient of \(T^0\) in \(Q_{p,r}\) is
\[
-\frac1r\ne0,
\]
while, by reflection, \(b_{p-1}=1\) in \(\mathbf F_p\), so the coefficient of \(T^{p-1}\) is
\[
\frac1{p-1-r}\ne0.
\]
Therefore \(H_{p,r}\) has exact pole orders \(r\) and \(p-1-r\) at \(0\) and \(\infty\), and
\[
\deg H_{p,r}=r+(p-1-r)=p-1.
\]
Both pole orders are less than \(p\), hence tame.

By Theorem 1, the roots of \(B_p\) are simple and disjoint from \(T\Delta=0\).  Equation (23) has a zero of order exactly \(2\) at each such root, hence the local ramification index is \(3\).  When \(\varepsilon_p=1\), each simple root of \(\Delta\) gives a simple zero of the derivative and hence ramification index \(2\).

Finally the displayed points already exhaust Riemann–Hurwitz.  The two poles contribute
\[
(r-1)+(p-2-r)=p-3.
\]
The \(M_p\) roots of \(B_p\) contribute
\[
2M_p=p-1-2\varepsilon_p,
\]
and the conifold roots contribute \(2\varepsilon_p\).  The total is
\[
(p-3)+(p-1-2\varepsilon_p)+2\varepsilon_p
=2p-4
=2\deg(H_{p,r})-2.
\]
Thus there can be no additional ramification. \(\square\)

This is the first point where the large Kummer order becomes geometric rather than merely a character label.

# 3. Large Kummer order is an exact decomposition obstruction

Set
\[
g=\gcd(r,p-1),
\qquad
d=\frac{p-1}{g}.
\tag{25}
\]
Thus \(d\) is exactly the Kummer order from established fact (5).

## Theorem 3 — outer-degree divisibility and cubic packet amplification

Assume \(p\ge7\), \(b_r\equiv0\pmod p\), and let \(H=H_{p,r}\).  Suppose that over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\)
\[
H=F\circ G
\tag{26}
\]
is a nontrivial rational decomposition, with
\[
a=\deg F>1,
\qquad
b=\deg G>1,
\qquad
ab=p-1.
\tag{27}
\]
Then:

\[
\boxed{a\mid g=\frac{p-1}{d},}
\tag{28}
\]
and therefore
\[
\boxed{b\ge d.}
\tag{29}
\]

If in addition \(d>2\), then
\[
\boxed{a\text{ is odd}.}
\tag{30}
\]
Moreover some finite branch value of \(F\) pulls back to at least \(b\ge d\) **distinct roots of \(B_p\)**, all of ramification index \(3\) for \(H\), and all having the same \(H\)-value.

Consequently, when \(d>2\) and \(g\) is a power of \(2\), \(H_{p,r}\) is indecomposable.  In particular it is indecomposable for maximal Kummer order \(d=p-1\).

### Proof

The pole set of \(H\) is exactly \(\{0,\infty\}\).  Every pole of \(F\) has a nonempty inverse image under the surjective map \(G\), and every such inverse image is a pole of \(H\).  Therefore \(F\) has either one or two poles.

### The two-pole outer case is impossible

Suppose \(F\) has two poles.  Their two nonempty inverse images under \(G\) must partition the two-point set \(\{0,\infty\}\).  Hence each pole of \(F\) has exactly one preimage under the degree-\(b\) map \(G\), so \(G\) is totally ramified at both \(0\) and \(\infty\).  After a Möbius change in the intermediate coordinate,
\[
G(T)=cT^{\pm b}.
\tag{31}
\]
It follows that
\[
H(\zeta T)=H(T)
\qquad(\zeta^b=1).
\tag{32}
\]

But \(H=T^{-r}Q_{p,r}\) is a Laurent polynomial.  Because \(p\ge7\) and \(b_r=0\), one cannot have \(r=1\), since \(b_1=5\not\equiv0\pmod p\).  Therefore the \(m=0\) and \(m=1\) terms in (18) are both present and nonzero.  They give two nonzero Laurent exponents
\[
-r,
\qquad
1-r,
\]
which differ by \(1\).  A \(\mu_b\)-invariant Laurent polynomial with \(b>1\) can have nonzero terms only in a single congruence class modulo \(b\) (after the harmless middle-coordinate normalization); in particular it cannot contain two nonzero exponents differing by \(1\).  Thus (32) forces \(b=1\), contradicting nontriviality.

So \(F\) has exactly one pole.

### Divisibility by the pole gcd

A degree-\(a\) rational function with one pole has pole order \(a\) there.  The inverse image of that pole under \(G\) is exactly \(\{0,\infty\}\).  Hence the two pole orders of the composite are
\[
r=a\,e_G(0),
\qquad
p-1-r=a\,e_G(\infty).
\]
Thus
\[
a\mid r,
\qquad
a\mid p-1-r,
\]
which gives
\[
a\mid\gcd(r,p-1)=g.
\]
This proves (28), and (29) follows from
\[
b=\frac{p-1}{a}\ge\frac{p-1}{g}=d.
\]

### When \(d>2\), every finite critical point of \(F\) is cubic

After a Möbius change of the intermediate coordinate, the one-pole map \(F\) is a polynomial of degree \(a\).  Since \(a>1\) and \(p\nmid a\), it has a finite critical point \(y\).  For every \(x\in G^{-1}(y)\), ramification indices multiply:
\[
e_H(x)=e_F(y)e_G(x).
\tag{33}
\]
By Theorem 2, every finite ramification index of \(H\) is either \(2\) or \(3\).  Therefore \(e_F(y)\) is \(2\) or \(3\), and in either case (33) forces \(e_G(x)=1\) for every point in that fiber.

If \(e_F(y)=2\), the fiber \(G^{-1}(y)\) consists of \(b\) distinct index-\(2\) ramification points of \(H\).  There are at most two such points in total, namely the two conifold roots when \(\varepsilon_p=1\).  But (29) and \(d>2\) give \(b>2\), a contradiction.

Thus every finite critical point of \(F\) has ramification index exactly \(3\), and \(G\) is unramified above it.  In particular, any one such critical point produces a fiber of \(b\) distinct index-\(3\) points of \(H\).  By Theorem 2 these are distinct roots of \(B_p\), all sharing the same \(H\)-value.  This proves the packet statement and its lower bound \(b\ge d\).

Finally, since \(F\) is a degree-\(a\) polynomial, its point at infinity contributes \(a-1\) to Riemann–Hurwitz.  The total finite ramification contribution is therefore also \(a-1\).  Every finite critical point contributes \(3-1=2\), so \(a-1\) is even.  Thus \(a\) is odd, proving (30).

If \(g\) is a power of \(2\), no odd integer \(a>1\) can divide \(g\), contradicting (28)--(30).  Hence no nontrivial decomposition exists. \(\square\)

### What this theorem actually buys

The established small-order estimate controls primes with \(d\le D\).  The complementary range \(d>D\) now has the following rigid alternative at each bad prime:

- either the degree-\(p-1\) cover \(H_{p,r}\) is indecomposable; or
- every imprimitive outer degree is at most \((p-1)/d<(p-1)/D\), is odd when \(d>2\), and a decomposition forces a critical-value packet containing at least \(d>D\) distinct Apéry square-root roots.

This is an actual structural saving parameter attached to the same \(d\) that occurs in fact (5).  No generic P-recursive statement gives it.

# 4. Necessary falsification: the \(\asymp p\) critical equations have rank one

It would be tempting to say: at a bad residue all \(M_p\asymp p\) roots of \(B_p\) become critical for \(H_{p,r}\), so perhaps one can multiply those conditions and gain a factor \(p\).  That is false.

For arbitrary \(r\), not assumed bad, (22) gives at a root \(\alpha\) of \(B_p\)
\[
H_{p,r}'(\alpha)
=-b_r\alpha^{-1}.
\tag{34}
\]
Algebraically, from (1),
\[
A_p-b_rT^r\equiv-b_rT^r\pmod{B_p}.
\tag{35}
\]
Since \(B_p(0)=1\), \(T\) is a unit in \(\mathbf F_p[T]/(B_p)\).  Hence
\[
\gcd(B_p,A_p-b_rT^r)
=\begin{cases}
B_p,&b_r=0,\\
1,&b_r\ne0.
\end{cases}
\tag{36}
\]
and, with \(M_p=\deg B_p\),
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_T(B_p,A_p-b_rT^r)
=\pm b_r^{M_p}.
}
\tag{37}
\]
(The remaining factor is \(B_p(0)^r=1\).)

Thus the \(M_p\) geometric incidences are not \(M_p\) independent arithmetic conditions.  Their raw resultant is merely the original marked coefficient raised to a power.  This kills the ordinary-resultant version of the route and is exactly consistent with established obstruction (4).

What survives is the **global cover structure**: pole gcd, decomposition, ramification packets, and the rank-two defining-characteristic trace.  Those are not visible in the scalar resultant (37).

# 5. The next irreducible statement: a defining-characteristic Cartier–Hurwitz large sieve

The repository already supplies the fixed toric/Kummer realization and, more sharply in `lemmaBprime_result.tex`, a rank-three effective Frobenius factor
\[
P_{p,r}(X)
=(X-p)\bigl(X^2-\mathfrak a_{p,r}X+p^3\bigr),
\tag{38}
\]
where
\[
\mathfrak a_{p,r}\equiv b_r\pmod{\mathfrak p}.
\tag{39}
\]
Thus the bad event is exactly defining-characteristic loss of the unit root in the rank-two factor.  Generic fixed-conductor monodromy does not control (39); the project has already isolated that obstruction.  The new Theorems 1--3 add the missing large-order Hurwitz rigidity.

Here is the precise statement I would now attack.

## CHLS — Cartier–Hurwitz operator large sieve

There exist absolute constants \(\eta>0\) and \(C>0\) such that the following holds uniformly for \(P\ge2\), \(D\ge2\), and every interval \(I\subset\mathbf Z\) of \(\lceil P\rceil\) consecutive integers.

For \(P<p\le2P\) prime and \(m\in I\), let
\[
r_p(m)=m\bmod p\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\},
\qquad
 d_p(m)=\frac{p-1}{\gcd(r_p(m),p-1)},
\tag{40}
\]
and define the incidence matrix
\[
\mathsf M_{P,D,I}(p,m)
=
\mathbf1_{\{d_p(m)>D\}}
\mathbf1_{\{b_{r_p(m)}\equiv0\pmod p\}}.
\tag{41}
\]
Equivalently the second indicator is the defining-characteristic condition
\[
\mathfrak p\mid\mathfrak a_{p,r_p(m)}
\]
in (38)--(39), or the existence of the exact Cartier–Hurwitz cover (21)--(23).

The desired large-sieve statement is
\[
\boxed{
\left\|\mathsf M_{P,D,I}\right\|_{\ell^2(I)\to
 \ell^2(\{p:P<p\le2P\})}^{\,2}
\le
C\left(
\frac{P}{D^{\eta}\log P}+P^{1-\eta}
\right).
}
\tag{CHLS}
\]

This is deliberately stronger than a bound for one moving target.  It controls every linear combination of targets in an interval and therefore rules out the anchored-star pathology that defeats average-zero information.  It is not an equivalent rewriting of \(W_n=o(n)\).

## CHLS gives the all-index conjecture

Fix \(n\), and consider one dyadic block \(P<p\le2P\) inside \((\sqrt n,n]\).  Choose any interval \(I\) of length \(\lceil P\rceil\) containing \(n\), and apply (CHLS) to the delta vector \(e_n\).  Since matrix entries are \(0\) or \(1\),
\[
\#\left\{
P<p\le2P:
 d_p(n)>D,\ p\mid b_{n\bmod p}
\right\}
\le
\|\mathsf M_{P,D,I}\|^2.
\tag{42}
\]
Multiplying by \(\log(2P)\) gives
\[
\sum_{\substack{P<p\le2P\\d_p(n)>D\\p\mid b_{n\bmod p}}}
\log p
\ll
\frac{P}{D^\eta}+P^{1-\eta}\log P.
\tag{43}
\]
Summing the dyadic blocks geometrically,
\[
W_n^{(d>D)}
\ll
\frac{n}{D^\eta}+n^{1-\eta}\log n.
\tag{44}
\]
Established fact (5) gives the complementary estimate
\[
W_n^{(d\le D)}
\ll D^2\log(nD).
\tag{45}
\]
Choose, for example,
\[
D=(\log n)^{2/\eta}.
\tag{46}
\]
Then
\[
D^2\log(nD)=n^{o(1)}=o(n),
\qquad
\frac n{D^\eta}=\frac n{(\log n)^2}=o(n),
\]
and
\[
n^{1-\eta}\log n=o(n).
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{W_n=o(n).}
\tag{47}
\]
By established facts (1)--(2), this proves the desired all-index Apéry GCD estimate.

So (CHLS) is strong enough, has the correct quantifiers, and interfaces exactly with the already proved small-order theorem.

## Why Theorem 3 is relevant to CHLS rather than cosmetic

A proof of (CHLS) cannot come from “fixed conductor implies random traces”; the project already has counterexamples to that inference.  The intended leverage is more specific:

- the bad event has the exact rank-two unit-root interpretation (38)--(39);
- Theorem 1 makes its Hasse square-root divisor reduced, so the defining-characteristic degeneration is spread across \(\asymp p\) *distinct* tame cubic critical points rather than concentrated in multiplicity;
- Theorem 2 packages the marked coefficient obstruction into one degree-\(p-1\) tame cover with a completely known passport;
- Theorem 3 says that in the large-order range every imprimitive quotient has outer degree at most \(P/D\), and for \(d>2\) must be odd; a nontrivial quotient also forces a packet of at least \(D\) distinct cubic critical points with the same critical value;
- equation (37) warns that the sieve must use the cover/rank-two trace as a whole, not count the individual roots as independent congruences.

This is exactly the kind of information on which a defining-characteristic horizontal large sieve can distinguish the Apéry family from a generic diagonal.

# 6. Audit against the five established facts

### Facts (1) and (2): exact target

The route never replaces the target by a zero-count statement.  It starts with the exact event
\[
p\mid b_{n\bmod p}
\]
and keeps the target residue \(r=n\bmod p\) in the nonhomogeneous operator \(\vartheta-n\).  If (CHLS) is proved, (42)--(47) give the required pointwise \(W_n=o(n)\) for every \(n\).

### Fact (3): the top-half carrier

Nothing here assumes that the nonlinear carrier \(C_n\) has controlled total height, nor does it repackage \(C_n\).  The cover is attached prime-by-prime to the exact residue event.  Thus the fact that only contamination of \(C_n\) is presently controlled is irrelevant to the validity of Theorems 1--3 and remains fully respected.

### Fact (4): generic P-recursive/resultant/monodromy obstructions

The positive input is specifically Apéry:

1. the CFVZ quadratic-times-square Hasse factorization;
2. the two explicit universal second-order square-root operators \(L_\pm\);
3. the exact toric/hypergeometric marked trace already built in the repository.

Moreover, (37) explicitly proves that the naive ordinary resultant route gives no gain: it is just \(b_r^{M_p}\).  No fixed-width recurrence, fixed-depth CRT jet, or generic monodromy theorem is being invoked to conclude anti-concentration.  The proposed final step is explicitly a **defining-characteristic moving-character** estimate with stronger operator-norm quantifiers.

### Fact (5): small versus large Kummer order

This is where the route fits best.  The small-order range \(d\le D\) is left to the already proved
\[
O(D^2\log(nD))
\]
mass estimate.  In the complementary range the *same* number \(d\) acquires an exact geometric meaning:
\[
\frac{p-1}{d}=\gcd(\text{the two pole indices of }H_{p,r}).
\]
Theorem 3 then bounds every imprimitive outer degree by \((p-1)/d\) and forces inner degree at least \(d\).  Thus increasing \(d\) genuinely strengthens the geometry, instead of merely moving the hard set elsewhere.

The Poisson-looking numerical zero distribution is not used anywhere in the proofs.

# 7. Central-binomial counterexample audit

The mechanism above does **not** apply generically to diagonals.  The central binomial sequence gives the clean comparison.

For odd \(p\), let
\[
c_m=\binom{2m}{m}.
\]
Kummer's carry criterion gives
\[
c_m\equiv0\pmod p
\qquad\left(\frac{p+1}{2}\le m\le p-1\right),
\]
and for \(0\le m\le(p-1)/2\),
\[
\binom{2m}{m}
\equiv(-4)^m\binom{(p-1)/2}{m}\pmod p.
\]
Hence its Hasse truncation is exactly
\[
\boxed{
\sum_{m=0}^{p-1}\binom{2m}{m}T^m
=(1-4T)^{(p-1)/2}
\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_p[T].
}
\tag{48}
\]
So the entire geometric critical divisor is concentrated at **one** point with multiplicity \((p-1)/2\).  There is no analogue of Theorem 1's \(\asymp p\) distinct square-root roots and hence no analogue of the tame cubic passport in Theorem 2.

This is precisely why the argument is not a disguised “diagonal sequences should have few smooth primes” principle.  The central-binomial counterexample fails at the Apéry-specific squarefree-Hasse-square-root input before the large sieve is even formulated.

At the same time, equation (37) prevents us from overclaiming: distinct Apéry critical points alone do not prove independence.  Their arithmetic correlation must be handled through the full Cartier–Hurwitz/rank-two object.
