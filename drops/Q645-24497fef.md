# Galois group of the symmetric Apéry polynomial

## Verdict

The proposed statement is impossible in its literal form.

For a polynomial over a finite field, its splitting field is again a finite
field.  Hence the Galois group of the splitting field of a single
\(Q_p(U)\in\mathbf F_p[U]\) is **cyclic**, generated by arithmetic Frobenius.
For \(d\ge 4\), it can neither be \(S_d\) nor contain \(A_d\).

There is a nearby, meaningful \(S_d\)-question:

\[
\operatorname{Gal}\bigl(Q_p(U)-T/\overline{\mathbf F}_p(T)\bigr)
\stackrel{?}=S_{d_p},
\qquad d_p:=\deg Q_p.
\]

This is the permutation monodromy of the polynomial map
\(Q_p:\mathbf A^1_U\to\mathbf A^1_T\).  It governs the factorization of
\(Q_p(U)-t\) **as \(t\) varies**.  Even if this group is \(S_{d_p}\) for every
large \(p\), it says essentially nothing about the single distinguished fiber
\(t=0\) as \(p\) varies.

The other principal conclusions are:

1. Reflection proves only
   \(\deg Q_p\le (p-1)/2\), not equality.  The displayed factorizations at
   \(p=37\) and \(p=127\) have total degree one less than the claimed degree,
   if those lists are complete.
2. The observed Poisson laws have a direct random-interpolation explanation
   that does not use an \(S_d\) splitting group: random roots in all of
   \(\mathbf F_p\) tend to Poisson\((1)\), while roots in the half-sized
   quadratic-image set corresponding to reflection pairs tend to
   Poisson\((1/2)\).
3. \(Q_p\) is not naturally the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius on the
   Apéry K3 motive.  There are tautological multiplication and permutation
   representations attached to \(Q_p\), but neither identifies it with a
   Picard--Fuchs Frobenius polynomial.
4. The classical Apéry Picard--Fuchs system used in this repository is a
   rank-three symmetric-square system of \(SO_3/PSL_2\) type, not a full
   rank-four \(Sp_4\) system.  The repository's own exact K3/elliptic
   decomposition makes this explicit.
5. Sato--Tate equidistribution for a fixed-rank \(Sp_4\), \(SO_3\), or
   \(GL_2\) local system does not give the factorization statistics of this
   degree-\(\asymp p\) interpolation polynomial.  The correct bridge from the
   geometric parameter to the coefficient index is the finite-field Mellin
   transform already established in the repository.

So the answer to the literal question is **no**, for a structural reason.  A
corrected \(S_d\)-monodromy theorem is plausible and testable, but it is a
separate theorem and would not prove AFP.

## 1. The symmetric quotient polynomial has degree at most \((p-1)/2\)

Let

\[
\sigma(R)=-1-R,
\qquad
u(R)=-R(R+1).
\]

Modulo \(p\), the reflection \(r\mapsto p-1-r\) is exactly
\(R\mapsto-1-R\), and the variable in the question is

\[
u(r)=r(p-1-r)=-r(r+1).
\]

Let \(B_p(R)\) be the unique polynomial of degree at most \(p-1\) satisfying

\[
B_p(r)=b_r\qquad(r\in\mathbf F_p).
\]

The reflection law gives equality of functions
\(B_p(\sigma(R))=B_p(R)\) on all \(p\) points.  Their difference has degree at
most \(p-1\), so it is the zero polynomial.  Since \(p\) is odd,

\[
\mathbf F_p[R]^{\langle\sigma\rangle}
 =\mathbf F_p[R(R+1)]
 =\mathbf F_p[\nu].
\]

Consequently there is a unique \(Q_p(U)\) with

\[
B_p(R)=Q_p(-R(R+1)),
\qquad
\deg Q_p\le d:=\frac{p-1}{2}.
\tag{1.1}
\]

Reflection alone does not prove that the top coefficient is nonzero.
Indeed, there is a useful exact formula for it.

### Lemma 1.1: the leading coefficient

If \(c_{p,d}=[U^d]Q_p(U)\), then

\[
\boxed{
 c_{p,d}=(-1)^{d+1}\sum_{r\in\mathbf F_p}b_r.
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

#### Proof

In \(B_p(R)=Q_p(-R(R+1))\), only the term \(c_{p,d}U^d\) can contribute to
\(R^{p-1}=R^{2d}\), and its contribution is

\[
[R^{p-1}]B_p=(-1)^d c_{p,d}.
\]

For every polynomial \(F(R)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}a_jR^j\) of degree at most
\(p-1\), power-sum orthogonality gives

\[
\sum_{r\in\mathbf F_p}F(r)=-a_{p-1},
\]

because all power sums of degrees \(0,1,\ldots,p-2\) vanish in
\(\mathbf F_p\), while
\(\sum_r r^{p-1}=-1\).  Apply this to \(B_p\).  This yields (1.2).  \(\square\)

Thus

\[
\deg Q_p<d
\iff
\sum_{r\in\mathbf F_p}b_r=0.
\tag{1.3}
\]

This should be recorded in the computation.  In particular, if the listed
factorizations are complete, then

- \(p=37\): \(1+16=17<18\);
- \(p=127\): \(6+27+29=62<63\);

so these are already degree-drop examples.  Any monodromy statement must use
\(d_p=\deg Q_p\), not automatically \((p-1)/2\).

There is also a conceptual warning: interpolation is an invertible linear
change of coordinates.  Evaluating a polynomial of degree at most \(d\) at
the \(d+1\) distinct quotient values gives an isomorphism

\[
\{Q:\deg Q\le d\}
\longleftrightarrow
\{(b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_d)\}.
\tag{1.4}
\]

Therefore saying that \(Q_p\) looks random is essentially equivalent to
saying that the first half of the Apéry row looks random.  Interpolation
repackages the AFP phenomenon; it does not by itself create a new independent
source of randomness.

## 2. Which roots of \(Q_p\) are actual Apéry zero-pairs?

The quotient map satisfies

\[
1-4\nu(r)=(2r+1)^2.
\tag{2.1}
\]

Let

\[
H_p=\{u\in\mathbf F_p:\chi_p(1-4u)=1\}.
\]

Then \(|H_p|=(p-1)/2\), and every \(u\in H_p\) has exactly two preimages
under \(r\mapsto-r(r+1)\).  The additional branch value \(u=1/4\) has the
single preimage \(r=(p-1)/2\).  Hence, writing

\[
P_p=\#\{u\in H_p:Q_p(u)=0\},
\qquad
\varepsilon_p=\mathbf 1_{Q_p(1/4)=0},
\]

one has

\[
\boxed{Z_p=2P_p+\varepsilon_p.}
\tag{2.2}
\]

Also \(Q_p(0)=b_0=1\), so the endpoint pair \(r=0,p-1\) never contributes.
If the central singleton is known to be nonzero in the range under study,
then \(Z_p=2P_p\).

This distinction matters: a linear factor \(U-u\) of \(Q_p\) with
\(\chi_p(1-4u)=-1\) is a genuine \(\mathbf F_p\)-root of the interpolation
polynomial but has no \(r\in\mathbf F_p\) above it.  It is a “ghost root” from
the point of view of Apéry zeros.  Thus

- all linear factors of \(Q_p\) naturally have mean about \(1\);
- the admissible linear factors in \(H_p\) naturally have mean about \(1/2\).

That is exactly the two empirical laws in the question.

## 3. The splitting-field Galois group of \(Q_p\) is cyclic

### Proposition 3.1

Let \(f\in\mathbf F_q[U]\), and let \(L\) be its splitting field over
\(\mathbf F_q\).  Then

\[
L=\mathbf F_{q^m}
\quad\text{for some }m,
\qquad
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbf F_q)
 =\langle x\mapsto x^q\rangle\cong C_m.
\tag{3.1}
\]

If the squarefree part of \(f\) has irreducible factor degrees
\(e_1,\ldots,e_s\), then

\[
m=\operatorname{lcm}(e_1,\ldots,e_s).
\tag{3.2}
\]

#### Proof

Every finite algebraic extension of a finite field is a finite field.  The
smallest field containing a root of every factor of degree \(e_i\) is
\(\mathbf F_{q^m}\) with \(m=\operatorname{lcm}(e_i)\).  Its automorphism
group over \(\mathbf F_q\) is cyclic, generated by \(q\)-power Frobenius.
\(\square\)

Therefore, assuming the factors in the examples are distinct, the actual
splitting-field groups are:

| \(p\) | factor degrees | \(\operatorname{Gal}(Q_p/\mathbf F_p)\) |
|---:|:---|:---|
| 37 | \(1,16\) | \(C_{16}\) |
| 59 | \(1,1,27\) | \(C_{27}\) |
| 127 | \(6,27,29\) | \(C_{1566}\) |
| 193 | \(1,1,4,4,20,66\) | \(C_{660}\) |

The factor degrees nevertheless define a cycle type in a symmetric group:
Frobenius permutes the geometric roots, and an irreducible factor of degree
\(e\) is an \(e\)-cycle.  But the subgroup generated by this one Frobenius
permutation is cyclic.  This is the precise distinction between

- an **ambient generic monodromy group** \(S_d\), and
- the **specialized finite-field splitting group**, generated by one
  Frobenius element.

The frequently quoted statement that random polynomial factorization looks
like random permutation cycle structure refers to the first viewpoint, not
to the splitting group of an individual polynomial over \(\mathbf F_p\).

## 4. Why the Poisson laws do not require Galois theory

The root statistics have an elementary exact factorial-moment proof.

Let \(F\) be uniformly random in the affine space

\[
\mathcal V_{p,d}=\{F\in\mathbf F_p[U]:\deg F\le d,\ F(0)=1\}.
\]

The condition \(F(0)=1\) mirrors \(Q_p(0)=1\).  Let
\(S\subseteq\mathbf F_p^\times\), \(|S|=h\), and put

\[
R_S(F)=\#\{u\in S:F(u)=0\}.
\]

For every fixed \(k\le d\), evaluations at \(k\) distinct nonzero points are
independent and uniform.  Indeed, the relevant Vandermonde matrix has rank
\(k\).  Thus

\[
\Pr(F(u_1)=\cdots=F(u_k)=0)=p^{-k}
\]

for distinct \(u_i\), and hence

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf E\,(R_S)_k=\frac{(h)_k}{p^k}.
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Here \((x)_k=x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)\).  If \(d\to\infty\) and \(h/p\to\alpha\),
then every fixed factorial moment tends to \(\alpha^k\), the factorial moments
of Poisson\((\alpha)\).  Therefore

\[
R_S\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(\alpha).
\tag{4.2}
\]

Apply this in two ways:

\[
S=\mathbf F_p^\times
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R_S\Rightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1),
\]

and

\[
S=H_p\setminus\{0\}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R_S\Rightarrow\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2).
\]

The same calculation with two disjoint subsets gives joint factorial moments,
so admissible and inadmissible roots are asymptotically independent
Poisson\((1/2)\) variables in the random model.

This explains the computation almost perfectly.  It is, however, still a
**model**, because the Apéry \(Q_p\) is one deterministic polynomial for each
prime.  To turn it into a theorem one must prove, for example,

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}\sum_{p\le X}(P_p)_k
\longrightarrow\left(\frac12\right)^k
\tag{4.3}
\]

for fixed \(k\), or at least the bounded versions needed by the project.
That is a horizontal zero-correlation theorem, not a consequence of the
formal interpolation.

There is also an important project-level consequence.  A Poisson random
variable has unbounded support.  Under an independence heuristic over primes,

\[
\max_{p\le X}P_p
\asymp\frac{\log\pi(X)}{\log\log\pi(X)},
\qquad
\max_{p\le X}Z_p
\asymp\frac{2\log X}{\log\log X}.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Thus random-polynomial behavior is evidence **against** a uniform pointwise
bound \(Z_p=O(1)\).  It supports a bounded mean, bounded fixed moments, and the
Cesàro hypotheses now used in `problems/3.2/proof.tex`.  Any statement in the
manuscript deriving a \(2\log\log X\) maximum from a Poisson tail should be
rechecked: the standard iid Poisson maximum is of order
\(\log n/\log\log n\) for \(n\) samples.

## 5. The meaningful \(S_d\)-object is \(Q_p(U)-T\)

Put \(d_p=\deg Q_p\), and define

\[
G_{p,\mathrm{geom}}
 =\operatorname{Gal}
 \bigl(\operatorname{Spl}(Q_p(U)-T)/
       \overline{\mathbf F}_p(T)\bigr)
 \subseteq S_{d_p},
\tag{5.1}
\]

\[
G_{p,\mathrm{arith}}
 =\operatorname{Gal}
 \bigl(\operatorname{Spl}(Q_p(U)-T)/
       \mathbf F_p(T)\bigr)
 \subseteq S_{d_p}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

These are the geometric and arithmetic permutation-monodromy groups of the
cover \(T=Q_p(U)\).

### A sufficient theorem: the Morse criterion

A polynomial \(f\) of degree \(n\) is Morse if

1. \(f'\) has \(n-1\) simple roots; and
2. the corresponding critical values are pairwise distinct.

When the characteristic is prime to \(2n\), a Morse polynomial has

\[
\operatorname{Gal}(f(U)-T/\overline{k}(T))=S_n.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Indeed, every finite branch inertia group contributes a transposition.  The
cover is connected, and a transitive group generated by transpositions is the
full symmetric group.  In the present setting \(d_p<p\), so tameness is not
the issue.

A convenient computational certificate is

\[
\Delta_p(T)=\operatorname{disc}_U(Q_p(U)-T).
\tag{5.4}
\]

Up to a nonzero constant,

\[
\Delta_p(T)=
\prod_{Q_p'(c)=0}(T-Q_p(c)).
\]

Thus \(Q_p\) is Morse exactly when the expected degree is obtained and

\[
\gcd(\Delta_p,\Delta_p')=1.
\tag{5.5}
\]

There is a weaker useful criterion.

### Proposition 5.1

Suppose \(Q_p\) is indecomposable over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\), and the
cover \(Q_p(U)=T\) has at least one simple branch value.  Then

\[
G_{p,\mathrm{geom}}=S_{d_p}.
\tag{5.6}
\]

#### Proof

Indecomposability of a polynomial is equivalent to primitivity of its
geometric monodromy action.  A simple branch value gives a transposition in
inertia.  A primitive permutation group containing a transposition is
\(S_{d_p}\).  \(\square\)

This gives a concrete program for proving a corrected theorem:

1. determine the actual degree \(d_p\);
2. prove absolute indecomposability of \(Q_p\);
3. prove that \(\Delta_p(T)\) has a simple root, or prove the stronger Morse
   condition.

But these are new global identities involving all of the coefficients
\(b_0,\ldots,b_d\).  Neither Picard--Fuchs monodromy nor the \(2\times2\)
transfer-matrix group automatically proves them.

### Why even \(G_{p,\mathrm{geom}}=S_{d_p}\) does not solve the question

If \(G_{p,\mathrm{geom}}=S_{d_p}\), function-field Chebotarev describes the
factorization of

\[
Q_p(U)-t,
\qquad t\in\mathbf F_p,
\]

as \(t\) varies outside the branch locus.  The cycle type of the Frobenius
class at \(t\) is the factorization type of that shifted polynomial.  It does
not force the particular fiber \(t=0\) to be generic.

Moreover, standard estimates have errors of the form

\[
O_{d_p}(\sqrt p),
\]

with constants controlled by the complexity/genus of the Galois closure.
Here \(d_p\asymp p\), so fixed-degree Chebotarev theorems are not uniform in
the regime required here.  The complexity of an \(S_{d_p}\)-Galois closure
can grow catastrophically.  Consequently, even averaging over all shifts
would require a genuinely large-degree uniform theorem.

Finally, the polynomials \(Q_p\) themselves change with \(p\).  There is no
fixed finite extension of a global function field to which ordinary
Chebotarev across primes can be applied.

## 6. Is \(Q_p\) a Frobenius characteristic polynomial?

Not in the motivic sense suggested in the question.  There are two
canonical-but-tautological constructions worth distinguishing.

### 6.1 Multiplication by \(U\)

Normalize \(Q_p\) to be monic and put

\[
A_p=\mathbf F_p[U]/(Q_p(U)).
\]

Multiplication by \(U\),

\[
m_U:A_p\to A_p,
\]

has characteristic polynomial \(Q_p\).  This is the companion-matrix
construction.  It is not a Frobenius action and contains no extra geometric
information.

### 6.2 Frobenius on the zero-dimensional root scheme

Let

\[
X_p=\operatorname{Spec}
 \mathbf F_p[U]/(Q_p^{\mathrm{sf}}(U)).
\]

If the irreducible factors have degrees \(e_1,\ldots,e_s\), Frobenius acts on
\(X_p(\overline{\mathbf F}_p)\) as cycles of those lengths.  On the
permutation representation one has

\[
\boxed{
\det\bigl(1-T\operatorname{Frob}_p
  \mid H_c^0(X_{p,\overline{\mathbf F}_p},\mathbf Q_\ell)\bigr)
 =\prod_{i=1}^s(1-T^{e_i}).
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
Z(X_p,T)=\prod_i(1-T^{e_i})^{-1}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

This Frobenius polynomial encodes the factorization of \(Q_p\), but it is not
\(Q_p(U)\).  It is constructed after the root scheme is already known.

### 6.3 Degree and symmetry rule out the proposed fixed-rank origin

The primitive Apéry K3 system in this repository has fixed rank three.  The
standard representation of \(Sp_4\) has rank four.  Their Frobenius
characteristic polynomials therefore have degrees three and four,
respectively, independent of \(p\).  In contrast,

\[
\deg Q_p\sim p/2.
\]

Any representation whose Frobenius characteristic polynomial were \(Q_p\)
would have to have rank growing with \(p\), and no such representation has
been identified.

Even hypothetically, a characteristic polynomial coming from a regular
semisimple element of a fixed reductive group has splitting behavior
controlled by the group's Weyl group.  For \(Sp_4\), the Weyl group in the
standard situation is \(W(C_2)\), a dihedral group of order \(8\), and the
eigenvalues have reciprocal-pair constraints.  This is the opposite of an
unconstrained degree-\(p/2\) random polynomial with ambient group \(S_{p/2}\).

## 7. The \(Sp_4\) premise does not match the classical Apéry system in this repository

For

\[
A(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nz^n,
\qquad \theta=z\frac{d}{dz},
\]

the recurrence gives the order-three Apéry operator

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L_A
=\theta^3
-z(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+z^2(\theta+1)^3.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Dwork exhibited this order-three equation as the symmetric square of an
order-two equation.  After the usual determinant normalization, the
rank-two differential Galois group is \(SL_2\), and its symmetric-square
image is

\[
SL_2/\{\pm1\}=PSL_2\cong SO_3.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Dreyfus--Rivoal give a modern differential-Galois verification of this
\(PSL_2\) group.

The repository's arithmetic geometry says the same thing more concretely.
In `problems/3.2/toric_fiber_sym2.tex`, the rank-four Kummer/Asai summand has
a rational Tate line supplied by the six-isogeny graph; its orthogonal
complement is the generic **rank-three** transcendental part of the K3
surface.  The exact trace is a symmetric-square trace in the split case and
an Asai trace in the inert case.  In
`problems/3.2/ORACLE_COMM/chatgpt_q32_fixed_exponent_mellin_attack.md`, the
connected local-system monodromy is explicitly identified as \(SO_3\)-type.

There are two independent reasons full \(Sp_4\) is not the relevant group:

1. a rank-four representation with an invariant Tate line is reducible and
   cannot be the standard irreducible representation of full \(Sp_4\);
2. the polarized primitive \(H^2\) of a K3 surface carries a symmetric cup
   product, so its natural monodromy is orthogonal, not symplectic.

A separate rank-four hypergeometric or convolution sheaf with \(Sp_4\)
monodromy may exist in another realization.  If that is what is intended, it
must be named together with an explicit transform identifying its trace with
\(b_r\).  It is not the classical Picard--Fuchs local system generated by
\(A(z)\), and its group cannot simply be transferred to \(Q_p\).

The \(2\times2\) recurrence transfer group has a similarly limited role.  A
large \(GL_2\) difference-Galois group controls algebraic relations among
solutions of the recurrence.  Taking a particular solution, reducing modulo
\(p\), sampling \(p\) values, quotienting by reflection, interpolating, and
then factoring is a highly nonlinear, \(p\)-dependent operation.  There is
no standard functor sending the difference-Galois group \(GL_2\) to the
permutation group of the roots of \(Q_p\).

## 8. Why Sato--Tate does not give the factorization of \(Q_p\)

Sato--Tate and polynomial-factorization Chebotarev are different kinds of
equidistribution.

A Sato--Tate theorem for a rank-\(r\) local system distributes normalized
Frobenius conjugacy classes in a compact form of an algebraic group such as
\(SO_3\), \(USp_4\), or \(U_r\).  It controls fixed-degree observables such as
traces, eigenangles, and coefficients of a degree-\(r\) characteristic
polynomial.

Factorization of \(Q_p\), on the other hand, is governed by Frobenius in a
finite permutation group acting on \(d_p\asymp p\) roots.  The relevant
ambient group, when it is full, is the \(S_{d_p}\) monodromy of
\(Q_p(U)-T\).  The appropriate theorem is Chebotarev for that cover as \(T\)
varies, not Sato--Tate for the K3 local system.

There is an additional defining-characteristic obstruction.  The desired
event is

\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Complex or \(\ell\)-adic Sato--Tate equidistribution is a weak-distribution
statement at a fixed analytic scale.  Detecting one residue class among
\(p\) classes is a scale-\(1/p\) arithmetic question.  Exact divisibility by
the same prime that is the base characteristic is not detected by ordinary
Sato--Tate test functions.  An algebraic trace can be nonzero in
characteristic zero and still vanish after reduction modulo \(p\).

Katz's finite-field Mellin Sato--Tate theory is much closer to the actual
geometry, because it varies multiplicative characters.  But it still does
not supply the required conclusion:

- all-character equidistribution may ignore one deterministic selected
  character per field, whose relative mass is \(1/(p-1)\);
- unitary/complex equidistribution does not detect reduction at the defining
  prime;
- it controls individual Mellin values, not the nonlinear factorization of
  the interpolation polynomial assembled from all values.

These are exactly the quantifier and defining-prime obstructions already
recorded in
`problems/3.2/ORACLE_COMM/chatgpt_q32_fixed_exponent_mellin_attack.md` and
`problems/3.2/ORACLE_COMM/chatgpt_q32_padic_horizontal_mellin.md`.

## 9. The correct horizontal framework

The relevant objects should be kept separate:

| object | varying variable | natural group | what it controls |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Picard--Fuchs/Gauss--Manin local system | geometric parameter \(z\) or \(a\) | \(PSL_2\cong SO_3\) here | analytic continuation and Frobenius traces of fibers |
| rank-two underlying elliptic system | geometric parameter | \(GL_2/SL_2\) type | the symmetric-square rank-three system |
| recurrence difference module | coefficient index shifted by \(r\mapsto r+1\) | difference-Galois subgroup of \(GL_2\) | algebraic relations among recurrence solutions |
| finite-field Mellin transform | multiplicative character \(\chi=\omega^r\) | convolution/Tannakian monodromy | distribution of Mellin trace values as characters vary |
| polynomial cover \(Q_p(U)-T\) | output shift \(T=t\) | permutation group in \(S_{d_p}\) | factorization of \(Q_p(U)-t\) as \(t\) varies |
| single polynomial \(Q_p(U)\) | no parameter | cyclic Frobenius group | its one observed factorization pattern |

The repository already contains the correct bridge from geometry to the
coefficient index.  In the normalization used there,

\[
M_p(m)=
\sum_{a\in\mathbf F_p^\times}
 \theta_p(a)\,\omega_p(a)^m,
\qquad
M_p(m)\equiv-b_r\pmod{\mathfrak p_{p,m}}
\quad(m\equiv r\bmod p-1).
\tag{9.1}
\]

Thus \(r\) is a **Mellin-character exponent**, not a point of the geometric
parameter line.  Fixing \(z=1/16\) gives one period or one fiber; it does not
turn the coefficient index \(r\) into a geometric specialization variable.
The operation “take the \(r\)-th coefficient” is dual to the geometric
variable, and in the toric realization it is implemented by constant-term
or Mellin extraction.

The construction is best summarized as follows:

```text
rank-three K3 trace sheaf in the fiber parameter a
        |
        | finite-field Mellin transform against omega^r
        v
Apéry coefficient b_r modulo p
        |
        | quotient the exponent labels by r <-> -1-r,
        | then perform p-dependent polynomial interpolation
        v
Q_p(U)
```

The first arrow is geometric and Tannakian.  The second arrow is a
\(p\)-dependent linear-algebra encoding of the list of Mellin coefficients.
There is no reason for monodromy to be preserved by that encoding.

A further subtlety is that the character exponent naturally lives in
\(\mathbf Z/(p-1)\), while \(Q_p\) is formed by treating the integer labels as
points of \(\mathbf F_p\).  This is not a fixed algebraic parameter space as
\(p\) varies.  It is another reason that \(Q_p\) does not automatically form
a compatible family of Frobenius characteristic polynomials.

## 10. What is worth proving or computing next

The \(Q_p\)-experiment is valuable, but it should be split into two logically
different experiments.

### 10.1 Test the generic polynomial-map monodromy

For each prime, record:

1. \(d_p=\deg Q_p\), together with the row sum in (1.2);
2. whether \(Q_p\) is squarefree;
3. \(\Delta_p(T)=\operatorname{disc}_U(Q_p(U)-T)\);
4. whether \(\gcd(\Delta_p,\Delta_p')=1\), the Morse test;
5. if Morse fails, whether \(Q_p\) is indecomposable and \(\Delta_p\) has at
   least one simple root.

This can certify \(G_{p,\mathrm{geom}}=S_{d_p}\) for individual primes.  Then
factor \(Q_p(U)-t\) for many or all \(t\in\mathbf F_p\).  If the shift-family
factorization types match random permutation cycle types, that tests the
correct Chebotarev object.

Crucially, compare the distinguished \(t=0\) fiber with the full shift
family.  It may be generic, or it may occupy a thin arithmetically special
section.  Ambient \(S_{d_p}\) monodromy alone cannot decide which.

### 10.2 Test the actual AFP/mean-zero theorem

For the Apéry problem, the relevant random variable is

\[
P_p=\#\{u\in H_p:Q_p(u)=0\}=Z_p/2
\]

up to the central branch correction.  Compute its factorial moments across
primes:

\[
\frac1{\pi(X)}\sum_{p\le X}(P_p)_k,
\qquad k=1,2,3,4,\ldots.
\tag{10.1}
\]

The random model predicts \((1/2)^k\).  Even the bounds

\[
\sum_{p\le X}(P_p)_k\ll_k\pi(X)
\tag{10.2}
\]

would be structurally aligned with the bounded-moment hypotheses in the
current proof strategy.  This is much more relevant than proving generic
\(S_{d_p}\) monodromy of the shifted polynomial map.

The geometric route to (10.2) remains the Mellin/Kummer route: one needs
mod-\(p\), defining-characteristic anti-concentration or correlation estimates
for the Mellin coefficients.  Ordinary Sato--Tate and fixed-degree
Chebotarev do not reach that scale.

## Direct answers to the three numbered questions

### 1. Is \(Q_p(U)\) the characteristic polynomial of a Frobenius element?

**No natural such realization is presently present.**  It is tautologically
the characteristic polynomial of multiplication by \(U\) on
\(\mathbf F_p[U]/(Q_p)\).  Frobenius on the root scheme instead has
characteristic polynomial \(\prod_i(1-T^{e_i})\), which records factor
degrees.  Neither is the fixed-rank Frobenius polynomial of the Apéry K3
system.

### 2. Does \(Sp_4\) Sato--Tate give the factorization statistics of \(Q_p\)?

**No.**  First, the relevant classical Apéry system in this repository is
rank-three \(SO_3/PSL_2\)-type, not full \(Sp_4\).  Second, Sato--Tate controls
fixed-rank Frobenius conjugacy classes and their traces/eigenangles.  The
factorization of \(Q_p\) is a degree-\(\asymp p\) finite-permutation problem.
The relevant theorem would be Chebotarev for the cover \(Q_p(U)-T\) as \(T\)
varies, and even that does not control \(T=0\) across \(p\).

### 3. What is the correct horizontal framework?

The coefficient index \(r\) is a Mellin-character exponent.  The correct
geometric object is the Mellin transform of the fixed rank-three K3 trace
sheaf, together with a defining-characteristic reduction theorem.  The
recurrence can also be studied through difference Galois theory.  The
polynomial \(Q_p\) is a useful finite-field interpolation diagnostic, while
\(Q_p(U)-T\) has its own permutation monodromy.  These are three distinct
Galois theories; there is no automatic homomorphism from the Picard--Fuchs or
recurrence group to the permutation group of \(Q_p\)'s roots.

---

## Bottom line

The random factorization computation is strong evidence for the **statistical
model** behind the current Cesàro/moment approach.  It is not evidence that
the splitting-field Galois group of \(Q_p\) is \(S_d\), because that group is
always cyclic.  A corrected theorem asserting

\[
\operatorname{Gal}(Q_p(U)-T/\overline{\mathbf F}_p(T))=S_{d_p}
\]

can be attacked via indecomposability and simple branching, or via the
stronger Morse criterion.  Such a result would explain factorization under
additive shifts \(T=t\), but it would not prove the distinguished zero-fiber
statistics and would not yield a pointwise \(O(1)\) zero bound.

For P3.2, the useful target remains bounded prime-averaged factorial moments
or the corresponding horizontal Mellin anti-concentration estimate.  The
Poisson\((1/2)\) model supports exactly that target and simultaneously predicts
that a uniform AFP bound is false.
