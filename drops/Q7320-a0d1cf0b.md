# Top-shell anti-diagonal continuants: exact factorization and a linear-height no-go

## Verdict

Let

\[
\mathcal T_n=\{p\text{ prime}: n/2<p\le n,\ p\mid b_{n-p}\},
\qquad
\mathfrak P_n=\prod_{p\in\mathcal T_n}p.
\]

For a target prime put \(j=n-p\) and

\[
k=p-1-j=n-2j-1.
\]

The proposed two-zero reduction is correct in both orientations:

\[
p\mid b_j,\ b_k,\ \gcd(b_j,b_k),
\]

and, when \(j\ne k\),

\[
p\mid N_{|j-k|}(\min(j,k)).
\]

The special relation \(j+k=p-1\), however, makes this continuant divisibility **universal rather than hit-sensitive**. The exact polynomial identity is

\[
\boxed{N_h(X)=(2X+h+1)Q_h(X)\qquad(h\text{ even}),}
\]

where \(Q_h\in\mathbb Z[X]\), the displayed factor has multiplicity exactly one in \(\mathbb Q[X]\), and

\[
Q_h(-h-1-X)=Q_h(X).
\]

For the top-shell pair

\[
u=\min(j,k),\qquad h=|j-k|,
\]

one has \(h\) even and

\[
2u+h+1=j+k+1=p.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
N_{|n-3j-1|}\!\left(\min(j,n-2j-1)\right)
=(n-j)Q_{|n-3j-1|}\!\left(\min(j,n-2j-1)\right).
}
\]

This identity holds for every admissible odd integer \(n-j\), whether or not it is prime and whether or not \(b_j\) vanishes. Thus the factor \(p\) in the proposed carrier is exactly the anti-diagonal reflection factor; it supplies no additional information beyond the linear relation.

After dividing this universal factor, the first possible extra condition is genuinely first order:

\[
p\mid Q_h(u)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
b_{p-1-u}\equiv b_u\pmod {p^2}
\]

when \(p\mid b_u\). A top-shell zero modulo \(p\) does not imply this mod-\(p^2\) reflection congruence. The exact audit found no such extra factor among all 588 reflected Apéry-zero pairs for primes \(p\le10{,}000\); that is finite evidence only, not a theorem of nonoccurrence.

There is therefore no \(\exp(o(n))\)-height carrier obtained by aggregating the forced continuant factors. Indeed, the same factor occurs for every candidate prime \(p\in(n/2,n]\), and

\[
\log\prod_{n/2<p\le n}p
=\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(n/2)
=\frac n2+o(n).
\]

A fixed number of nonzero integers collectively carrying all these universal factors must have total logarithmic height at least \(n/2+o(n)\). This is the explicit linear-height obstruction.

This does **not** prove that the actual target product \(\mathfrak P_n\) cannot be \(\exp(o(n))\): the target set may be sparse. It proves that reflection plus this specialized continuant cannot establish such a bound, because its divisibility does not distinguish targets from non-target candidate primes.

# 1. Recurrence and exact continuant identity

Write

\[
P(m)=(2m+1)(17m^2+17m+5)
=34m^3+51m^2+27m+5.
\]

The Apéry numbers satisfy

\[
(m+1)^3b_{m+1}=P(m)b_m-m^3b_{m-1},
\qquad b_0=1,\quad b_1=5. \tag{1}
\]

Let \(c_m\) be the second divided solution with

\[
c_0=0,\qquad c_1=1,
\]

and the same recurrence. Although \(c_m\) need not be integral, its denominator divides \((m!)^3\), so it is \(p\)-integral for every \(m<p\).

Equivalently, put

\[
B_m=(m!)^3b_m,\qquad D_m=(m!)^3c_m.
\]

Then both \(B,D\) satisfy the division-free recurrence

\[
Y_{m+1}=P(m)Y_m-m^6Y_{m-1}, \tag{2}
\]

with

\[
(B_0,B_1)=(1,5),\qquad(D_0,D_1)=(0,1).
\]

Define the gap continuants by

\[
N_0(X)=0,\qquad N_1(X)=1,
\]

\[
N_{h+1}(X)=P(X+h)N_h(X)-(X+h)^6N_{h-1}(X). \tag{3}
\]

## Proposition 1: exact two-endpoint determinant

For integers \(u\ge0\), \(h\ge0\), and \(v=u+h\),

\[
\boxed{
N_h(u)=\left(\prod_{r=1}^{h}(u+r)^3\right)
       \bigl(b_uc_v-c_ub_v\bigr).
} \tag{4}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
B_uD_v-D_uB_v=(u!)^6N_h(u).
} \tag{5}
\]

### Proof

The divided Casoratian

\[
W_m=b_mc_{m+1}-b_{m+1}c_m
\]

satisfies, by (1),

\[
W_m=\left(\frac m{m+1}\right)^3W_{m-1}.
\]

Since \(W_0=1\),

\[
W_m=\frac1{(m+1)^3}. \tag{6}
\]

Let the right side of (4) be \(G_h(u)\). Then \(G_0=0\), and (6) gives \(G_1=1\). Applying (1) to the endpoint \(u+h\) for both \(b\) and \(c\) gives

\[
G_{h+1}(u)=P(u+h)G_h(u)-(u+h)^6G_{h-1}(u).
\]

Thus \(G_h=N_h\) by the same initial values and recurrence. Multiplying (4) by \((u!)^6\) gives (5). \(\square\)

## Corollary 2: generic two-zero divisibility

Let \(p\) be prime and \(0\le u<v<p\). If

\[
p\mid b_u,\qquad p\mid b_v,
\]

then

\[
\boxed{p\mid N_{v-u}(u).} \tag{7}
\]

Indeed, \(u!\) is a unit modulo \(p\), while both \(B_u\) and \(B_v\) vanish modulo \(p\), so (5) vanishes modulo \(p\).

This is the **generic gcd fact**: two actual Apéry zeros in the same characteristic produce a continuant divisor. No relation between \(u+v\) and \(p\) is needed.

# 2. Apéry reflection and the two top-shell orientations

For every prime \(p\) and \(0\le r<p\),

\[
\boxed{b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.} \tag{8}
\]

For completeness, this follows directly from the recurrence. First,

\[
b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p,\qquad b_{p-2}\equiv5\pmod p. \tag{9}
\]

For \(b_{p-1}\), every summand with binomial index at least one contains the factor
\(\binom{p-1+r}{r}\equiv0\pmod p\). For \(b_{p-2}\), only indices zero and one survive modulo \(p\), giving \(1+4=5\).

Now define \(\widetilde b_r=b_{p-1-r}\). Since

\[
P(-1-r)=-P(r), \tag{10}
\]

reversing (1) shows that \(\widetilde b_r\) satisfies the same recurrence as \(b_r\) for \(0<r<p-1\). The initial values (9) are \(\widetilde b_0=1\), \(\widetilde b_1=5\), so uniqueness gives (8).

Now suppose

\[
p=n-j>\frac n2,\qquad p\mid b_j.
\]

Then \(0\le j<p\). Put

\[
k=p-1-j=n-2j-1.
\]

The strict top-shell inequality ensures \(0\le k<p\). Reflection gives

\[
p\mid b_k,
\]

and hence

\[
\boxed{p\mid\gcd(b_j,b_k).} \tag{11}
\]

The primes \(2,3\) yield no top-shell hit because \(b_0=1\), \(b_1=5\), and \(b_2=73\), so every target prime considered below is odd.

## Orientation A: \(j<k\)

This is equivalent to

\[
n-3j-1>0.
\]

Applying Corollary 2 with lower endpoint \(j\) gives

\[
\boxed{
 p\mid N_{k-j}(j)=N_{n-3j-1}(j).
} \tag{12}
\]

## Orientation B: \(k<j\)

This is equivalent to

\[
3j+1-n>0.
\]

The lower endpoint is now \(k=n-2j-1\), so

\[
\boxed{
 p\mid N_{j-k}(k)
 =N_{3j+1-n}(n-2j-1).
} \tag{13}
\]

## Central case: \(j=k\)

Here

\[
n=3j+1,\qquad p=2j+1,\qquad h=0.
\]

There is only one zero index, not a distinct two-zero gap. The convention \(N_0=0\) makes divisibility vacuous. This case must be separated from any nonzero carrier argument.

Combining the two noncentral orientations, define

\[
u=\min(j,k),\qquad h=|j-k|=|n-3j-1|.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{p\mid N_h(u).} \tag{14}
\]

Up to this point, (14) is merely the generic two-zero fact applied after reflection. The next section identifies what is special about the anti-diagonal \(j+k=p-1\).

# 3. Exact polynomial reflection and the universal center factor

For \(h\ge2\), let \(T_h(X)\) be the symmetric \((h-1)\times(h-1)\) tridiagonal matrix

\[
T_h(X)=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(X+1)&(X+2)^3&&&0\\
(X+2)^3&P(X+2)&(X+3)^3&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&(X+h-2)^3&P(X+h-2)&(X+h-1)^3\\
0&&&(X+h-1)^3&P(X+h-1)
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Expansion along the last row gives

\[
\det T_h(X)=N_h(X). \tag{15}
\]

Let \(J\) be the reversal matrix. Using (10) and reversing the off-diagonal entries gives the exact matrix identity

\[
T_h(-h-1-X)=-JT_h(X)J.
\]

Taking determinants proves:

## Proposition 3: continuant reflection

For every \(h\ge1\),

\[
\boxed{
N_h(-h-1-X)=(-1)^{h-1}N_h(X).
} \tag{16}
\]

Put

\[
L_h(X)=2X+h+1.
\]

The involution in (16) sends \(L_h\) to \(-L_h\). Therefore, over \(\mathbb Q\),

\[
N_h(X)=
\begin{cases}
A_h(L_h(X)^2),&h\text{ odd},\\[2mm]
L_h(X)\,\widetilde A_h(L_h(X)^2),&h\text{ even},
\end{cases} \tag{17}
\]

for suitable rational polynomials \(A_h,\widetilde A_h\).

When \(h\) is even, the fixed point \(X=-(h+1)/2\) is a root. Since \(L_h\) is primitive in \(\mathbb Z[X]\), Gauss's lemma gives an integral quotient:

## Theorem 4: exact even-gap factorization

For every even \(h\ge2\), there is a unique \(Q_h\in\mathbb Z[X]\) such that

\[
\boxed{N_h(X)=L_h(X)Q_h(X).} \tag{18}
\]

Moreover,

\[
\boxed{Q_h(-h-1-X)=Q_h(X),} \tag{19}
\]

and the factor \(L_h\) has multiplicity exactly one in \(N_h\).

### Proof of the exact multiplicity

Let \(X_0=-(h+1)/2\). At \(X_0\), all off-diagonal entries of \(T_h(X_0)\) are nonzero half-integer cubes. Hence \(T_h(X_0)\) is an irreducible real symmetric tridiagonal matrix, whose eigenvalues are simple. Since its determinant is zero, its kernel is one-dimensional.

It remains to rule out tangential contact of the zero eigenvalue. Write

\[
z_i=X_0+i+\frac12=i-\frac h2.
\]

Then

\[
P'(X_0+i)=102z_i^2+\frac32,
\]

while the derivative of the off-diagonal entry joining rows \(i,i+1\) is

\[
w_i=3\left(z_i+\frac12\right)^2>0.
\]

Conjugate \(T_h'(X_0)\) by the alternating-sign diagonal matrix. Its off-diagonal entries become \(-w_i\). For an interior row,

\[
P'(X_0+i)-w_{i-1}-w_i=96z_i^2\ge0,
\]

with strict inequality away from the central row; the boundary remainders are also positive. Thus its quadratic form is

\[
\sum_i w_i(y_i-y_{i+1})^2+\sum_i r_i y_i^2,
\qquad r_i\ge0,
\]

with a connected edge graph and at least one \(r_i>0\). It is positive definite. Therefore, for a nonzero kernel vector \(v\),

\[
v^TT_h'(X_0)v>0.
\]

The determinant derivative is nonzero, so the root \(X_0\), and hence the factor \(L_h\), is simple. \(\square\)

## Degree and leading coefficient

Let \(\ell_h\) be the leading coefficient of \(N_h\), with \(\ell_0=0\), \(\ell_1=1\). Comparing the top-degree terms in (3) gives

\[
\ell_{h+1}=34\ell_h-\ell_{h-1}. \tag{20}
\]

Hence

\[
\ell_h=U_{h-1}(17)
=\frac{(17+12\sqrt2)^h-(17-12\sqrt2)^h}{24\sqrt2}>0. \tag{21}
\]

There is no degree cancellation, and

\[
\boxed{\deg N_h=3(h-1).} \tag{22}
\]

For even \(h\),

\[
\boxed{\deg Q_h=3h-4.} \tag{23}
\]

The first factorization is

\[
N_2(X)=P(X+1)
=(2X+3)(17X^2+51X+39). \tag{24}
\]

The exact PARI audit found, over \(\mathbb Q\),

\[
N_4(X)=2(2X+5)R_8(X),
\]

where

\[
\begin{aligned}
R_8(X)={}&9809X^8+196180X^7+1688435X^6+8160775X^5\\
&+24211565X^4+45131275X^3+51613735X^2\\
&+33123050X+9139756,
\end{aligned}
\]

and \(R_8\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). The primitive quotients for \(h=6,8\) were also irreducible in that finite computation:

\[
N_6=(2X+7)R_{14},\qquad
N_8=4(2X+9)R_{20}.
\]

No general irreducibility claim is made. The full exact coefficients are in `problems/3.2/research/q7320/factor.txt` on the audit branch.

# 4. Specialization to the top-shell anti-diagonal

For a noncentral top-shell pair, recall

\[
u=\min(j,k),\qquad h=|j-k|,\qquad j+k=p-1.
\]

Since \(p\) is odd,

\[
h=p-1-2u
\]

is even. More importantly,

\[
L_h(u)=2u+h+1=j+k+1=p=n-j. \tag{25}
\]

Substituting (25) into Theorem 4 yields the exact integer identity

\[
\boxed{N_h(u)=pQ_h(u).} \tag{26}
\]

In the two orientations this reads as follows.

### If \(j<k\)

\[
2j+(n-3j-1)+1=n-j=p,
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
N_{n-3j-1}(j)=(n-j)Q_{n-3j-1}(j).
} \tag{27}
\]

### If \(k<j\)

\[
2(n-2j-1)+(3j+1-n)+1=n-j=p,
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
N_{3j+1-n}(n-2j-1)
=(n-j)Q_{3j+1-n}(n-2j-1).
} \tag{28}
\]

Thus (12) and (13) are consequences of the integer factorization (27)--(28), without using \(p\mid b_j\).

This is the exact point at which the **special anti-diagonal relation differs from the generic gcd fact**:

* For arbitrary \(u<v<p\), the condition \(p\mid N_{v-u}(u)\) records a projective-state collision and follows from two actual zeros.
* On \(u+v=p-1\), the same divisibility is forced identically by \(p=2u+(v-u)+1\). It is present for every pair on that anti-diagonal.

There is a useful fully centered form. Since

\[
h=|j-k|=|3p-2n-1|,\qquad
u=\frac{p-1-h}{2},
\]

and \(Q_h(X)\) is an even polynomial in \(L_h(X)\) over \(\mathbb Q\), there is \(S_h\in\mathbb Q[Y]\) such that

\[
\boxed{
N_h(u)=pS_h(p^2).
} \tag{29}
\]

The index \(h=|3p-2n-1|\) varies with \(p\), however; (29) does not turn the family into evaluations of one fixed polynomial.

# 5. What remains after dividing by the universal factor

The quotient \(Q_h(u)\) is where a genuinely new target-prime factor would have to occur. It has an exact mod-\(p\) interpretation in terms of first-order reflection.

## Lemma 5: every divided solution reflects modulo \(p\)

Let \(p\) be odd. On the two-dimensional space of \(p\)-integral solutions of (1) over \(\mathbb F_p\), define

\[
(Ry)_m=y_{p-1-m}.
\]

Equation (10) shows that \(R\) preserves the solution space, and \(R^2=1\). The Apéry solution is fixed by (8).

For two solutions \(y,z\), their Casoratian satisfies

\[
W_{p-2}=\left(\prod_{m=1}^{p-2}\frac m{m+1}\right)^3W_0
=\frac1{(p-1)^3}W_0=-W_0.
\]

Reversing the endpoint order contributes another minus sign, so

\[
\det R=1.
\]

Since \(R^2=1\) and \(p\) is odd, \(R\) is diagonalizable with eigenvalues in \(\{1,-1\}\). It has one eigenvalue \(1\) and determinant \(1\), so both eigenvalues are \(1\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{y_{p-1-m}\equiv y_m\pmod p} \tag{30}
\]

for every divided solution, in particular for both \(b\) and \(c\).

## Proposition 6: quotient/reflection-defect identity

Let

\[
v=u+h=p-1-u,\qquad
F_{u,v}=\left(\frac{v!}{u!}\right)^3.
\]

This is a unit modulo \(p\). Define the first reflection defects in the localized ring \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}\) by

\[
\delta_b=\frac{b_v-b_u}{p}\pmod p,
\qquad
\delta_c=\frac{c_v-c_u}{p}\pmod p. \tag{31}
\]

These quotients are well defined by (30). Combining (4), (18), and \(L_h(u)=p\), and expanding modulo \(p^2\), gives

\[
\boxed{
Q_h(u)\equiv
F_{u,v}\bigl(b_u\delta_c-c_u\delta_b\bigr)\pmod p.
} \tag{32}
\]

If \(p\mid b_u\), then both endpoints are zeros modulo \(p\), and (32) reduces to

\[
\boxed{
Q_h(u)\equiv-F_{u,v}c_u\delta_b\pmod p.
} \tag{33}
\]

At a zero, \(c_u\not\equiv0\pmod p\): from (6),

\[
-b_{u+1}c_u\equiv(u+1)^{-3}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Therefore all factors multiplying \(\delta_b\) in (33) are units, and

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p\mid Q_h(u)
&\Longleftrightarrow p^2\mid N_h(u)\\
&\Longleftrightarrow b_{p-1-u}\equiv b_u\pmod {p^2}.
\end{aligned}
} \tag{34}
\]

This is not the assertion \(p^2\mid b_u\). It says that the two reflected multiples of \(p\) have the same first \(p\)-adic coefficient.

Equation (34) isolates the exact non-tautological residue of the route. A modulo-\(p\) top-shell hit forces the first factor in (26), but it does not force the quotient to vanish modulo \(p\).

# 6. Height of an individual specialized continuant

Assume \(0\le u<u+h<n\). For \(0\le m<n\),

\[
P(m)\le117n^3,\qquad m^6\le n^6.
\]

Put \(A=118n^3\). An induction in (3), starting with \(N_1=1\) and \(|N_2|\le117n^3\), gives

\[
\boxed{|N_h(u)|\le(118n^3)^{h-1}.} \tag{35}
\]

Indeed, if the bound holds at two consecutive indices, then

\[
117n^3A^{h-1}+n^6A^{h-2}
\le A^h.
\]

For a top-shell specialization, \(N_h(u)=pQ_h(u)\), so

\[
\boxed{
\log\max(1,|Q_h(u)|)
\le(h-1)(3\log n+\log118).
} \tag{36}
\]

The polynomial coefficient height also has the standard continuant bound

\[
\log\|N_h\|_1=O(h\log(2h)), \tag{37}
\]

because the tridiagonal determinant has exponentially many matching terms, each a product of \(O(h)\)-height shifted cubic or sextic polynomials. Evaluating at \(u\le n\) again gives \(O(h\log n)\), consistent with (35).

# 7. Multiplying over all indices is much too large

Let

\[
m=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor,
\qquad
a=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{3}\right\rfloor.
\]

The total gap length over all top-shell indices is exactly

\[
\begin{aligned}
S_n
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}|n-3j-1|\\
&=(2a+1-m)(n-1)+\frac32m(m+1)-3a(a+1)\\
&=\frac5{24}n^2+O(n). \tag{38}
\end{aligned}
\]

Consequently, the naive product of all noncentral specialized continuants or quotients has only the bound

\[
\log\prod_j\max(1,|Q_{h_j}(u_j)|)
=O(n^2\log n). \tag{39}
\]

This is far larger than linear height.

The generic gcd carrier is similarly expensive if multiplied directly. Since

\[
b_r\le(r+1)64^r,
\]

one obtains

\[
\log\prod_{j=0}^{m}\gcd(b_j,b_{n-2j-1})
\le \frac{\log64}{12}n^2+O(n\log n), \tag{40}
\]

using

\[
\sum_{j=0}^{m}\min(j,n-2j-1)=\frac1{12}n^2+O(n).
\]

Thus neither the raw continuant product nor the raw gcd product supplies a small aggregate.

# 8. An exact one-integer gcd carrier, but only of linear height

Define

\[
g_{n,j}=\gcd\bigl(n-j,b_j,b_{n-2j-1}\bigr),
\qquad 0\le j\le\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor,
\]

and

\[
\mathcal A_n=\operatorname{lcm}_{j}g_{n,j}. \tag{41}
\]

Every target prime \(p=n-j\) divides the corresponding \(g_{n,j}\). Since the target primes are distinct,

\[
\boxed{\mathfrak P_n\mid\mathcal A_n.} \tag{42}
\]

Moreover, \(g_{n,j}\mid n-j\), so

\[
\mathcal A_n
\mid\operatorname{lcm}\left(\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor+1,\ldots,n\right)
=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n). \tag{43}
\]

For the equality, take any prime power \(q\le n\). If \(q>n/2\), it is already in the upper half. If \(q\le n/2\), the largest multiple of \(q\) not exceeding \(n\) lies strictly above \(n/2\). Thus every prime-power divisor of \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)\) occurs in the upper-half lcm.

Writing \(\psi\) for the second Chebyshev function,

\[
\boxed{
\log\mathcal A_n\le\psi(n)=n+o(n).
} \tag{44}
\]

This gives a rigorous single carrier of \(\exp(O(n))\) height, but not \(\exp(o(n))\).

The specialized continuant does not improve (41). For every odd noncentral candidate \(n-j\), equation (26) gives \(n-j\mid N_h(u)\), and therefore

\[
\boxed{
\gcd(n-j,b_j,b_k,N_h(u))
=\gcd(n-j,b_j,b_k).
} \tag{45}
\]

For the central case \(N_0=0\), the same equality is immediate. Thus adding the continuant to the local gcd is exactly redundant.

# 9. Explicit linear-height obstruction

Let

\[
\mathcal I_n=\{p\text{ prime}:n/2<p\le n\},
\qquad
\Pi_n=\prod_{p\in\mathcal I_n}p. \tag{46}
\]

For every odd \(p\in\mathcal I_n\), set \(j=n-p\). Except possibly for the one central index, Theorem 4 gives

\[
p\mid N_{|n-3j-1|}\!\left(\min(j,n-2j-1)\right) \tag{47}
\]

without any Apéry-zero hypothesis. The central value is \(N_0=0\), which is even more vacuous.

Now suppose \(E_{n,1},\ldots,E_{n,t}\) are nonzero integers, with fixed \(t\), that aggregate the divisors forced solely by (47), so every noncentral \(p\in\mathcal I_n\) divides at least one \(E_{n,r}\). Then, after omitting at most one prime,

\[
\Pi_n\mid\prod_{r=1}^tE_{n,r}.
\]

By the prime number theorem,

\[
\log\Pi_n
=\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(n/2)
=\frac n2+o(n). \tag{48}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{r=1}^t\log|E_{n,r}|
\ge\frac n2+o(n).
} \tag{49}
\]

In particular,

\[
\boxed{
\max_r\log|E_{n,r}|
\ge\frac{n}{2t}+o(n).
} \tag{50}
\]

So a fixed number of universal anti-diagonal carriers cannot all have \(\exp(o(n))\) height. This is a genuine linear obstruction, not a heuristic size estimate.

There is an exact two-integer encoding at the same linear scale. Choose \(R_n\) by CRT with

\[
R_n\equiv b_{n-p}\pmod p
\qquad(p\in\mathcal I_n),\qquad 0\le R_n<\Pi_n.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\mathfrak P_n=\gcd(R_n,\Pi_n),} \tag{51}
\]

and both integers have logarithmic height at most \(n/2+o(n)\). This shows what a tautological exact carrier can achieve; it does not give a sublinear bound.

## Precise limitation of the no-go

Equations (49)--(50) apply to carriers that retain the **universal anti-diagonal factor**. They do not prove

\[
\log\mathfrak P_n\gg n.
\]

The actual target set could be much smaller than \(\mathcal I_n\). Proving

\[
\log\mathfrak P_n=o(n)
\]

would require a new hit-sensitive statement: for example, a bound on the number of residues \(j\) for which \(p=n-j\mid b_j\), a cross-\(j\) identity, or a controlled first-order defect. The factor \(p\mid N_h(u)\) cannot supply it, because it is true before the zero condition is imposed.

# Final conclusion

The focused route produces a clean theorem, but it is a no-go theorem for the intended carrier:

1. The reflected index \(k=p-1-j\) is a genuine second Apéry zero, and the generic two-zero continuant lemma works in both orientations.
2. On the special anti-diagonal, the resulting continuant has the exact universal factor
   \(2\min(j,k)+|j-k|+1=p\).
3. Therefore \(p\mid N_h(u)\) is not evidence of the hit; it holds for every candidate prime.
4. The quotient is hit-sensitive only through a mod-\(p^2\) reflection defect, which is not forced by \(p\mid b_j\).
5. Aggregating the universal factors necessarily incurs logarithmic height at least \(n/2+o(n)\) across any fixed number of nonzero carriers.
6. A sublinear-height carrier remains possible only if it exploits genuinely new information about the sparse target set, not the reflection continuant itself.
