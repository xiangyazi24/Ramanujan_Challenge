# Function-field analogue of P3.2 via Mason–Stothers: definitive audit

## Executive verdict

There are three different constructions in the question, and they must not be conflated.

1. **The original scalar Apéry recurrence over \(\mathbf F_q(t)\)** with initial values \(b_0=1,b_1=5\) is isotrivial: as long as the recurrence can be solved, every \(b_n\) remains a constant in \(\mathbf F_q\). The variable \(t\) never enters. Moreover, the recurrence ceases to determine the next term at indices \(n\equiv-1\pmod{\operatorname{char}\mathbf F_q}\).
2. **The standard Apéry polynomial**
   \[
   A_n(T)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2T^k
   \]
   is genuinely nonconstant, but it has degree \(n\), not \(2n\), and it does **not** satisfy the scalar Apéry three-term recurrence except after specializing \(T=1\).
3. **The Apéry K3 Picard–Fuchs family** is a genuine geometric object over a parameter curve, but its \(p\)-curvature and monodromy concern variation in the geometric base variable. The sets
   \(Z_p=\{r:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\}\) are coefficient/Mellin zeros in the index variable. Large geometric monodromy does not turn those coefficient zeros into ordinary evaluation roots.

The central positive result is the following.

> For the standard Apéry polynomials over a fixed finite field, a corrected function-field analogue of P3.2 is not merely true: it has a polylogarithmic degree bound. The proof is an exact polynomial Lucas–Frobenius factorization, not Mason–Stothers.

If \(q=\ell^f\), where \(\ell\) is prime, and \(\overline A_n(T)\) is the reduction of \(A_n(T)\) in \(\mathbf F_q[T]\), then

\[
\deg\operatorname{rad}(\overline A_n)\le s_\ell(n),
\]

where \(s_\ell(n)\) is the sum of the base-\(\ell\) digits of \(n\). Hence

\[
\deg\operatorname{rad}(\overline A_n)=O_\ell(\log n).
\]

For the natural function-field lcm

\[
\mathcal L_D(T)
=\operatorname{lcm}\{F(T):F\in\mathbf F_q[T]\text{ monic},\ \deg F\le D\},
\]

one obtains

\[
\deg\gcd(\mathcal L_D,\overline A_n)
\le D\,s_\ell(n).
\]

Taking \(D=\lfloor\log_q n\rfloor\), so that \(\deg\mathcal L_D\asymp q^D\asymp n\), gives

\[
\boxed{
\deg\gcd(\mathcal L_{\lfloor\log_q n\rfloor},\overline A_n)
=O_{q}((\log n)^2)=o(n).
}
\]

Thus a clean function-field theorem is rigorously available. But it is **Frobenius-degenerate**: in fixed characteristic, all distinct factors of \(A_n(T)\) come from the finitely many digit polynomials \(A_0(T),\ldots,A_{\ell-1}(T)\), while only their Frobenius multiplicities grow. This mechanism has no analogue that specializes to the integer value \(A_n(1)=b_n\) across varying ordinary primes.

The correct strategic verdict for P3.2 is therefore:

- the literal \(\mathbf F_q(t)\) formulation is vacuous or ill-defined;
- the corrected Apéry-polynomial function-field theorem is easy and strong, but for the wrong reason;
- Mason–Stothers does not control the required GCD;
- non-nilpotent \(p\)-curvature does not imply small \(z_p\);
- generic function-field control does not specialize to \(T=1\) over \(\mathbf Z\);
- the repository’s current marked-toric construction already reaches the right geometric object, and the remaining missing input is horizontal two-characteristic dispersion, not existence of a function-field model.

# 1. First correct the three proposed models

## 1.1 The scalar recurrence over \(\mathbf F_q(t)\)

Write

\[
P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

The recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}.
\]

As a polynomial identity with known integer values \(b_n\), it may be reduced modulo any characteristic. But it is not a recurrence that can always be solved over an arbitrary ring: to determine \(b_{n+1}\), one must divide by \((n+1)^3\). In characteristic \(\ell\), the step \(n\equiv-1\pmod\ell\) is singular.

Before that singular step, if

\[
b_0=1,\qquad b_1=5
\]

are constants, then every subsequent term obtained from the recurrence is again a constant. The indeterminate \(t\) never occurs. More generally, because the recurrence coefficients are constants with respect to \(t\), any solution with initial data \(y_0(t),y_1(t)\) lies in their two-dimensional constant-coefficient span:

\[
y_n(t)=u_ny_0(t)+v_ny_1(t).
\]

Its \(t\)-degree is bounded by the maximum of the degrees of the two initial values. This is an isotrivial difference system, not a growing polynomial family.

There is a second literal problem. In the field \(\mathbf F_q(t)\), every nonzero element is a unit, so a polynomial gcd is not an invariant until one chooses the coordinate ring \(\mathbf F_q[t]\) or a divisor model. In \(\mathbf F_q[t]\), the images of the integers \(1,\ldots,n\) are constants:

- while they are nonzero, they are units and their lcm is a unit;
- once a multiple of \(\ell\) occurs, the list contains zero, so its lcm is zero or undefined, depending on convention.

If one defines the lcm by reducing the integer \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)\), then for \(n\ge\ell\) it becomes zero. Consequently

\[
\gcd(0,A_n(T))=A_n(T),
\]

whose degree is of order \(n\), not \(o(n)\). Before \(n=\ell\), the gcd with a nonzero constant has degree zero. Thus the literal definition alternates between trivial and maximally degenerate behavior.

It is not a meaningful function-field analogue of the original problem.

## 1.2 The standard Apéry polynomial

Set

\[
A_n(T)=\sum_{k=0}^n
\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2T^k.
\]

This is the standard Apéry polynomial. It satisfies

\[
A_n(1)=b_n,
\qquad
\deg A_n=n,
\qquad
[T^n]A_n(T)=\binom{2n}{n}^2.
\]

The claim \(\deg A_n=2n\) is therefore a typo: the exponent of \(T\) is \(k\le n\).

This polynomial does not satisfy the scalar Apéry recurrence. Already

\[
A_0(T)=1,\qquad
A_1(T)=1+4T,\qquad
A_2(T)=1+36T+36T^2.
\]

At \(n=1\), the scalar recurrence would require

\[
8A_2(T)=117A_1(T)-A_0(T).
\]

But

\[
8A_2(T)=8+288T+288T^2,
\]

whereas

\[
117A_1(T)-A_0(T)=116+468T.
\]

They agree at \(T=1\), as they must, but not as polynomials.

The bivariate generating series \(\sum_nA_n(T)x^n\) is holonomic because its summand is hypergeometric. A geometric Picard–Fuchs realization may be sought, but it is a different differential system from the original one-variable Apéry operator. One may not insert \(T\) into the original recurrence and then invoke its K3 geometry.

## 1.3 The genuine Apéry K3 family

The generating function

\[
F(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nx^n
\]

satisfies the order-three operator

\[
\mathcal L
=
\theta^3
-x(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+x^2(\theta+1)^3,
\qquad \theta=x\frac d{dx}.
\]

This is the Picard–Fuchs operator of the Apéry K3 family. Its differential module has rank three. It can be related to a rank-two system by a symmetric-square construction after the appropriate geometric transformations, but the displayed Apéry operator itself is not a rank-two \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) system.

The variable \(x\) here is the geometric base/Taylor variable. The integer \(n\) labels Taylor coefficients. These are distinct roles. The \(p\)-curvature acts in the \(x\)-direction; it does not act on the discrete coefficient index \(n\).

# 2. A rigorous corrected function-field P3.2 theorem

The standard Apéry polynomials have an exact Lucas property that completely controls their factor support over a fixed finite field.

## Proposition 2.1: polynomial Lucas factorization

Let \(\ell\) be prime. For \(m\ge0\) and \(0\le r<\ell\), one has in \(\mathbf F_\ell[T]\)

\[
\boxed{
\overline A_{\ell m+r}(T)
=\overline A_m(T^\ell)\,\overline A_r(T)
=\overline A_m(T)^\ell\,\overline A_r(T).
}
\]

Consequently, if

\[
n=n_0+n_1\ell+\cdots+n_s\ell^s
\qquad(0\le n_i<\ell),
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
\overline A_n(T)
=\prod_{i=0}^s\overline A_{n_i}(T)^{\ell^i}.
}
\]

### Proof

Write

\[
n=\ell m+r,\qquad k=\ell j+s,\qquad 0\le r,s<\ell.
\]

Lucas gives

\[
\binom nk\equiv\binom mj\binom rs\pmod\ell.
\]

For the second binomial, there are two cases.

If \(r+s<\ell\), then no carry occurs in the lowest digit, and Lucas gives

\[
\binom{n+k}{k}
\equiv
\binom{m+j}{j}\binom{r+s}{s}\pmod\ell.
\]

If \(r+s\ge\ell\), the lowest digit of \(n+k\) is \(r+s-\ell\). Since

\[
r+s-\ell<s
\]

because \(r<\ell\), Lucas gives

\[
\binom{n+k}{k}\equiv0\pmod\ell.
\]

On the digit side, \(\binom{r+s}{s}\equiv0\pmod\ell\) for exactly the same carry reason. Thus in all cases

\[
\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2
\equiv
\left(\binom mj^2\binom{m+j}{j}^2\right)
\left(\binom rs^2\binom{r+s}{s}^2\right)\pmod\ell.
\]

Summing over \(j,s\) and using \(T^{\ell j+s}=T^s(T^\ell)^j\) proves

\[
\overline A_{\ell m+r}(T)
=\overline A_m(T^\ell)\overline A_r(T).
\]

Frobenius gives \(\overline A_m(T^\ell)=\overline A_m(T)^\ell\), and iteration proves the digit factorization. ∎

## Corollary 2.2: logarithmic radical degree

Let

\[
s_\ell(n)=n_0+\cdots+n_s
\]

be the base-\(\ell\) digit sum. Then over every extension \(\mathbf F_q/\mathbf F_\ell\),

\[
\boxed{
\deg\operatorname{rad}(\overline A_n)
\le s_\ell(n)
\le(\ell-1)(1+\lfloor\log_\ell n\rfloor).
}
\]

Indeed, every distinct factor of \(\overline A_n\) divides

\[
\prod_{i=0}^s\overline A_{n_i}(T),
\]

whose degree is at most \(\sum_i n_i=s_\ell(n)\).

In particular, every irreducible factor of every \(\overline A_n\) has degree at most \(\ell-1\). The support of the entire sequence is contained in the fixed finite product

\[
H_\ell(T)=\prod_{d=0}^{\ell-1}\overline A_d(T).
\]

Only multiplicities change with \(n\).

The extreme example is

\[
\overline A_{\ell^j}(T)
=\overline A_1(T)^{\ell^j}
=(1+4T)^{\ell^j}
\]

for odd \(\ell\). Thus a degree-\(\ell^j\) polynomial has only one distinct geometric root.

## Corollary 2.3: a natural function-field lcm bound

For \(D\ge1\), define

\[
\mathcal L_D(T)
=\operatorname{lcm}\{F(T):F\in\mathbf F_q[T]\text{ monic},\ \deg F\le D\}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\mathcal L_D(T)
=\prod_P P(T)^{\lfloor D/\deg P\rfloor},
\]

where \(P\) ranges over monic irreducibles in \(\mathbf F_q[T]\). This is the exact analogue of including every prime power of norm at most \(q^D\). The prime-polynomial theorem gives

\[
\deg\mathcal L_D\asymp q^D.
\]

Factor \(\overline A_n=\prod_PP^{a_P}\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\deg\gcd(\mathcal L_D,\overline A_n)
&=\sum_{P\mid\overline A_n}
\deg P\,\min\left(a_P,\left\lfloor\frac D{\deg P}\right\rfloor\right)\\
&\le\sum_{P\mid\overline A_n}D\\
&\le D\,\deg\operatorname{rad}(\overline A_n)\\
&\le D\,s_\ell(n).
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, with

\[
D=\lfloor\log_q n\rfloor,
\]

one has

\[
\deg\mathcal L_D\asymp n
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\deg\gcd(\mathcal L_D,\overline A_n)
=O_q((\log n)^2)=o(n).
}
\]

Equivalently, in norm language,

\[
\left|\gcd(\mathcal L_D,\overline A_n)\right|_q
=q^{O((\log n)^2)}
=e^{o(n)}.
\]

This is a complete, unconditional function-field analogue.

## Why this theorem does not help the integer problem

The theorem is not a geometric shadow of the desired cross-prime cancellation. It says that in one fixed residue characteristic \(\ell\), the sequence is generated by Frobenius powers of finitely many digit polynomials. The zero support is essentially constant, while multiplicities grow.

The integer problem is the opposite regime: the residue characteristic itself varies with the prime divisor. There is no single fixed finite set of digit polynomials controlling all ordinary primes.

Thus this function-field theorem validates a formal degree analogue but not the mechanism required for P3.2.

# 3. Mason–Stothers: what it actually says in positive characteristic

The positive-characteristic caveat is essential.

Let \(A+B=C\) with \(A,B,C\in K[T]\) pairwise coprime. Mason–Stothers gives

\[
\max(\deg A,\deg B,\deg C)
\le \deg\operatorname{rad}(ABC)-1
\]

provided that \(A',B',C'\) are not all zero. In characteristic \(\ell\), all three derivatives can vanish even for nonconstant polynomials: this means that the three polynomials lie in \(K[T^\ell]\), after a suitable Frobenius descent when the constants permit it.

The elementary identity

\[
T^\ell+1=(T+1)^\ell
\]

shows why the derivative hypothesis cannot be omitted.

For the Apéry polynomials, this is not a peripheral exceptional case. Proposition 2.1 shows that large Frobenius powers are the dominant structure. For example,

\[
\overline A_{\ell^j}(T)=\overline A_1(T)^{\ell^j}.
\]

Any Mason argument must first descend through those Frobenius powers. After descent, it sees only the digit-level core of degree \(O(\log n)\), which is already completely described by Lucas.

## 3.1 Why the scalar recurrence gives no Mason input

The identity

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}+n^3b_{n-1}=P(n)b_n
\]

is an identity among constants with respect to \(T\). It has degree zero in \(T\). Mason–Stothers says nothing.

If one replaces \(b_n\) by \(A_n(T)\), the identity is false, as shown in §1.2.

Moreover, the coefficients \(n^3,(n+1)^3,P(n)\) are constants in \(\mathbf F_q[T]\). When nonzero, they are units and have no nonconstant common polynomial factors with \(A_n(T)\). When zero, the recurrence degenerates. Therefore there is no function-field analogue of “primes dividing the recurrence coefficients” in this model.

## 3.2 Mason removes the gcd one wants to bound

Suppose one did have a genuine polynomial identity

\[
U_n(T)+V_n(T)=W_n(T).
\]

To apply Mason, one first divides by

\[
D_n(T)=\gcd(U_n,V_n,W_n).
\]

The theorem then bounds the degrees of the coprime quotients in terms of their radical. The common divisor \(D_n\)—the object one wanted to bound—has disappeared from the inequality. Extra information is required to recover it.

This is the wrong direction for P3.2. Mason forces the union of distinct zero divisors of a coprime additive triple to be large. P3.2 asks for an upper bound on a selected common or low-norm divisor.

## 3.3 The honest polynomial recurrence in the repository

There is one place where a Mason experiment is formally legitimate: the gap polynomials

\[
N_{h+1}(x)=P(x+h)N_h(x)-(x+h)^6N_{h-1}(x).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
N_{h+1}(x)+(x+h)^6N_{h-1}(x)=P(x+h)N_h(x).
\]

These are genuine polynomial identities in \(x\). But Mason gives no useful power saving. The three terms all have degree \(3h\), while the radical of their product has available degree \(O(h)\). The resulting inequality is of the tautological form

\[
3h\le C h.
\]

It may certify that an identity is not composed entirely of huge repeated powers after suitable coprimality hypotheses, but it does not bound:

- the number of primes for which two \(N_h\)'s acquire a common root after reduction;
- the multiplicity with which one residue serves as a root for many moving gaps;
- the moving-target count \(n\bmod p\in Z_p\).

The repository’s renewal identities, root strip, special-point evaluations, and resultant budgets are much sharper for these questions. Mason does not replace them.

# 4. Answer to the common-factor question in the recurrence

The proposed terms are

\[
A=n^3b_{n-1},\qquad
B=(n+1)^3b_{n+1},\qquad
C=P(n)b_n,
\]

with \(A+B=C\).

Over \(\mathbf F_q[T]\), the scalar coefficients are constants. Hence:

- if \(n\not\equiv0\pmod\ell\), then \(n^3\) is a unit;
- if \(n\equiv0\pmod\ell\), then \(n^3=0\);
- similarly for \((n+1)^3\) and \(P(n)\).

There are no nonconstant polynomial primes common to \(b_n(T)\) and a nonzero scalar coefficient. The only phenomenon is singular reduction of the recurrence when a scalar coefficient vanishes.

For common factors of neighboring sequence terms, the Casoratian is the correct tool. In the original Apéry pair,

\[
a_nb_{n+1}-a_{n+1}b_n=-\frac6{(n+1)^3}.
\]

Away from the primes dividing \(6(n+1)\), it forbids simultaneous vanishing of the two solution coordinates. That is a direct determinant identity. Mason does not strengthen it.

# 5. What large monodromy and \(p\)-curvature can—and cannot—give

## 5.1 Three different invariants are being mixed

One must distinguish:

1. **difference/transfer dynamics in the index \(n\)**, coming from the three-term recurrence;
2. **differential monodromy in the generating variable \(x\)**, coming from the Picard–Fuchs operator;
3. **étale Frobenius monodromy at closed points of a parameter curve over \(\mathbf F_q\)**.

The recurrence has a two-dimensional transfer state, but \(p\)-curvature is not an invariant of that discrete difference equation. The Apéry Picard–Fuchs differential equation has rank three. An \(\ell\)-adic sheaf attached to a K3 family has Frobenius classes at geometric points, but those points are not the coefficient indices \(r\).

## 5.2 Large monodromy gives only the equidistribution encoded by a fixed sheaf

Suppose one has a fixed lisse \(\ell\)-adic sheaf \(\mathcal F\) on a curve over \(\mathbf F_q\) with large geometric monodromy. Deligne–Katz theory can then give square-root error terms for sums of the form

\[
\sum_{x\in C(\mathbf F_{q^m})}
\varphi(\operatorname{Frob}_x\mid\mathcal F)
\]

for suitable trace functions \(\varphi\).

This does not automatically count roots of a changing polynomial \(A_n(T)\), nor does it count the coefficient zero set

\[
Z_p=\{r:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\}.
\]

The repository’s `oracleB_result.tex` isolates the exact category error:

\[
Z_p
\]

is a coefficient/finite-Mellin zero set, not the evaluation-root locus of the truncation polynomial

\[
\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rT^r.
\]

A point-count theorem for an evaluation divisor therefore does not bound \(z_p\).

## 5.3 The repository already has a bounded-complexity marked object

`problems/3.2/oracleC_result.tex` constructs a fixed Laurent polynomial \(\Lambda\) and the marked scalar

\[
c_{p,j}^{\mathrm{tor}}
=-\sum_{u\in(\mathbf F_p^\times)^3}\Lambda(u)^j
=b_j\pmod p
\]

for \(0\le j\le p-2\), with a separately handled endpoint. It also packages this as a bounded-geometric-complexity Kummer-sheaf construction.

Thus the obstacle is no longer “find some geometric object whose marked coordinate vanishes exactly when \(b_j\equiv0\pmod p\).” Such an object exists in the repository.

The remaining obstruction is horizontal:

- the character exponent \(j\) moves with \(p\);
- the zero event is reduction modulo a prime above the same characteristic;
- two-characteristic dispersion is needed to control simultaneous hits.

The same file gives a concrete warning: a characteristic-zero cyclotomic trace may be nonzero while its reduction modulo the defining prime is zero. Ordinary Frobenius trace equidistribution does not see that divisibility event.

Therefore “large monodromy” is at most one ingredient in a future horizontal large-sieve theorem. It does not itself imply small \(Z_p\).

# 6. Specialization from generic \(T\) to \(T=1\)

There is no direct specialization principle of the required type.

Polynomial gcds can jump arbitrarily at a special point. For example, for any integer \(M\), the two polynomials

\[
F_M(T)=T+M-1,
\qquad
G_M(T)=T+2M-1
\]

are coprime in \(\mathbf Q[T]\), but at \(T=1\),

\[
F_M(1)=M,\qquad G_M(1)=2M,
\]

so their specialized integer gcd is \(M\), which can be arbitrarily large.

The same horizontal/vertical distinction applies here:

- factor degrees in \(\mathbf F_q[T]\) measure horizontal divisors on a parameter curve;
- prime factors of \(A_n(1)\in\mathbf Z\) are vertical divisors on an arithmetic surface.

A horizontal degree bound does not control vertical intersection at one fixed section \(T=1\).

The Lucas factorization makes this especially clear. In fixed characteristic \(\ell\),

\[
\overline A_n(T)
=\prod_i\overline A_{n_i}(T)^{\ell^i}.
\]

After specializing \(T=1\), this gives only the familiar congruence

\[
b_n\equiv\prod_i b_{n_i}^{\ell^i}\pmod\ell.
\]

It says something about divisibility by the one fixed characteristic \(\ell\). It says nothing about all other ordinary primes dividing the integer \(b_n\).

A transfer theorem would need uniform control of vertical bad divisors for the fixed section \(T=1\). That is essentially the original arithmetic problem, not a formal consequence of genericity or Hilbert irreducibility.

# 7. The Grothendieck–Katz premise needs two corrections

## 7.1 Vanishing is not nilpotence

The Grothendieck–Katz \(p\)-curvature conjecture concerns **vanishing** \(p\)-curvature for almost all residue characteristics. Its conclusion is finite monodromy, equivalently a full set of algebraic solutions after a finite cover.

“Nilpotent \(p\)-curvature” is much weaker. Picard–Fuchs/Gauss–Manin connections coming from geometry are typically globally nilpotent: their \(p\)-curvatures are nilpotent for almost all primes. Such systems can and often do have infinite monodromy and transcendental period solutions.

The Apéry K3 operator is precisely of this type. Its geometric origin predicts global nilpotence; its infinite monodromy is not a contradiction.

Katz’s nilpotent-monodromy theorem uses global nilpotence to obtain regular singularities and quasi-unipotent local monodromy. It does not conclude finite global monodromy.

## 7.2 The blanket “proved in rank two” statement is too broad

The full \(p\)-curvature conjecture is not simply a theorem for every rank-two connection on every fixed curve. Katz proved important rigid/hypergeometric and Gauss–Manin cases; the rank-two conjecture is known on \(\mathbf P^1\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}\), and Patel–Shankar–Whang proved it for rank-two connections on generic curves. Those are not a blanket theorem for all rank-two systems.

In any case, the Apéry Picard–Fuchs operator displayed above has rank three, even though it is related to a rank-two system through a symmetric-square construction.

## 7.3 Neither nilpotence nor non-nilpotence controls coefficient zeros

There is a simple decisive counterexample to any such implication. Let

\[
u_n=\binom{2n}{n},
\qquad
\sum_{n\ge0}u_nx^n=(1-4x)^{-1/2}.
\]

The generating function is algebraic, so its differential equation has finite monodromy and zero \(p\)-curvature for almost all primes. Nevertheless, for every odd prime \(p\), Kummer’s carry criterion gives

\[
p\mid\binom{2r}{r}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{p+1}{2}\le r\le p-1.
\]

Thus the coefficient zero set in one residue block has size

\[
\frac{p-1}{2}.
\]

So even **zero** \(p\)-curvature can coexist with a linear-sized coefficient zero set. A fortiori, non-nilpotent or nonzero \(p\)-curvature cannot force \(z_p\) to be small.

The small empirical Apéry zero sets are an additional arithmetic phenomenon. They are not a formal consequence of global nilpotence, non-nilpotence, large monodromy, or geometric origin.

# 8. Direct answers to the six questions

## 1. Is \(G_n(T)=e^{o(n)}\) over \(\mathbf F_q(T)\)?

**Literal formulation:** no meaningful nontrivial statement exists.

- With the scalar recurrence and constant initial values, the sequence is constant in \(T\).
- In the field \(\mathbf F_q(T)\), gcds are only defined after choosing a divisor/ring model.
- The integer lcm becomes a unit or zero after reduction.

**Corrected formulation:** yes, strongly. For the standard Apéry polynomial and the natural function-field lcm \(\mathcal L_D\),

\[
\deg\gcd(\mathcal L_{\lfloor\log_q n\rfloor},\overline A_n)
=O_q((\log n)^2)=o(n).
\]

This follows from the polynomial Lucas factorization.

## 2. Can Mason–Stothers prove the GCD bound?

No. In positive characteristic one must exclude the all-derivatives-zero/Frobenius case, and the Apéry polynomials are dominated by exactly that Frobenius structure. Moreover, Mason bounds coprime quotients after the common gcd has been divided out. It does not bound the selected common divisor required by P3.2.

## 3. Can the recurrence identity bound common factors with its coefficients?

No. In \(\mathbf F_q[T]\), the recurrence coefficients are constants. Nonzero constants are units, and zero constants make the recurrence singular. The Casoratian, not Mason, is the relevant common-factor constraint.

The only honest polynomial recurrence is the gap-polynomial recurrence in the index variable, but Mason applied there yields only a linear-versus-linear tautology and no cross-prime saving.

## 4. Does large monodromy or \(p\)-curvature equidistribute \(Z_p\)?

Not the \(Z_p\) relevant to P3.2. Large monodromy can equidistribute Frobenius classes for a fixed sheaf over geometric base points. The Apéry \(Z_p\) are moving coefficient/Mellin zeros in the same defining characteristic. The repository already constructs a fixed bounded-complexity marked toric object, but the missing theorem is horizontal two-characteristic dispersion for its reductions.

## 5. Does a generic function-field theorem specialize to \(T=1\) over \(\mathbf Z\)?

No. Gcd degree can jump at a special section, and horizontal factor degrees do not control vertical integer prime divisors. The Frobenius–Lucas proof of the function-field theorem is characteristic-specific and disappears when one asks about all ordinary primes dividing \(A_n(1)\).

## 6. Does non-nilpotent \(p\)-curvature force small \(z_p\)?

No. The premise confuses nilpotent with zero \(p\)-curvature. The Apéry K3 operator is globally nilpotent and has infinite monodromy. More fundamentally, coefficient zero counts are not controlled by \(p\)-curvature: the algebraic central-binomial generating function has zero \(p\)-curvature but about \(p/2\) zero coefficients in one block.

# 9. Strategic conclusion for the repository

The preliminary assessment in `drops/Q8793-102172af.md` was directionally useful but too optimistic about Deligne removing the original Stage C obstruction. The current repository state in `oracleB_result.tex` and `oracleC_result.tex` gives the sharper conclusion:

1. A fixed geometric marked coordinate exists.
2. Coefficient zeros are not evaluation roots.
3. Fixed geometric complexity does not control same-characteristic divisibility of moving Mellin characters.
4. The remaining theorem is horizontal/two-characteristic dispersion.

The Mason–Stothers function-field lane should therefore be recorded as follows.

### Bankable positive theorem

For fixed \(\mathbf F_q\), the standard Apéry polynomials satisfy

\[
\deg\operatorname{rad}(\overline A_n)=O_q(\log n)
\]

and the natural function-field lcm gcd has degree \(O_q((\log n)^2)\).

### Death certificate as a route to integer P3.2

The theorem is caused by Frobenius digit factorization and has no specialization mechanism to \(T=1\). Mason does not see the required gcd, and \(p\)-curvature/large monodromy does not control coefficient zero sets. Therefore this route does not replace the marked crystalline Mellin dispersion, the large-prime-divisor energy theorem, or the atom-tail problem.

That is the definitive function-field outcome: **a clean standalone analogue is provable, but it is structurally too degenerate to transfer to the Ramanujan Challenge.**
