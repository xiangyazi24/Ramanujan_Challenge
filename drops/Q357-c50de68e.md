# Q357 — Large-prime-divisor energy of Apéry values

## Executive verdict

Let

\[
 b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2,
 \qquad
 \kappa=4\log(1+\sqrt2)=\log(17+12\sqrt2).
\]

Then

\[
 \log b_n=\kappa n+O(\log n).
\]

The proposed estimate

\[
 \sum_{c\le N}\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_c)^2
 =o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right)
 \tag{E}
\]

is **not presently a consequence of any known Erdős–Kac theorem, abc/Szpiro principle, generic holonomic theorem, modular-form theorem, or Lucas congruence**. In the literature I could verify, no unconditional upper bound of this strength is known for the Apéry numbers.

The sharp conclusions are:

1. **There is a generic \(o(n)\) bound, but no generic power saving.** Every nonzero integer sequence with \(\log|a_n|=O(n)\) satisfies
   \[
   \omega(a_n)=O\!\left(\frac n{\log n}\right)=o(n),
   \]
   simply by the primorial bound. Holonomicity is not used. This order is best possible even for hypergeometric, hence holonomic, sequences: \(a_n=\binom{2n}{n}\) has \(\gg n/\log n\) distinct prime divisors larger than \(\sqrt n\). Thus no theorem of the form \(\omega(a_n)\ll n^{1-\varepsilon}\) can hold for all exponentially growing holonomic integer sequences.

2. **The strongest directly relevant published Apéry theorem I found goes in the opposite direction.** Luca–Shparlinski prove, for a density-one set of \(n\), lower bounds
   \[
   \omega(b_n)>c_0\log\log\log n,
   \qquad
   P(b_n)>c_0(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2}.
   \]
   These do not upper-bound the number of large prime factors.

3. **abc gives lower radical bounds, not the required upper control.** Applied to the recurrence or Wronskian, it could force large radical or primitive prime factors among neighboring terms. It does not prevent one term from having many distinct primes \(>\sqrt N\).

4. **The modular-form congruence controls only one moving diagonal.** The relation
   \[
   b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p\bigl(\eta(2z)^4\eta(4z)^4\bigr)\pmod p
   \]
   identifies the central zero event with the **nonordinary** condition \(p\mid a_p(f)\). In weight \(4\), this is not equivalent to \(a_p(f)=0\). The density-zero conjecture for these nonordinary primes is itself open for this non-CM form, and even a complete result would say nothing about most prime divisors of a fixed \(b_n\).

5. **The most credible path is Apéry-specific two-prime dispersion.** For \(p>\sqrt N\) and \(c\le N\), write
   \[
   c=q_pp+r_p,\qquad 0\le r_p<p,\qquad q_p<\sqrt N.
   \]
   Lucas gives the exact criterion
   \[
   p\mid b_c
   \iff
   p\mid b_{q_p}\quad\text{or}\quad p\mid b_{r_p}.
   \tag{L2}
   \]
   Expanding the energy shows that the missing theorem is a joint correlation estimate for these digit events at two different primes. This is exactly the cross-prime dispersion problem isolated in the repository, not a one-variable prime-factor theorem.

Therefore the answer to “is there any path?” is:

> **Yes, but not through a known black-box theorem about \\(\omega\\), radicals, or holonomic sequences. The viable route is to prove an Apéry-specific two-prime incidence theorem, most naturally after the two-digit Lucas decomposition. A pointwise bound \\(\omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_n)=O(n^{1/2-\varepsilon})\\) would be much stronger than necessary and currently has no credible generic source.**

## 0. Two scope corrections

### 0.1 The prime range relevant to the GCD problem is truncated

The original large-prime part of the Apéry GCD problem uses

\[
 \sqrt N<p\le N.
\]

It is therefore useful to distinguish

\[
 F_N(c):=\#\{p:\sqrt N<p\le N,\ p\mid b_c\}
 \tag{0.1}
\]

from the stronger quantity in the question,

\[
 F_N^\infty(c):=\#\{p:p>\sqrt N,\ p\mid b_c\}.
 \tag{0.2}
\]

Clearly \(F_N(c)\le F_N^\infty(c)\), so (E) with the unbounded range is sufficient. But it is strictly stronger than what pair dispersion needs, because primes \(p>N\) never occur in the original prime window.

This distinction also changes the random-integer heuristic dramatically. For \(c\asymp N\), a random integer of size \(\exp(\kappa c)\) has expected

\[
 \sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}\frac1p=\log2+o(1)
 \tag{0.3}
\]

prime divisors in the truncated window, but expected

\[
 \sum_{\sqrt N<p\le b_c}\frac1p
 =\log\log b_c-\log\log\sqrt N+O(1)
 =\log N-\log\log N+O(1)
 \tag{0.4}
\]

prime divisors in the unbounded window. Both heuristics imply an energy far below the target, but only the truncated one predicts the observed \(O(1)\) row counts.

Accordingly, the empirical statement “\(\omega_{>70}(b_n)\le4\) for all \(n\le5000\)” should be audited carefully: if the computation tested primes only up to \(5000\), then it concerns \(F_{5000}\), not \(F_{5000}^\infty\). Complete factorization of all \(b_n\) through \(n=5000\) would be a much stronger computation.

### 0.2 A small numerical correction

For the standard Apéry normalization used here,

\[
 b_{12}=12073365010564729,
\]

which is prime; \(33801\) is not \(b_{12}\). The initial sequence is

\[
1,5,73,1445,33001,819005,\ldots.
\]

This has no effect on the theoretical question, but finite factorization evidence should be recorded with the precise index and prime range.

### 0.3 Near-squarefreeness does not imply small \(\omega\)

The observations that \(b_n\) is often squarefree or that \(\operatorname{rad}(b_n)/b_n\) is close to \(1\) do not by themselves upper-bound \(\omega(b_n)\). A primorial is squarefree and has essentially the largest possible number of distinct prime factors for its size. Near-squarefreeness controls multiplicities, whereas (E) asks how many distinct large primes occur.

## 1. The exact energy decomposition

For the relevant truncated range, set

\[
 x_{c,p}:=\mathbf1_{p\mid b_c},
 \qquad
 F_N(c)=\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}x_{c,p}.
\]

Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal E_N
 &:=\sum_{c\le N}F_N(c)^2\\
 &=\sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}A_p(N)
   +2\sum_{\sqrt N<p<q\le N}A_{p,q}(N),
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.1}
\]

where

\[
 A_p(N):=\#\{c\le N:p\mid b_c\},
 \tag{1.2}
\]

and

\[
 A_{p,q}(N):=\#\{c\le N:pq\mid b_c\}.
 \tag{1.3}
\]

Thus (E) is not principally a theorem about the one-variable function \(\omega(b_c)\). It is the sum of:

- a one-prime incidence term \(\sum_pA_p(N)\);
- a genuinely two-prime intersection term \(\sum_{p<q}A_{p,q}(N)\).

Since there are \(\asymp N/\log N\) primes in the range, the trivial estimate “one common \(c\) per prime pair” already has size \(N^2/\log^2N\). The required little-\(o\) is exactly the assertion that the average codegree

\[
 \frac{1}{\#\{(p,q):\sqrt N<p<q\le N\}}
 \sum_{p<q}A_{p,q}(N)
\]

tends to zero, after also disposing of the diagonal. This is the arithmetic version of the pair-dispersion condition.

A much stronger but conceptually clean target would be the random-scale estimate

\[
 \sum_{\sqrt N<p<q\le N}A_{p,q}(N)\ll N^{1+o(1)}.
 \tag{1.4}
\]

For the GCD theorem one does not need anything this sharp: **any fixed power saving**

\[
 \sum_{\sqrt N<p<q\le N}A_{p,q}(N)\ll N^{2-\delta}
 \tag{1.5}
\]

would imply the desired little-\(o\), because \(N^{2-\delta}=o(N^2/\log^2N)\).

This is the most useful calibration: one need not prove an Erdős–Kac law or a pointwise \(O(1)\) bound. One needs some nontrivial two-prime decorrelation.

## 2. What follows from size alone

### Proposition 2.1 — the universal primorial bound

Let \((u_n)\) be any sequence of nonzero integers satisfying

\[
 \log|u_n|\le Cn+O(\log n).
\]

Then

\[
 \omega(u_n)\le (C+o(1))\frac n{\log n}.
 \tag{2.1}
\]

In particular, \(\omega(u_n)=o(n)\).

#### Proof

If \(k=\omega(m)\), then \(|m|\) is at least the product of the first \(k\) primes. The prime number theorem for the primorial gives

\[
 \log(p_1p_2\cdots p_k)=(1+o(1))k\log k.
\]

Hence

\[
 k\log k\le(1+o(1))\log|m|.
\]

With \(m=u_n\) and \(\log|u_n|=O(n)\), inversion gives (2.1). ∎

For the Apéry numbers this yields

\[
 \omega(b_n)\le(\kappa+o(1))\frac n{\log n}.
 \tag{2.2}
\]

This is a logarithmic improvement over \(\omega(b_n)\le\log_2b_n\), but it does not improve the energy exponent: for \(c\asymp N\), it is still of order \(N/\log N\).

### Proposition 2.2 — no generic power saving for holonomic sequences

There is no \(\varepsilon>0\) such that every exponentially growing holonomic integer sequence satisfies

\[
 \omega(u_n)\ll n^{1-\varepsilon}.
\]

Indeed, take

\[
 u_n=\binom{2n}{n}.
\]

This is hypergeometric and satisfies the first-order polynomial-coefficient recurrence

\[
 (n+1)u_{n+1}=2(2n+1)u_n.
 \tag{2.3}
\]

For every prime

\[
 \frac n2<p\le\frac{2n}{3},
\]

and all sufficiently large \(n\), one has \(p^2>2n\) and

\[
 v_p\binom{2n}{n}
 =\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{p}\right\rfloor
  -2\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor
 =3-2=1.
 \tag{2.4}
\]

Therefore

\[
 \omega_{>\sqrt n}\!\left(\binom{2n}{n}\right)
 \ge \pi(2n/3)-\pi(n/2)
 \asymp\frac n{\log n}.
 \tag{2.5}
\]

This not only makes (2.1) sharp in order of magnitude; it shows that the desired energy estimate is false for general holonomic sequences. At \(c=N\), the single term \(u_N\) already contributes

\[
 \gg\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}
\]

to the squared large-prime count.

Hence any proof for \(b_n\) must use structure much more specific than D-finiteness.

### Proposition 2.3 — the low-index range is automatically harmless

The elementary threshold estimate gives

\[
 F_N^\infty(c)
 \le\frac{\log b_c}{\log\sqrt N}
 \ll\frac c{\log N}.
 \tag{2.6}
\]

Consequently, for every \(Y\le N\),

\[
 \sum_{c\le Y}F_N^\infty(c)^2
 \ll\frac{Y^3}{\log^2N}.
 \tag{2.7}
\]

Thus every range

\[
 c\le \frac{N^{2/3}}{L(N)}
\]

with \(L(N)\to\infty\) contributes

\[
 o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right).
\]

The difficult part of (E) is concentrated in the upper \(N^{2/3+o(1)}\)-to-\(N\) range. This is a genuine unconditional reduction and should be used in any attempted proof.

## 3. Question (a): can Erdős–Kac help?

### 3.1 Standard Erdős–Kac does not apply to a deterministic orbit

The classical theorem samples an integer uniformly from \([1,X]\). Its proof ultimately uses the approximate relations

\[
 \#\{m\le X:d\mid m\}\approx\frac Xd
\]

for squarefree \(d\) built from small primes. There is no theorem allowing one to replace the random integer \(m\) by the highly structured value \(b_n\) merely because \(b_n\) has exponential size.

For the Apéry sequence one would need estimates for

\[
 A_d(N):=\#\{c\le N:d\mid b_c\}
 \tag{3.1}
\]

uniformly over squarefree products \(d\). At second order in the desired large-prime range, \(d=pq>N\). Thus the needed information is precisely

\[
 A_{p,q}(N)=\#\{c\le N:pq\mid b_c\},
\]

the off-diagonal term in (1.1). Invoking an Erdős–Kac theorem would therefore require proving a level of distribution at least as strong as the Sparse Bridge input one is trying to obtain.

### 3.2 Even a normal-order heuristic is only heuristic

If \(b_c\) behaved like a random integer of comparable size, then for \(c\asymp N\):

- \(F_N(c)\), with primes restricted to \((\sqrt N,N]\), would have bounded mean \(\log2+o(1)\);
- \(F_N^\infty(c)\) would have mean of order \(\log N\).

Either model predicts

\[
 \sum_{c\le N}F_N(c)^2\ll N(\log N)^2,
\]

well inside (E). But the entire problem is to rule out a thin set of highly atypical Apéry values. Erdős–Kac for ordinary integers says nothing about that deterministic thin set.

### 3.3 The direct published Apéry result

Luca and Shparlinski, *Arithmetic properties of Apéry numbers*, J. London Math. Soc. 78 (2008), 545–562, prove that for a density-one set of \(n\),

\[
 \omega(b_n)>c_0\log\log\log n
\]

and

\[
 P(b_n)>c_0(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2}.
\]

Their method combines the polynomial-coefficient recurrence with Lucas-type congruences. It gives lower bounds on arithmetic complexity, not an upper bound on \(\omega(b_n)\), and it does not provide a second moment for prime divisors in a moving interval.

I found no published Erdős–Kac theorem for the Apéry values themselves, nor an upper-bound analogue capable of proving (E).

### Verdict for (a)

Erdős–Kac supplies the correct probabilistic prediction but no unconditional input. To make it applicable, one would first have to prove the two-prime divisibility estimates that essentially constitute the desired theorem.

## 4. Question (b): abc, Szpiro, radicals, and the Wronskian

### 4.1 The direction of abc is opposite to the desired one

The abc conjecture says, schematically, that for coprime \(X+Y=Z\),

\[
 \max(|X|,|Y|,|Z|)
 \ll_\varepsilon \operatorname{rad}(XYZ)^{1+\varepsilon}.
 \tag{4.1}
\]

This is a **lower bound on a radical**. It says that a large additive triple cannot be supported on too small a set of primes.

By contrast, an upper bound on

\[
 \omega_{>\sqrt N}(b_n)
\]

would have to show that \(b_n\) is not divisible by too many different large primes. A lower bound on \(\operatorname{rad}(b_n)\) does not do that. A squarefree product of many primes has the largest possible radical relative to its size.

### 4.2 Applying abc to the Apéry recurrence

The recurrence can be written as

\[
 (n+1)^3b_{n+1}+n^3b_{n-1}=P(n)b_n,
 \tag{4.2}
\]

with \(P(n)=(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)\). After dividing common factors, abc could plausibly force a large radical in

\[
 n(n+1)P(n)b_{n-1}b_nb_{n+1}.
\]

At best, this says that the product of three neighboring Apéry numbers has many or large prime divisors. It does not bound the number of large primes in the middle term from above.

The Wronskian identity

\[
 a_nb_{n+1}-a_{n+1}b_n=-\frac6{(n+1)^3}
 \tag{4.3}
\]

has the same issue. Clearing denominators introduces the large least-common-multiple factors attached to \(a_n\), so it is not a three-term \(S\)-unit equation with fixed \(S\). The relevant set \(S\) grows with \(n\), and abc would again force radical rather than suppress it.

### 4.3 What abc might prove instead

In many genuine divisibility sequences, abc can imply large primitive prime divisors or strong lower bounds for the largest prime factor. Such a result for \(b_n\) would be interesting, but one huge prime factor can coexist with \(n/\log n\) other medium-sized prime factors. It would not imply (E).

Similarly, Szpiro controls the radical of the conductor/discriminant of an elliptic curve. There is no fixed elliptic curve naturally attached to \(b_n\) whose conductor exponent is the count \(F_N(c)\), and the usual Szpiro direction is again a lower-bound/height relation, not an upper count of distinct divisors of one coefficient.

### Verdict for (b)

Neither abc nor Szpiro is a plausible direct route to (E). They may prove that Apéry numbers possess large new primes; they do not prevent a term from possessing many large primes. The Wronskian is valuable for coprimality and valuation bookkeeping, but it is not a fixed-\(S\) unit equation.

## 5. Questions (c) and (d): what does holonomicity give?

### 5.1 What D-finiteness does give

The polynomial-coefficient recurrence implies:

- effective computation and precise asymptotics;
- a \(G\)-function/diagonal structure;
- strong congruences and finite-state descriptions modulo a fixed prime;
- transfer matrices and gap polynomials;
- restrictions on common zeros at one fixed characteristic.

These are substantial. None is a generic upper-bound theorem for distinct prime divisors of a single term.

The state space modulo \(p\), the degree of the relevant gap word, and the effective constants all grow with \(p\) or the gap. Thus “finite-dimensional recurrence” does not mean “uniformly bounded arithmetic complexity across all primes.”

### 5.2 The strongest universal upper statement

For any nonzero integer-valued sequence of exponential growth, holonomic or not,

\[
 \omega(a_n)=O(n/\log n)=o(n)
\]

by Proposition 2.1. This answers one part of question (d) affirmatively: an \(o(n)\) bound is automatic.

But Proposition 2.2 shows that this is the correct general order. There is no universal \(n^{1-\varepsilon}\) bound, and even the large-prime energy (E) fails for the central binomial coefficients.

### 5.3 What the holonomic prime-divisor literature proves

Florian Luca, *Prime divisors of binary holonomic sequences*, Adv. Appl. Math. 40 (2008), 168–179, proves, for a broad class of second-order rational holonomic sequences that are not eventually constant-coefficient binary recurrences, a lower bound of order \(\log N\) for the number of primes appearing among the numerators and denominators of the nonzero terms through \(N\).

Again this concerns the union of prime divisors across many terms and is a lower bound. It is not an upper bound for \(\omega(a_n)\) of an individual term.

### Verdict for (c) and (d)

- Holonomicity alone gives no useful upper bound beyond the universal primorial estimate.
- The generic \(O(n/\log n)\) estimate is sharp.
- Any power-saving result for the Apéry numbers must exploit their specific Lucas, reflection, K3/Frobenius, or transfer-matrix structure.

## 6. Question (e): the modular-form connection

Let

\[
 f(z)=\eta(2z)^4\eta(4z)^4.
\]

This is the rational, non-CM newform of weight \(4\) and level \(8\) labeled \(8.4.a.a\) in the LMFDB. The project has the congruence

\[
 b_{(p-1)/2}\equiv a_p(f)\pmod p.
 \tag{6.1}
\]

### 6.1 Nonordinary is not the same as trace zero

Equation (6.1) gives

\[
 p\mid b_{(p-1)/2}
 \iff p\mid a_p(f).
 \tag{6.2}
\]

The right side is the nonordinary condition at \(p\). Since \(f\) has weight \(4\), Deligne’s bound is

\[
 |a_p(f)|\le2p^{3/2}.
\]

Therefore \(p\mid a_p(f)\) permits

\[
 a_p(f)=mp,\qquad |m|\le2\sqrt p,
\]

and does **not** force \(a_p(f)=0\). The familiar equivalence between supersingularity and \(a_p=0\) for elliptic curves relies on the weight-\(2\) Hasse bound and does not transfer verbatim to weight \(4\).

### 6.2 Density information

For non-CM modular forms, effective Sato–Tate/Atkin–Serre results imply that \(a_p(f)\ne0\) for a density-one set of primes. Thus exact trace-zero primes have density zero. The generalized Lang–Trotter heuristic in weight \(4\) predicts that exact zeros should be extremely sparse, plausibly finite, because the heuristic probability is of order \(p^{-3/2}\).

But (6.2) concerns the much larger congruence event \(p\mid a_p(f)\). A random-integer heuristic gives probability about \(1/p\), hence a conjectural count of order \(\log\log x\), but proving even the needed density statement for this same-characteristic divisibility event is open. This precise warning is already recorded in `problems/3.2/oracleB_result.tex`: ordinary Sato–Tate does not control the shrinking condition \(p\mid a_p(f)\).

### 6.3 Why the diagonal cannot control \(\omega(b_n)\)

For a fixed \(n\), (6.1) involves only the single prime

\[
 p=2n+1
\]

when that number is prime. It says nothing about the other primes dividing \(b_n\). Across \(p\le2N+1\), it samples one moving index per prime, whereas (E) involves every pair \((c,p)\) with \(c\le N\).

Even a complete classification of the central nonordinary primes would therefore contribute only a thin diagonal to the energy matrix.

### Verdict for (e)

The modular connection is arithmetically deep but dimensionally too thin for (E). It identifies one special incidence and leads to an open nonordinary-prime problem; it does not bound the full large-prime divisor count of \(b_n\).

## 7. Question (f): the binomial formula, Lucas, and p-adic valuation

### 7.1 Positivity over \(\mathbb Z\) does not survive modulo \(p\)

The formula

\[
 b_n=\sum_k\left(\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}\right)^2
\]

is a sum of nonnegative integers, but modulo \(p\) the summands can cancel. Termwise Kummer or carry information does not by itself prevent the total sum from vanishing.

The strongest aggregate consequence is the Lucas congruence

\[
 b_{ap+r}\equiv b_ab_r\pmod p,
 \qquad 0\le r<p.
 \tag{7.1}
\]

Recent work of Delaygue and of Beukers–Tsai–Ye gives broad p-adic and modular explanations for such congruences and stronger valuation lower bounds. These results tell us when divisibility propagates; they do not upper-bound how many different primes divide one term.

### 7.2 The exact two-digit reduction for large primes

If \(p>\sqrt N\) and \(c\le N\), write

\[
 c=q_pp+r_p,\qquad 0\le r_p<p.
\]

Then

\[
 q_p\le N/p<\sqrt N<p,
\]

so \(c\) has at most two base-\(p\) digits. From (7.1),

\[
 \boxed{
 p\mid b_c
 \iff
 q_p\in Z_p\quad\text{or}\quad r_p\in Z_p,
 }
 \tag{7.2}
\]

where

\[
 Z_p:=\{0\le r<p:p\mid b_r\}.
\]

This is the key Apéry-specific reformulation.

For one prime, it even gives an exact complete-block count. Put \(Q=\lfloor N/p\rfloor\), and ignore only the final incomplete block. For each \(0\le a<Q\), the block \(ap\le c<(a+1)p\) contributes

\[
 \begin{cases}
 p,&a\in Z_p,\\
 |Z_p|,&a\notin Z_p.
 \end{cases}
 \tag{7.3}
\]

Thus one-prime incidence consists of:

- **leading-digit infection:** if \(p\mid b_a\), an entire length-\(p\) block vanishes modulo \(p\);
- **suffix hits:** otherwise the allowed suffixes are exactly \(Z_p\).

This exposes why a mere bound on \(|Z_p|\) or on a column norm is insufficient: the leading digit can move with both \(c\) and \(p\).

### 7.3 Two primes produce the exact missing problem

For distinct \(p,q>\sqrt N\), the condition \(pq\mid b_c\) becomes

\[
 (q_p\in Z_p\ \text{or}\ r_p\in Z_p)
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 (q_q\in Z_q\ \text{or}\ r_q\in Z_q).
 \tag{7.4}
\]

There are four channels: leading–leading, leading–suffix, suffix–leading, and suffix–suffix. The two digit decompositions use different bases, so Lucas does not factor the joint condition. A theorem showing cancellation or sparse intersection among these four moving families would prove (E).

This is exactly the “marked two-characteristic” or cross-prime dispersion input isolated in `oracleB_result.tex`. Lucas converts the arithmetic question into a precise incidence problem; it does not solve that incidence problem.

### 7.4 Valuation theorems have the wrong direction

Delaygue’s theorem proves lower bounds for \(v_p(b_n)\) in terms of base-\(p\) digit/carry data and establishes p-Lucas criteria for broad Apéry-like families. Such results are extremely useful after a prime divisor has appeared. But (E) counts how many different primes appear. A stronger lower bound on each \(v_p(b_n)\) can only help if one can show that many distinct large primes would consume too much of \(\log b_n\); the current lower bounds generally give only one unit per zero digit, returning to the size estimate \(O(n/\log N)\).

### Verdict for (f)

The binomial formula does not presently give a pointwise upper bound on the number of large prime divisors. Its most promising contribution is the exact two-digit formula (7.2), which should be used as the starting point for a two-prime dispersion argument.

## 8. What would actually suffice?

### 8.1 A two-prime incidence theorem

The cleanest target is:

```text
LPD2(N):

sum_{sqrt(N) < p < q <= N}
  #{ c <= N : p*q divides b_c }
    = o(N^2 / log(N)^2).
```

Together with the easier diagonal estimate

\[
 \sum_{\sqrt N<p\le N}A_p(N)
 =o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right),
 \tag{8.1}
\]

this is exactly (E) for the relevant prime window.

A fixed power saving, as in (1.5), is already enough. The expected truth is much stronger, approximately \(N^{1+o(1)}\).

### 8.2 A dyadic Lucas-dispersion theorem

For dyadic \(P,Q\in[\sqrt N,N]\), define

\[
 \mathcal C(N;P,Q)
 :=\sum_{p\sim P}\sum_{\substack{q\sim Q\\q\ne p}}
 \#\{c\le N:pq\mid b_c\}.
 \tag{8.2}
\]

Using (7.4), one can split \(\mathcal C\) into four explicit digit channels. A theorem of the form

\[
 \mathcal C(N;P,Q)
 \ll N^{2-\delta}(\log N)^{O(1)}
 \tag{8.3}
\]

uniformly in the dyadic boxes would close the desired energy after summing \(O(\log^2N)\) boxes.

This is not a standard Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem. The modulus \(pq>N\), the residue sets depend on the same primes, and the two bases are different. But it is a concrete analytic target on which one can try:

- bilinear large-sieve methods;
- dispersion after sorting by the small leading digits \(\lfloor c/p\rfloor\);
- product-monodromy input for the two prime characteristics;
- gap-polynomial/resultant control of exceptional alignments;
- a decomposition into leading-digit and suffix channels.

### 8.3 A tail theorem instead of a pointwise theorem

A pointwise estimate

\[
 F_N(c)=O(c^{1/2-\varepsilon})
\]

is sufficient but much stronger than needed. One may instead use the exact identity

\[
 \sum_{c\le N}F_N(c)^2
 =\sum_{T\ge1}(2T-1)\#\{c\le N:F_N(c)\ge T\}.
 \tag{8.4}
\]

Therefore a strong enough large-deviation estimate for rows with many prime divisors would suffice even if a few individual values have large \(F_N(c)\).

For example, it would be enough to prove, on dyadic \(T\)-ranges, bounds whose insertion into (8.4) gives

\[
 \sum_T T\,\#\{c:T\le F_N(c)<2T\}
 =o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right).
\]

This formulation may be more compatible with the project’s many-prime forcing and resultant-budget methods than a uniform pointwise exponent.

### 8.4 A many-prime forcing theorem

If \(T\) different primes \(p>\sqrt N\) divide one \(b_c\), then the Apéry transfer product at the common index \(c\) satisfies \(T\) rank-drop conditions in different characteristics. A successful integer-certificate argument would have to compress these conditions into an integer of height much smaller than the naive \(\exp(O(cT))\) product of separate certificates.

The existing size argument only recovers

\[
 T\ll c/\log N.
\]

To reach \(T=o(\sqrt N/\log N)\) pointwise, one needs a new shared minor, determinant, or common algebraic carrier. This is speculative but genuinely Apéry-specific; it is more plausible than expecting a generic holonomic theorem.

### 8.5 Restrict first to the correct prime window

Because primes \(p>N\) are irrelevant to the GCD reduction, the first theorem to pursue should be

\[
 \sum_{c\le N}\omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(b_c)^2
 =o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right).
 \tag{8.5}
\]

Only after this is proved should one ask for the stronger unbounded-prime version. The truncated version has bounded random mean (0.3), an exact two-digit Lucas description, and direct relevance to the project.

## 10. Direct answers to (a)–(f)

### (a) Erdős–Kac

No existing Erdős–Kac theorem applies to the deterministic sequence \(b_n\). Proving the needed local moment hypotheses would already require control of \(A_{p,q}(N)\), essentially the desired two-prime theorem. It is useful as a prediction, not as an input.

### (b) abc or Szpiro

They naturally lower-bound radicals or produce large primitive factors. They do not upper-bound the number of distinct large factors of \(b_n\). The Wronskian is not a fixed-\(S\) unit equation after denominators are cleared.

### (c) Holonomicity

D-finiteness supplies congruences and transfer algebra but no generic upper bound for \(\omega(a_n)\). The central binomial sequence is a decisive counterexample to any generic power saving.

### (d) Strongest generic upper result

Yes:

\[
 \omega(a_n)=O(n/\log n)=o(n)
\]

for every nonzero integer sequence with exponential growth. This follows from the primorial bound, not holonomicity. It is best possible in general; no \(n^{1-\varepsilon}\) bound holds for all holonomic sequences.

### (e) Modular form

The congruence controls \(p\mid a_p(f)\), the nonordinary condition, not just \(a_p(f)=0\). Exact zeros have density zero for the non-CM form, while the same-characteristic divisibility event is much harder and remains open. In any case, the congruence samples only \(n=(p-1)/2\), so it cannot bound the full prime-divisor set of arbitrary \(b_n\).

### (f) Binomial/Lucas structure

Yes, it gives the strongest concrete reduction:

\[
 p\mid b_c
 \iff
 p\mid b_{\lfloor c/p\rfloor}
 \text{ or }
 p\mid b_{c\bmod p},
 \qquad p>\sqrt N,\ c\le N.
\]

But bounding \(\sum F_N(c)^2\) then requires joint control of these events for two different primes. Present p-adic valuation theorems propagate divisibility and give lower bounds on valuations; they do not upper-bound the number of distinct primes.

## 11. Recommended next theorem

The most useful next statement for the project is not a pointwise bound on \(\omega(b_n)\). It is the following explicitly truncated, two-prime theorem.

```text
APERY LARGE-PRIME DIVISOR DISPERSION (ALPDD)

There exists delta > 0 such that

  sum_{sqrt(N) < p < q <= N}
    #{ c <= N : p*q | b_c }
      << N^(2-delta).
```

By (1.1), together with any comparable diagonal bound, ALPDD immediately yields

\[
 \sum_{c\le N}\omega_{(\sqrt N,N]}(b_c)^2
 =o\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log^2N}\right),
\]

and hence the sufficient theorem quoted from Q352.

The proof program should be:

1. use the two-digit Lucas criterion to split each pair event into the four channels in (7.4);
2. isolate the leading-digit infections \(p\mid b_{\lfloor c/p\rfloor}\), whose indices are \(<\sqrt N\);
3. use the already-proved fixed-anchor finiteness only after dyadic grouping, never with the quantifiers interchanged;
4. seek a bilinear/large-sieve estimate for the suffix–suffix channel;
5. reserve gap-polynomial resultants and transfer certificates for the exceptional high-codegree configurations;
6. exploit the unconditional low-index reduction (2.7) to discard \(c<N^{2/3-o(1)}\).

This route directly addresses the arithmetic missing from the anchored-star counterexample. None of the generic theories in (a)–(e) currently substitutes for it.

## Final conclusion

The desired energy estimate is very plausible—indeed far weaker than the random model predicts—but it is not covered by existing probabilistic number theory or by the general theory of holonomic sequences. Generic holonomicity is provably too weak. The special Apéry congruences reduce the problem to a clean two-prime digit-incidence theorem, and that is the path with the correct logical shape.
