# Polynomial cocycle equidistribution over F_p

## Main conclusion

The proposed Bezout route is useful for identifying the obstruction, but it cannot directly prove AFP. The orbit

\[
\{(r,\rho_r):r\in \mathbf F_p\}\n\]

is generally **not** contained in a bounded-degree algebraic curve. The Apéry recurrence is a non-autonomous difference equation, and its orbit complexity grows with p. The right replacement is a difference-algebraic/trace-function argument, not a classical plane-curve intersection argument.

## (A) Resultant degree: no bounded-degree polynomial in r

The statement

\[
b_r\equiv0\pmod p
\]

is certainly a polynomial condition after expanding the recurrence, but the degree grows with r.

For a fixed r, b_r is a polynomial expression in the recurrence coefficients

\[
c(1),\ldots,c(r)
\]

and the degree grows roughly linearly with r. There is no elimination producing a polynomial

\[
P_p(r)=0
\]

of degree O(1).

This is the same phenomenon as for generic D-finite sequences: reduction modulo p gives a function on F_p, but not a low-degree polynomial function.

A bounded-degree resultant would imply much stronger algebraic rigidity than the Apéry equation has.

## (B) Transfer matrices and continued fractions

The correction is important: there is no single transfer matrix T(x). The system is a cocycle:

\[
M(r)M(r-1)\cdots M(1).
\]

The dependence on the entire path is essential.

The ratio

\[
\rho_r=b_r/b_{r-1}
\]

is exactly the projective orbit of this cocycle. This places the problem in the theory of non-autonomous PGL_2 dynamics.

For constant coefficient recurrences, the ratio dynamics reduce to powers of one matrix and are governed by eigenvalues. Here the changing matrices prevent that reduction.

## (C) Ratio equidistribution

The correct question is:

> Are projective orbits of polynomial matrix cocycles equidistributed in P^1(F_p)?

This is not a classical linear recurrence problem.

For constant matrices, yes: if the generated subgroup is large, one can use expansion of Cayley graphs of SL_2(F_p) (Bourgain-Gamburd, Helfgott, etc.).

However, the Apéry sequence uses a sequence of different matrices

\[
M_r=\begin{pmatrix}c(r)&-(r-1)^3\\r^3&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Large monodromy of the differential equation does not automatically imply finite-field orbit equidistribution. It controls the geometric monodromy of the associated sheaf, not arbitrary ordered products along the arithmetic progression r=1,...,p.

## (D) Bezout orbit curve approach

Define

\[
S_p=\{(r,\rho_r):0\le r<p\}.
\]

If S_p lay on a curve C of degree d independent of p, then indeed

\[
|S_p\cap V|\le d\deg(V)
\]

would prove AFP.

The problem is that such a bounded-degree C should not exist.

A generic p-point subset of A^2 needs degree about sqrt(p) to interpolate. The Apéry orbit appears pseudorandom in exactly this sense.

Therefore failure to find a degree-10 relation for p=97 is expected, and is actually evidence for the desired randomness.

## (E) Computation at p=97

The suggested computation should be interpreted as testing algebraic relations.

For a polynomial

\[
P(r,y)=\sum_{i+j\le d}a_{ij}r^iy^j,
\]

there are

\[
\binom{d+2}{2}
\]

unknown coefficients.

Construct the matrix

\[
(r^iy^j)_{(r,y)\in S_p}
\]

and compute its nullspace over F_97.

A nullspace for d=10 would have dimension at least 1 and reveal an algebraic relation. The expectation is:

- d <= 10: no relation;
- d increasing slowly: eventually interpolation relations appear only because every finite set has one.

This does not prove AFP.

## The correct replacement theorem

The desired statement is closer to a Deligne/Katz estimate:

\[
\#\{r:\rho_{r-1}= (r-1)^3/c(r)\}=O(1).
\]

Equivalently, one needs cancellation for the trace function associated with the cocycle orbit.

The collision condition is

\[
\det(v_{r-1},M(r)^{-1}(0,1)^T)=0.
\]

This is a zero set of a matrix coefficient of a non-constant l-adic sheaf. If the associated sheaf has bounded conductor and no exceptional geometric component, Deligne bounds become available.

## Relation to AFP

The empirical Poisson(1/2) law is exactly what one expects from a mixing projective cocycle:

- each step has collision probability approximately 1/p;
- there are approximately p/2 folded steps;
- the count converges to Poisson(1/2).

The missing theorem is therefore not a Bezout theorem but:

**Projective cocycle anti-concentration for the Apéry transfer matrices.**

A successful proof would likely combine:

1. Katz hypergeometric sheaves / Mellin transforms;
2. large geometric monodromy (SL_2-type);
3. Deligne bounds for exceptional trace functions.

The polynomial orbit-curve approach is a valuable diagnostic, but the absence of a low-degree relation is itself the expected behavior of the Apéry cocycle.
