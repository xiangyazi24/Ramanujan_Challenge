# Verdict

The PGL₂ orbit viewpoint is valuable, but the proposed implication

```
polynomial cocycle + algebraic generation => O(1) visits to every point
```

is not currently a theorem. The missing ingredient is **quantitative equidistribution of a deterministic non-autonomous cocycle**, which is substantially harder than expansion of a random walk on PGL₂.

The transfer matrices may generate a large group, and the product constraint may be compatible with mixing, but neither statement alone implies

$$\#\{r:x_r=w\}=O(1).$$

The realistic route to AFP is not full orbit equidistribution on P¹(F_p), but a weaker one-point estimate for the distinguished target `[0:1]`.

---

# 1. The orbit formulation is exact

Write

$$
M(r)=\begin{pmatrix}
P(r)/(r+1)^3&-r^3/(r+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For `r<p-1`, the determinant is

$$
\det M(r)=\frac{r^3}{(r+1)^3},
$$

so the matrices are invertible in PGL₂(F_p). The projective state

$$
x_r=[b_r:b_{r-1}]
$$

satisfies

$$
x_{r+1}=M(r)x_r.
$$

The zero condition is exactly

$$
b_r=0\pmod p
\iff x_r=[0:1].
$$

Therefore AFP is the statement that one particular point of P¹ is hit only boundedly many times.

However, upgrading this to

$$
\sup_w\#\{r:x_r=w\}=O(1)
$$

would be a much stronger theorem.

---

# 2. The p=89 histogram experiment

The suggested experiment is exactly the right diagnostic.

There are `p-1=88` orbit points and `|P¹(F_89)|=90` possible states. Uniform sampling predicts:

$$
\frac{88}{90}=0.977\ldots
$$

visits per state.

A random permutation-like orbit would have a histogram concentrated on:

- about 37 states never visited;
- about 36 states visited once;
- about 12 states visited twice;
- a few states visited three or more times.

But one must distinguish two possibilities:

1. the orbit behaves like a random permutation cycle;
2. the orbit is a short structured cycle inside P¹.

The SWAP identity alone does not distinguish them. It only proves a global relation on the product.

For AFP, the important statistic is the occupancy of `[0:1]`, not the total histogram.

---

# 3. Do the matrices generate PGL₂(F_p)?

The answer is very likely yes for generic primes, but this is not enough.

The set

$$
\{M(r):1\le r<p-1\}
$$

contains many matrices. A proper algebraic subgroup of PGL₂ is very restrictive:

- a Borel subgroup preserves a point of P¹;
- a torus normalizer preserves a pair;
- finite exceptional subgroups have bounded size.

The Apéry matrices do not appear to preserve such a structure. For example, the presence of the varying lower-left/upper-right terms prevents simultaneous triangularization. Computational generation tests for small primes support full PGL₂ generation.

But Bourgain-Gamburd type theorems concern an averaging operator

$$
\mu=\frac1{|S|}\sum_{g\in S}\delta_g
$$

for a fixed generating set S. They prove expansion of the random walk

$$
\mu^{*n}.
$$

The Apéry sequence is different:

$$
M(r)M(r-1)\cdots M(1)
$$

is one prescribed word. There is no randomization over the choices of generators.

Generation is therefore a necessary algebraic condition, not a quantitative orbit theorem.

---

# 4. Why polynomial cocycle equidistribution is harder

The desired theorem would be:

> If `g(t)` is a bounded-degree polynomial map A¹(F_p)→PGL₂(F_p)` whose image generates PGL₂, then the partial products `g(n)...g(1)` equidistribute.

No theorem of this generality is known.

The reason is that polynomial generation controls the algebraic envelope of the cocycle, but not the dynamics of a single trajectory.

A simple analogy:

Take a sequence of generators of a large finite group. The generated subgroup can be the entire group, but the first `p` partial products can still lie in a very small subset.

For random walks:

$$
\text{spectral gap}\Rightarrow\text{mixing}.
$$

For deterministic cocycles:

$$
\text{Zariski density}\not\Rightarrow\text{mixing}.
$$

One needs a new type of theorem: a **non-autonomous expander theorem**.

---

# 5. Does SWAP closedness help?

The identity

$$
M(p-2)\cdots M(1)=\mathrm{swap}
$$

is a strong global constraint.

It gives a relation between the forward orbit and the reflected orbit. It explains the symmetry

$$
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
$$

However, closedness does not imply equidistribution.

A closed random walk conditioned to return to its start is still usually well mixed, but a deterministic closed orbit may be highly periodic.

The good news:

- it prevents arbitrary drift;
- it creates the reflection pairing;
- it reduces independent zero events roughly by a factor of two.

The bad news:

- it does not provide cancellation estimates;
- it does not imply a spectral gap.

For AFP, the SWAP identity is a symmetry tool, not an equidistribution theorem.

---

# 6. Relation to existing expansion theorems

## Bourgain-Gamburd

These results prove expansion for Cayley graphs of groups such as SL₂(F_p) when the generating set is fixed and sufficiently non-degenerate.

They do not apply directly because the Apéry walk is:

- non-stationary;
- deterministic;
- length only p.

## Salehi Golsefidy-Varjú

These prove expansion in congruence quotients for arithmetic groups. Again, they concern averaging over a generating set, not a single polynomial word.

## Fouvry-Kowalski-Michel style trace methods

These handle algebraic trace functions with bounded conductor. The obstacle here is that the object is the orbit of a varying matrix product, not a fixed trace function of bounded conductor.

# 7. What weaker theorem would actually prove AFP?

Full P¹ equidistribution is overkill.

AFP only requires

$$
\#\{r:b_r=0\pmod p\}=O(1).
$$

A sufficient statement would be a target-specific discrepancy estimate:

$$
\left|
\sum_{r<p}1_{x_r=[0:1]}-\frac{p-1}{p+1}
\right|
\ll 1.
$$

Even a weaker power saving

$$
\#\{r:x_r=[0:1]\}\ll p^\epsilon
$$

would already dramatically improve the current AFP reduction.

This suggests studying the matrix coefficient

$$
\prod_{r=1}^n M(r)e_1
$$

rather than the full orbit.

---

# 8. The most plausible route: adelic/automorphic cocycle theory

The Apéry matrices come from a Picard-Fuchs/K3 motive. Therefore the correct replacement for Bourgain-Gamburd may be an automorphic equidistribution theorem for Frobenius orbits.

Possible ingredients:

1. interpret the orbit as a section of a finite-field local system;
2. prove large geometric monodromy;
3. apply Deligne/Katz equidistribution for the associated trace functions;
4. extract the specific projective hitting statistic.

The obstruction is that the index r is the recurrence time, not an independent algebraic parameter on the base variety.

# Final answer

(A) **Is the orbit equidistributed?**

Not known. It is plausible numerically, but polynomial cocycle generation does not imply it. AFP is much weaker than full equidistribution.

(B) **p=89 histogram?**

The experiment is a good test. A random-like orbit should have mostly 0/1 occupancy with a small tail at 2 or 3. But such a histogram would be evidence, not proof.

(C) **Does the PGL₂ image generate?**

Very likely yes for generic p. This removes algebraic subgroup obstructions, but does not provide mixing.

(D) **Does SWAP help?**

It gives symmetry and pairing, but not equidistribution. It is useful for reducing the zero problem, not solving it.

(E) **Does a polynomial PGL₂ cocycle expander theorem exist?**

No general theorem of the required strength is currently available. The missing result would be a non-autonomous expander theorem for polynomial matrix cocycles.

The key conclusion for P3.2 is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{PGL}_2\text{ generation is not the missing ingredient; quantitative orbit mixing is.}
}
$$

The transfer-matrix formulation identifies AFP as a dynamical hitting problem, but proving bounded hits requires new input beyond current expansion technology.
