# P3.2 — holonomic zero sets and the singular Apéry seam

## Executive verdict

There is a useful holonomic-zero-set theory here, but it is **not** an off-the-shelf Skolem–Mahler–Lech theorem, and the singular step at \(n=p-1\) does not by itself force \(|Z_p|\) to be small.

The precise answer is:

1. For \(p\ge 7\), the two singular ends of the one-digit recurrence are \(n=0\) for backward propagation and \(n=-1\equiv p-1\) for forward propagation. The right singular step gives the exact seam
   
   \[
   b_{p-2}\equiv 5b_{p-1}\pmod p.
   \]
   
   Since \(b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\), this gives \(b_{p-2}\equiv5\). Together with \(b_0=1,b_1=5\), reversal invariance gives
   
   \[
   b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p.
   \]
   
   Thus the seam explains reflection and the central parity of \(|Z_p|\).

2. On every interior step \(1\le r\le p-2\), the transfer matrix is invertible. Hence there are no two consecutive zeros. Seam, reflection, and nearest-neighbor exclusion give only an \(O(p)\) bound; they do not give sublinearity.

3. General characteristic-zero zero-set theorems for polynomial-coefficient recurrences are qualitative, apply to a fixed sequence, and are not uniform in the varying characteristic \(p\). Positive-characteristic theorems give **automaticity**, not a small cardinality. In the Apéry case automaticity is already tautological from Gessel’s Lucas law.

4. The project’s gap-polynomial method is exactly the effective, Apéry-specific holonomic zero-set theory. A return from a zero at \(r\) to another zero at \(r+h\) forces
   
   \[
   N_h(r)=0,
   \qquad \deg N_h\le 3(h-1).
   \]
   
   The boundary evaluations at \(-1\) and \(-2\)—the singular seam viewed modulo \(p\)—prove that \(N_h\not\equiv0\pmod p\). Summing generic root bounds over short gaps and treating long gaps by length gives the current
   
   \[
   |Z_p|=O(p^{2/3}).
   \]
   
   So the singular behavior is already being used in the strongest banked vertical theorem.

5. To beat \(p^{2/3}\), one needs a new **aggregate return-incidence theorem** for the family \(N_h\), not more local analysis at \(p-1\). A power saving
   
   \[
   I_p(H):=\#\{(r,h):2\le h\le H,\ N_h(r)=0\}
   \ll H^{2-\delta}p^{o(1)}
   \]
   
   would yield
   
   \[
   |Z_p|\ll p^{(2-\delta)/(3-\delta)+o(1)}.
   \]
   
   The endpoint \(\delta=1\) gives \(p^{1/2+o(1)}\). This is the clean Stepanov/return-variety target already foreshadowed by the project’s `q74_stepanov_apery.txt`.

In compact form:

```text
generic holonomicity                         -> no uniform saving
singular seam + self-adjoint recurrence      -> reflection, no adjacent zeros
gap-return polynomials + seam evaluations    -> O(p^(2/3))
aggregate root/incidence saving for N_h       -> possible improvement
regular-singular behavior alone               -> not enough
```

# 1. First separate three different zero-set questions

The phrase “zero set of the Apéry holonomic sequence” hides three mathematically different problems.

## 1.1 Characteristic zero

Over the integers,

\[
b_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2\binom{n+k}{k}^2>0,
\]

so the literal zero set of the characteristic-zero Apéry sequence is empty. General theorems about zeros of a fixed P-recursive sequence over a characteristic-zero field therefore say nothing about the congruence-zero problem.

## 1.2 Fix one prime and consider all indices

For fixed \(p\), Gessel’s Lucas congruence says that if

\[
n=n_0+n_1p+\cdots+n_sp^s,
\qquad 0\le n_i<p,
\]

then

\[
b_n\equiv\prod_{i=0}^s b_{n_i}\pmod p.
\]

Consequently

\[
p\mid b_n
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{at least one base-\(p\) digit \(n_i\) lies in \(Z_p\)}.
\]

Thus the infinite zero set modulo \(p\) is completely determined by the first digit block \(Z_p\).

## 1.3 Vary \(p\) and bound the first digit block

The P3.2 vertical problem is

\[
Z_p=\{0\le r<p:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\},
\qquad |Z_p|\ 	ext{uniformly in }p.
\]

This is the hard problem. A theorem describing the *shape* of the infinite zero language for each fixed \(p\) does not estimate how many alphabet symbols are already zero in the first block.

That distinction is decisive below.

# 2. The exact finite-field singular boundary problem

Write

\[
P(n)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5
     =(2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5).
\]

The recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

Modulo \(p\), forward propagation is nonsingular for

\[
0\le n\le p-2,
\]

because \(n+1\ne0\). The coefficient of the backward term vanishes at \(n=0\), and the coefficient of the forward term vanishes at

\[
n=-1\equiv p-1.
\]

It is therefore more precise to call this a **two-ended singular difference boundary problem**. The phrase “regular singular at \(n=p-1\)” is reasonable informally, but it should not be confused with the regular singularities of the Picard–Fuchs differential equation in its generating-function variable.

## 2.1 The right seam

At \(n=p-1\equiv-1\), equation (2.1) becomes

\[
0=P(-1)b_{p-1}-(-1)^3b_{p-2}.
\]

Since

\[
P(-1)=-5,
\]

we obtain

\[
\boxed{b_{p-2}=5b_{p-1}\pmod p.}
\tag{2.2}
\]

For the Apéry numbers,

\[
\boxed{b_{p-1}=1\pmod p.}
\tag{2.3}
\]

Indeed, in

\[
b_{p-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}
 \binom{p-1}{k}^2\binom{p-1+k}{k}^2,
\]

the second binomial coefficient is divisible by \(p\) for every \(1\le k\le p-1\), so only \(k=0\) survives. Hence, for \(p\ne5\),

\[
\boxed{b_{p-2}=5\pmod p.}
\tag{2.4}
\]

At \(p=5\), the scalar \(5\) itself vanishes. The seam gives \(b_3=0\); since \(b_1=5=0\), this recovers the reflected pair

\[
Z_5=\{1,3\}.
\]

## 2.2 Reversal and reflection

The middle coefficient satisfies

\[
P(-1-r)=-P(r).
\tag{2.5}
\]

Define the reversed sequence

\[
\widetilde b_r=b_{p-1-r}.
\]

Using (2.5), the recurrence at the index \(-1-r\) transforms into the same recurrence at \(r\):

\[
(r+1)^3\widetilde b_{r+1}
=P(r)\widetilde b_r-r^3\widetilde b_{r-1}
\qquad(1\le r\le p-2).
\]

The seam gives

\[
\widetilde b_0=b_{p-1},
\qquad
\widetilde b_1=b_{p-2}=5b_{p-1}.
\]

Uniqueness of forward propagation therefore gives the multiplier form

\[
\widetilde b_r=b_{p-1}b_r.
\]

Using (2.3),

\[
\boxed{b_{p-1-r}=b_r\pmod p.}
\tag{2.6}
\]

Thus \(Z_p\) is invariant under \(r\mapsto p-1-r\). If

\[
m=(p-1)/2,
\]

then

\[
|Z_p|=2M_p+\varepsilon_p,
\qquad
\varepsilon_p=1_{\{p\mid b_m\}},
\tag{2.7}
\]

where \(M_p\) counts lower-half noncentral zeros.

The factor \(2n+1\) in \(P(n)\) also vanishes at \(n=m\). At that point the recurrence gives

\[
b_{m+1}=b_{m-1},
\]

which is the local midpoint manifestation of reflection. Again, it pairs data; it does not bound the number of pairs.

## 2.3 Interior transfer and no adjacent zeros

For \(1\le r\le p-2\), put

\[
V_r=\binom{b_{r+1}}{b_r}.
\]

Then

\[
V_r=M_rV_{r-1},
\qquad
M_r=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(r)/(r+1)^3&-r^3/(r+1)^3\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2.8}
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det M_r=\frac{r^3}{(r+1)^3}\ne0.
\tag{2.9}
\]

Hence the projective state never loses information in the interior. In particular, if \(b_r=b_{r+1}=0\), backward propagation forces \(b_0=0\), a contradiction. Therefore

\[
\boxed{Z_p\text{ contains no two consecutive residues}.}
\tag{2.10}
\]

This gives only the linear bound

\[
|Z_p|\le (p-1)/2.
\]

Reflection changes constants and parity, not the exponent.

# 3. Why “regular singular” is not a finite-field Sturm theorem

Over an ordered field, a self-adjoint three-term recurrence can support Sturm oscillation and nodal-count arguments. Equation (2.1) is indeed Jacobi/self-adjoint in the sense that the two off-diagonal coefficients are consecutive cubes.

Over \(\mathbf F_p\), however, there is no sign, no positivity, and no eigenvalue ordering. The strongest universal nodal statement for an irreducible tridiagonal recurrence is essentially nearest-neighbor exclusion.

There is a sharp counterexample with the same off-diagonal coefficients and the same singular endpoints. Consider

\[
(n+1)^3u_{n+1}=-n^3u_{n-1},
\qquad
u_0=1,\quad u_1=0.
\tag{3.1}
\]

For every odd prime \(p\),

\[
u_{2k+1}=0,
\]

while

\[
u_{2k}=(-1)^k
\left(\prod_{j=1}^k\frac{2j-1}{2j}\right)^3\ne0
\qquad(2k<p).
\tag{3.2}
\]

At the singular step \(n=p-1\), compatibility requires \(u_{p-2}=0\), which already holds. Writing \(p=2m+1\), the odd and even products satisfy

\[
\prod_{j=1}^m(2j-1)
=(-1)^m\prod_{j=1}^m2j
\pmod p,
\]

so (3.2) gives

\[
u_{p-1}=1.
\]

The reversed solution has the same initial data, hence this example also has exact reflection

\[
u_{p-1-r}=u_r.
\]

It therefore has all of the following:

```text
order two;
polynomial coefficients of fixed degree;
self-adjoint off-diagonal cubes;
interior transfer matrices invertible;
the same singular endpoints 0 and -1;
full seam compatibility;
endpoint normalization u_0=u_{p-1}=1;
exact reflection;
no consecutive zeros;
(p-1)/2 zeros in one block.
```

This proves a useful no-go statement:

> No theorem depending only on order, fixed polynomial degree, self-adjointness, the singularity type, seam closure, reflection, and nearest-neighbor exclusion can give \(|Z_p|=o(p)\).

Any sublinear theorem must exploit the **specific Apéry middle coefficient and its iterated return maps**.

# 4. What general holonomic zero-set theory actually provides

## 4.1 Characteristic-zero P-recursive zero sets

Bell, Burris, and Yeats proved a Skolem–Mahler–Lech-type theorem for certain polynomial-coefficient recurrences over characteristic-zero fields. Under a nondegeneracy condition—most notably that the furthest-lag polynomial is a nonzero constant—the zero set is a finite union of arithmetic progressions and a finite set.

This does not solve the present problem for three independent reasons.

First, it concerns a fixed characteristic-zero sequence, whereas \(Z_p\) is a congruence-zero set that changes with \(p\).

Second, it is qualitative, not a uniform bound for the number of zeros in a block whose length is the characteristic.

Third, the directly cleared Apéry recurrence does not satisfy its stated constant-tail hypothesis. If

\[
c_n=(n!)^3b_n,
\]

then

\[
c_{n+1}=P(n)c_n-n^6c_{n-1},
\tag{4.1}
\]

whose furthest coefficient is \(-n^6\), not a nonzero constant.

Even if one found a gauge placing the characteristic-zero equation in a related class, a finite union of arithmetic progressions can occupy a positive fraction of a finite field block. It would still not be a uniform cardinality theorem.

## 4.2 Positive-characteristic Skolem–Mahler–Lech

For constant-coefficient linear recurrences in characteristic \(p\), Derksen proved that the zero set is describable by a finite automaton. Positive characteristic admits much more complicated zero sets than finite unions of progressions.

Adamczewski and Bell proved a broader statement for coefficients of algebraic power series over fields of positive characteristic: the zero-index set is \(p\)-automatic.

These are structural classification theorems, not sparsity theorems. Automatic sets can have density \(0\), positive density, or density \(1\), and a small automaton need not mean a small first-digit zero set.

## 4.3 In the Apéry case, automaticity is already tautological

Let

\[
B(T)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nT^n
\]

in \(\mathbf F_p[[T]]\), and let

\[
A_p(T)=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}b_rT^r.
\]

Gessel’s Lucas law gives

\[
B(T)=A_p(T)B(T^p).
\tag{4.2}
\]

Since \(B(T^p)=B(T)^p\) in characteristic \(p\),

\[
\boxed{A_p(T)B(T)^{p-1}=1.}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Thus \(B(T)\) is algebraic and its coefficient-zero set is \(p\)-automatic.

But the zero indicator is recognized by a two-state automaton:

```text
state ALIVE:
    read digit d
    if d in Z_p, move to DEAD
    otherwise remain ALIVE

state DEAD:
    remain DEAD on every digit
```

The automaton has two states whether \(|Z_p|=0\), \(2\), \(p/2\), or \(p-1\). The complexity is stored in the set of digit labels that send `ALIVE` to `DEAD`, which is exactly the unknown set \(Z_p\).

Indeed, Lucas gives the exact count

\[
\#\{0\le n<p^k:p\nmid b_n\}=(p-|Z_p|)^k.
\tag{4.4}
\]

So automaticity propagates the first-block answer; it does not estimate it.

## 4.4 D-finiteness is especially weak after reduction modulo \(p\)

The generating function also satisfies the Picard–Fuchs equation

\[
\left[
\theta^3
-T(2\theta+1)(17\theta^2+17\theta+5)
+T^2(\theta+1)^3
\right]B(T)=0,
\qquad \theta=T\frac d{dT}.
\tag{4.5}
\]

The regular singularities of this differential equation control analytic/Frobenius behavior in the variable \(T\). They do not directly control which *coefficients* \(b_r\) vanish modulo \(p\).

Moreover, differential equations lose rigidity in characteristic \(p\): derivatives annihilate every \(p\)-th power series. Thus “D-finite modulo \(p\)” is far too broad to imply a useful coefficient-support bound without a separate Cartier/Frobenius analysis.

# 5. The project’s gap polynomials are the relevant holonomic zero-set theory

The effective way to count zeros is not to view each zero in isolation. One studies **returns to the zero divisor** under the recurrence transfer.

Suppose

\[
b_r=0,
\qquad b_{r+1}\ne0,
\]

and \(r+h\le p-1\). Iterating the recurrence gives

\[
b_{r+h}
=\frac{N_h(r)}{\prod_{j=2}^{h}(r+j)^3}\,b_{r+1},
\tag{5.1}
\]

where

\[
N_1(X)=1,
\qquad
N_2(X)=P(X+1),
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
N_{h+1}(X)=P(X+h)N_h(X)-(X+h)^6N_{h-1}(X).
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

The committed Lean file `lean/RamanujanChallenge/Problem32/GapPolynomial.lean` formalizes this continuant recurrence and the bound

\[
\deg N_h\le3(h-1).
\tag{5.3}
\]

If both endpoints are zero, then no-consecutive-zeros makes \(b_{r+1}\) a unit, while the denominator in (5.1) is a unit. Hence

\[
\boxed{b_r=b_{r+h}=0\Longrightarrow N_h(r)=0.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

This is a genuine holonomic return theorem: a gap of length \(h\) is cut out by one explicit polynomial of degree \(O(h)\).

## 5.1 Where the singular seam enters

The key danger is that \(N_h\) might become the zero polynomial modulo \(p\), in which case its degree gives no root bound. The project excludes this using two exact boundary evaluations:

\[
\boxed{N_h(-1)=b_{h-1}((h-1)!)^3,}
\tag{5.5}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{N_h(-2)=-5b_{h-2}((h-2)!)^3.}
\tag{5.6}
\]

For \(2\le h\le p\), the factorial factors are units modulo \(p\). If \(N_h\equiv0\pmod p\), equations (5.5)–(5.6) force

\[
b_{h-2}=b_{h-1}=0,
\]

contradicting nearest-neighbor exclusion.

Modulo \(p\), the points \(-1\) and \(-2\) are \(p-1\) and \(p-2\): the singular endpoint and its neighbor. Thus the regular-singular seam is not irrelevant. It is exactly what certifies that every return polynomial is nontrivial.

This answers the user’s central question in the strongest positive form:

> **Yes, the behavior at \(n=p-1\) can be used to bound \(|Z_p|\), and the current \(O(p^{2/3})\) theorem already uses it through the boundary nonvanishing of the gap polynomials.**

## 5.2 The \(p^{2/3}\) optimization

Order the zeros in \([0,p-1]\). Fix \(H<p\).

Every consecutive zero gap of length \(h\le H\) contributes a root of \(N_h\). Therefore the number of short gaps is at most

\[
\sum_{h=2}^{H}\deg N_h
\le
3\sum_{h=2}^{H}(h-1)
=O(H^2).
\tag{5.7}
\]

Every remaining gap has length \(>H\), so there are at most \(p/H\) long gaps. Hence

\[
|Z_p|\ll H^2+\frac pH.
\tag{5.8}
\]

Choosing \(H\asymp p^{1/3}\) yields

\[
\boxed{|Z_p|=O(p^{2/3}).}
\tag{5.9}
\]

This is stronger than anything supplied by generic holonomic zero-set classification.

# 6. The full-cycle singular closure gives a degree-two defect

There is another exact way to package the seam which is worth banking, even though it does not by itself improve (5.9).

Let

\[
\mathscr B_p(X)\in\mathbf F_p[X],
\qquad \deg\mathscr B_p\le p-1,
\]

be the unique interpolation polynomial satisfying

\[
\mathscr B_p(r)=b_r
\qquad(r\in\mathbf F_p).
\]

Define the difference operator

\[
(\mathcal Lf)(X)
=(X+1)^3f(X+1)-P(X)f(X)+X^3f(X-1).
\tag{6.1}
\]

For \(1\le r\le p-2\), the recurrence gives

\[
(\mathcal L\mathscr B_p)(r)=0.
\]

At \(r=0\), the backward coefficient vanishes and \(b_1=5b_0\), so the residual is also zero. At \(r=-1\), the forward coefficient vanishes and the seam (2.2) makes the residual zero. Consequently

\[
\mathcal L\mathscr B_p
\]

vanishes at every point of \(\mathbf F_p\). Its degree is at most \(p+2\), so

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L\mathscr B_p=(X^p-X)Q_p(X),
\qquad \deg Q_p\le2.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

This is the cleanest global expression of the singular boundary closure: the \(p-2\) ordinary recurrence rows plus the two singular boundary rows force the entire residual into a degree-two Frobenius defect.

However, (6.2) is **not** a root-count theorem. At an interior root \(r\) of \(\mathscr B_p\), it gives only

\[
(r+1)^3\mathscr B_p(r+1)
=-r^3\mathscr B_p(r-1),
\tag{6.3}
\]

which is the same isolated-zero transport already used in (2.10). A degree-\((p-1)\) polynomial can have \(p-1\) simple roots; a low-degree defect for its difference equation does not make its root set small.

Differentiating (6.2) can control first jets and root multiplicities. It does not control the number of simple roots. This is the same distinction exposed by the derivative-companion audit in Q3566: transversality is not sparsity.

A difference-Mason, Stepanov, or shift-gcd theorem applied to (6.2) would have to use much more than the fact that \(\deg Q_p\le2\). In characteristic \(p\), the factor \(X^p-X\) also makes ordinary shift-radical arguments degenerate on the full field.

# 7. Why the seam cannot improve \(p^{2/3}\) by itself

The current proof loses at one exact place:

\[
\#\{r:N_h(r)=0\}\le\deg N_h=O(h).
\tag{7.1}
\]

The seam proves that \(N_h\) is nonzero. Reflection gives symmetries of its boundary data. Neither assertion says that \(N_h\) has \(O(1)\) roots rather than \(O(h)\) roots.

Summing (7.1) for \(h\le H\) creates the \(H^2\) term. Any argument which continues to treat the \(N_h\) independently and uses only their degrees will reproduce the \(2/3\) exponent.

The project has an exact adjacent-resultant identity

\[
\left|\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_{h+1})\right|
=
\prod_{j=1}^{h-1}(j!^3b_j)^6.
\tag{7.2}
\]

This is valuable: before the first Apéry zero, adjacent return polynomials have no common root modulo \(p\). But pairwise coprimality alone still allows \(N_h\) to have \(O(h)\) distinct roots, and the right side becomes exceptional precisely when \(p\) divides an earlier Apéry value. Thus (7.2) does not by itself close a uniform induction.

Likewise, for a fixed \(h\), large Galois group plus Chebotarev can predict an \(O(1)\) average number of roots as \(p\) varies. P3.2 needs a pointwise statement with \(h\) growing with \(p\); fixed-\(h\) Chebotarev does not control exceptional primes uniformly.

# 8. The exact new theorem that would improve the bound

Define the admissible short-return incidence

\[
I_p(H)=
\#\left\{
(r,h):
\begin{array}{l}
2\le h\le H,\\
0\le r<r+h\le p-1,\\
N_h(r)=0
\end{array}
\right\}.
\tag{8.1}
\]

The gap decomposition gives the deterministic inequality

\[
\boxed{|Z_p|\ll I_p(H)+p/H.}
\tag{8.2}
\]

The generic degree argument gives \(I_p(H)=O(H^2)\). The natural improved interface is:

> **Holonomic return-incidence theorem \(\mathrm{HRI}_\delta\).** There exists \(\delta>0\) such that, uniformly for a useful range of \(H<p\),
> 
> \[
> I_p(H)\ll H^{2-\delta}p^{o(1)}.
> \tag{8.3}
> \]

Balancing the two terms in (8.2) with

\[
H=p^{1/(3-\delta)}
\]

gives

\[
\boxed{
|Z_p|\ll
p^{(2-\delta)/(3-\delta)+o(1)}.
}
\tag{8.4}
\]

Thus:

```text
delta = 0   -> p^(2/3), the current generic-degree bound
delta > 0   -> a genuine improvement
delta = 1   -> p^(1/2+o(1))
```

There are four plausible ways to attack (8.3).

## 8.1 Stepanov on the return variety

Treat the incidence set

\[
\{(r,h):N_h(r)=0\}
\]

as the coincidence variety of the nonautonomous projective recurrence. Construct an auxiliary polynomial that vanishes to high multiplicity on many return pairs, using continuant addition laws to supply algebraic relations between different \(h\).

This is the most literal “holonomic zero-set theorem” that could improve the exponent. It is also the route already formulated in

```text
problems/3.2/campaign3_questions/q74_stepanov_apery.txt
```

## 8.2 Uniform root sparsity for each return polynomial

A theorem of the form

\[
\#\{r\in\mathbf F_p:N_h(r)=0\}
\ll p^{o(1)}h^{o(1)}
\tag{8.5}
\]

for all \(h\le p^{1/2-\epsilon}\) would give \(I_p(H)\ll H^{1+o(1)}\) and hence the square-root-scale bound. This is much stronger than a degree estimate and cannot follow from irreducibility over \(\mathbf Q\) alone.

## 8.3 Multi-\(h\) resultant and addition-law control

The continuant family has addition/subtraction identities. One can seek a theorem saying that a point \(r\) cannot be a root for many different return lengths \(h\), except on a small explicitly classified singular locus. This attacks the sum over \(h\) directly rather than each polynomial separately.

## 8.4 Geometric monodromy of the zero divisor

One may try to realize the return equations as trace or Hasse sections of a bounded-complexity family and prove large monodromy for their zero cycles. Existing trace-function bounds do not automatically apply to the indicator of a trace-zero set; a new zero-divisor sheaf or convolution-energy theorem would be required.

The singular seam can provide boundary conditions and rule out identically zero fibers in all four approaches. It does not supply the needed incidence saving by itself.

# 9. A useful projective-orbit formulation

On the chart \(b_{r-1}\ne0\), set

\[
x_r=\frac{b_r}{b_{r-1}}.
\]

Then the recurrence becomes the nonautonomous Riccati iteration

\[
\boxed{
 x_{r+1}
 =\frac{P(r)}{(r+1)^3}
 -\frac{r^3}{(r+1)^3x_r}.
}
\tag{9.1}
\]

A zero \(b_r=0\) is a visit to the projective point \(x_r=0\); the following state is forced to infinity in the reciprocal chart, and a return after \(h\) steps is exactly \(N_h(r)=0\).

The singular seam says that this nonautonomous orbit closes under reversal after one full digit. It is a monodromy/boundary condition on the entire trajectory. Counting visits to one projective point is a different problem. For an autonomous group orbit, subgroup closure supplies many algebraic relations and Stepanov can be powerful. Here the map changes with \(r\), so the missing analogue is precisely the continuant return/addition structure across the \(N_h\).

This is why the promising new object is not merely

\[
\mathcal Lb=0,
\]

but the two-variable return family

\[
(r,h)\longmapsto N_h(r).
\]

# 10. Small-prime interpretation

The small primes fit the boundary picture cleanly.

## \(p=2\)

The stronger integral congruence \(b_n\equiv1\pmod4\) implies

\[
Z_2=\varnothing.
\]

## \(p=3\)

The sequence reduces to

\[
b_n\equiv(-1)^n\pmod3,
\]

so

\[
Z_3=\varnothing.
\]

## \(p=5\)

Here \(b_1=5\equiv0\). The exceptional seam coefficient \(P(-1)=-5\equiv0\) forces \(b_3=0\), while \(b_0,b_2,b_4\) are units. Thus

\[
Z_5=\{1,3\}.
\]

For \(p\ge7\), the seam scalar \(5\) is a unit, and the generic reflection/nonconsecutive/gap-polynomial theory applies without this degeneration.

# 11. Recommended next steps

The smallest mathematically meaningful continuation is not another general literature search for “zeros of holonomic sequences.” It is the following exact program.

1. **Bank the full-cycle defect identity (6.2)** in the research notes and verify \(Q_p\) symbolically for a prime range. Record whether \(Q_p\) has any constrained parity, reciprocity, or fixed-dimensional span. Do not infer root sparsity merely from \(\deg Q_p\le2\).

2. **Compute \(I_p(H)\), not only \(|Z_p|\).** Measure
   
   \[
   I_p(H),
   \qquad
   \max_r\#\{h\le H:N_h(r)=0\},
   \qquad
   \#\{r:N_h(r)=N_k(r)=0\}.
   \]
   
   These statistics distinguish a true aggregate saving from the already-known small marginal root count.

3. **Derive the exact continuant addition law** for \(N_{h+k}(r)\) in terms of the two fundamental return numerators at lengths \(h\) and \(k\). This is the algebraic relation supply needed by a Stepanov argument.

4. **Attempt \(\mathrm{HRI}_\delta\) first for one fixed \(\delta>0\).** Even a small power saving over \(H^2\) improves the current \(p^{2/3}\) exponent. The square-root endpoint is not required for the first proof.

5. **Use the seam only as a boundary/nondegeneracy input.** Its appropriate jobs are to prove reflection, identify endpoint rows, and ensure return polynomials or auxiliary determinants are nonzero. Asking it to count all interior returns is structurally too strong.

# Final answer

There is no known general theorem saying that an order-two polynomial-coefficient recurrence over \(\mathbf F_p\), even a self-adjoint one with regular singular endpoints, has \(o(p)\) zeros in a period-sized block. Such a theorem is false without extra hypotheses: the recurrence (3.1) has the same singular off-diagonal structure, exact seam closure and reflection, but \((p-1)/2\) zeros.

For the Apéry recurrence, the singular behavior at \(p-1\) is nevertheless useful and already fully exploited at the present vertical level:

\[
\text{seam}
\Longrightarrow
\text{reflection and boundary evaluations}
\Longrightarrow
N_h\not\equiv0
\Longrightarrow
|Z_p|=O(p^{2/3}).
\]

The obstruction beyond \(p^{2/3}\) is not local regular-singular analysis. It is the generic estimate

\[
\sum_{h\le H}\#\{r:N_h(r)=0\}=O(H^2).
\]

A genuinely stronger “holonomic zero-set theorem” for P3.2 would therefore be an aggregate incidence theorem for the return-polynomial family \(N_h\), such as \(\mathrm{HRI}_\delta\). That is the correct new angle. It is Apéry-specific, compatible with Stepanov/continuant methods, and sharply quantifies exactly how much improvement in \(|Z_p|\) each incidence saving would buy.
