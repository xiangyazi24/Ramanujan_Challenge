# Direct symmetric-index attack on the Apéry carrier

## Verdict

There is a useful exact symmetric-index identity, but it is **not** a scalar composite-modulus reflection. The correct mod-p state has two branches.

For a prime \(p\mid m\), write \(m=qp\), \(n=tp+a\) with \(0\le a<p\), and put
\[
 h=t-q,\qquad \ell=q-1-h=2q-t-1.
\]
For every admissible top-half \(m\) one has \(0\le h,\ell\le q-1\), and the exact Apéry--Lucas/reflection calculation gives
\[
 \boxed{(b_r,b_s)\equiv b_a\,(b_h,b_\ell)\pmod p,}
 \qquad r=n-m,\quad s=m-1-r.
 \tag{A}
\]
Consequently
\[
 \boxed{p\mid b_r,\ p\mid b_s
 \iff
 \bigl(p\mid b_a\bigr)
 \ \text{or}\ 
 \bigl(p\mid b_h\ \text{and}\ p\mid b_\ell\bigr).}
 \tag{B}
\]
The first alternative is the desired one-digit target; the second is the precise composite-multiple contamination.

This has a genuinely useful global consequence. For \(P>\sqrt n\), every secondary-branch prime \(p>P\) divides Apéry numbers with indices \(<n/P\). Hence **all** secondary-branch primes occurring in the lcm over all \(m\) are supported on the single nonzero arithmetic factor
\[
 E_n(P):=\operatorname{rad}\!\left(\operatorname{lcm}_{0\le j<J} b_j\right),
 \qquad J=\left\lceil\frac nP\right\rceil,
 \tag{C}
\]
and
\[
 \log E_n(P)=O(J^2)=O((n/P)^2).
 \tag{D}
\]
After removing this factor, the high-prime part of \(C_n\) is **exactly** the primary one-digit carrier. Combining (D) with the low-prime lcm bound and choosing \(P=n^{2/3}\) gives an \(O(n^{2/3})=o(n)\) total loss. Thus composite multiples of a high prime can be saturated away at sublinear height; they are not an additional all-index obstruction.

What fails is equally sharp:

* \(b_{m-1-r}-b_r\) is not generally divisible by \(m\), or even by a chosen prime \(p\mid m\). There is an infinite counterexample family for arbitrarily large \(p\).
* A characteristic-zero reflection exists termwise, but its natural universal integer-valued-polynomial clearing contains the target \(p\) for every genuinely composite witness \(m=qp\), \(q\ge2\).
* The first divided quotient after prime reflection is not a consequence of the target condition. There is an admissible target example at \(p=73\) where both endpoint Apéry numbers vanish mod \(73\), but the quotient is \(9\pmod{73}\).
* The ordinary resultant/elimination ideal is forced to be zero because the two endpoint equations have a common primary factor and a secondary component. Saturation is necessary, and (C) is an arithmetic saturation certificate of sublinear aggregate height.

The smallest viable interface left by this attack is therefore the primary digit condition
\[
 p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p},
 \tag{E}
\]
with the top-half admissibility of a multiple \(qp\). A further attack must retain the Lucas block state (or a first Lucas-lift defect); a scalar normalized endpoint difference cannot do it.

## 2. Exact characteristic-zero reflection and its denominators

Set
\[
 u_k(x):=\binom{x}{k}\binom{x+k}{k}
 =\frac{F_k(x)}{(k!)^2},
 \qquad
 F_k(x):=\prod_{j=-k+1}^{k}(x+j).
 \tag{4}
\]
Then \(u_k\in\mathbb Q[x]\) is integer-valued on every integer, has degree \(2k\), and
\[
 F_k(-1-x)=F_k(x),\qquad u_k(-1-x)=u_k(x).
 \tag{5}
\]
For \(T\ge0\), define
\[
 B_T(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{T}u_k(x)^2.
 \tag{6}
\]
Then
\[
 B_T(-1-x)=B_T(x).
 \tag{7}
\]
If \(r,s\ge0\) and \(T=\max(r,s)\), terms beyond the ordinary endpoint vanish, so
\[
 B_T(r)=b_r,\qquad B_T(s)=b_s.
 \tag{8}
\]
With \(s=m-1-r=(-1-r)+m\), (7) gives the exact divided-difference identity
\[
 b_s-b_r=B_T((-1-r)+m)-B_T(-1-r).
 \tag{9}
\]
More explicitly,
\[
 H_k(m,r):=
 \frac{F_k(m-1-r)-F_k(-1-r)}{m}
 \in\mathbb Z[m,r],
 \tag{10}
\]
and therefore
\[
 \boxed{
 b_s-b_r
 =m\sum_{k=0}^{T}
 \frac{H_k(m,r)\bigl(u_k(s)+u_k(r)\bigr)}{(k!)^2}.}
 \tag{11}
\]
This is an exact characteristic-zero reflection formula with all denominators visible. The crude clearing is
\[
 m\mid (T!)^2(b_s-b_r).
 \tag{12}
\]
There is a better universal clearing.

### Integer-valued finite-difference lemma

Let \(L_d=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,d)\), \(L_0=1\).

**Lemma.** If \(f\in\mathbb Q[x]\) is integer-valued on \(\mathbb Z\) and \(\deg f\le d\), then for every integer \(x\) and positive integer \(m\),
\[
 \boxed{m\mid L_d(f(x+m)-f(x)).}
 \tag{13}
\]
Moreover \(L_d\) is the minimal positive universal multiplier valid for all such degree-\(\le d\) integer-valued polynomials.

**Proof.** Write the Newton expansion
\[
 f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_j\binom{x}{j},\qquad c_j\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Vandermonde gives
\[
 \binom{x+m}{j}-\binom{x}{j}
 =\sum_{i=1}^{j}\binom{x}{j-i}\binom{m}{i},
\]
so
\[
 \frac{L_d}{m}
 \left(\binom{x+m}{j}-\binom{x}{j}\right)
 =\sum_{i=1}^{j}
 \binom{x}{j-i}\frac{L_d}{i}\binom{m-1}{i-1}\in\mathbb Z.
\]
For minimality, if a universal multiplier \(M\) works, take \(f(x)=\binom{x}{j}\), \(x=0\), \(m=j\). The difference is \(1\), so \(j\mid M\) for every \(j\le d\), hence \(L_d\mid M\). \(\square\)

Apply the lemma to \(u_k\) at \(x=-1-r\). From (5),
\[
 m\mid L_{2k}(u_k(s)-u_k(r)).
\]
Multiplying by \(u_k(s)+u_k(r)\in\mathbb Z\), using \(L_{2k}\mid L_{2T}\), and summing gives
\[
 \boxed{m\mid L_{2T}(b_s-b_r),\qquad T=\max(r,s).}
 \tag{14}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 \boxed{
 \frac{m}{\gcd(m,L_{2T})}\mid b_s-b_r.}
 \tag{15}
\]
This is a genuine characteristic-zero/composite-modulus reflection congruence, but exactly too weak for the target primes.

If \(m=qp\) with \(q\ge2\), then \(r+s=m-1\), hence
\[
 T\ge\left\lceil\frac{m-1}{2}\right\rceil\ge p.
\]
Thus \(p\mid L_{2T}\): the universal clearing has swallowed the target prime. Also
\[
 \log L_{2T}=\psi(2T)=\Theta(T),
 \tag{16}
\]
so when \(T=\Theta(n)\) its logarithmic height is linear. The minimality statement is deliberately narrow: it rules out improvement based only on generic integer-valued-polynomial finite differences; a special Apéry cancellation would need extra structure.

## 3. Raw composite reflection is false for arbitrarily large primes

Let \(p\ge3\) be prime and take
\[
 n=m=2p,\qquad r=0,\qquad s=2p-1.
\]
This is an admissible top-half pair. Apéry--Lucas and prime reflection give
\[
 b_{2p-1}\equiv b_1b_{p-1}\equiv5b_0\equiv5\pmod p,
\]
so
\[
 \boxed{b_{2p-1}-b_0\equiv4\pmod p.}
 \tag{17}
\]
Thus \(p\mid m\) but \(p\nmid b_s-b_r\) for every \(p\ge3\).

Consequently no scalar normalization \(N(m,r)\in\mathbb Z\) can both
\[
 m\mid N(m,r)(b_s-b_r)
 \tag{18}
\]
universally and remain a \(p\)-unit at every target prime \(p\mid m\): (17) forces \(p\mid N(2p,0)\).

The first member is

```text
p=3, n=m=6, r=0, s=5
b_0=1, b_5=819005
b_5-b_0=819004 = 4 (mod 6) = 1 (mod 3).
```

Another small top-half example is \(n=7,m=6,r=1,s=4\), where \(b_4-b_1=32996\equiv2\pmod6\).

## 4. Recurrence reflection, Casoratian, and transfer singularity

The recurrence coefficient factors as
\[
 P(j)=(2j+1)(17j^2+17j+5)
\]
and satisfies
\[
 P(-j-1)=-P(j).
 \tag{19}
\]
Thus reflection \(j\mapsto-1-j\) preserves the recurrence. The bilateral extension \(B_{-1-j}=b_j\) is a reflected solution. But the recurrence equations at \(j=-1\) and \(j=0\) are singular; they do not force the free scale on the two halves to agree. Characteristic-zero reflection across \(-1/2\) therefore does not imply translation by \(m\).

The one-step transfer is
\[
 v_{j+1}=T_jv_j,
 \qquad
 T_j=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 P(j)/(j+1)^3&-j^3/(j+1)^3\\
 1&0
 \end{pmatrix},
 \tag{20}
\]
with
\[
 \det T_j=\left(\frac{j}{j+1}\right)^3.
 \tag{21}
\]
The integral-cleared form is
\[
 \widetilde T_j=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 P(j)&-j^3\\
 (j+1)^3&0
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \widetilde T_jv_j=(j+1)^3v_{j+1},
 \tag{22}
\]
and
\[
 \det\widetilde T_j=j^3(j+1)^3.
 \tag{23}
\]
Modulo \(p\), (22) is singular at \(j\equiv0,-1\pmod p\). In particular at \(j=p-1\) the recurrence constrains the preceding block but does not determine \(y_p\); the new block starts with a fresh state. This is exactly the freedom that the Lucas factorization records. Any attempt to carry an invertible integral two-state transfer through that boundary must introduce a factor \(p\).

The companion solution in the repository exposes the determinant side:
\[
 \boxed{a_jb_{j-1}-a_{j-1}b_j=\frac6{j^3}.}
 \tag{24}
\]
For instance \(a_2=351/4\). The known universal clearing is
\[
 L_j^3a_j\in\mathbb Z,
 \tag{25}
\]
whose logarithmic height is \(3\psi(j)=\Theta(j)\). Thus the Casoratian is a strong nonzero adjacent certificate, but naively transporting and clearing the companion at an endpoint of size \(\Theta(n)\) has linear universal height and does not remove the mod-\(p\) block component.

For \(1\le r<s\), one can also write the exact characteristic-zero transfer as
\[
 F_sF_r^{-1},\qquad
 F_j=\begin{pmatrix}a_j&b_j\\a_{j-1}&b_{j-1}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \det F_j=6/j^3.
\]
This is exact, but its entries retain the rational companion values and therefore do not yield a target-preserving scalar reflection across a \(p\)-block.

## 5. Exact symmetric-index identity modulo every prime divisor of m

Let \(p\mid m\) and write
\[
 m=qp,\qquad n=tp+a,\qquad 0\le a<p.
 \tag{26}
\]
Since \(m\le n<2m\),
\[
 q\le t\le2q-1.
\]
Define
\[
 h=t-q,\qquad \ell=2q-t-1=q-1-h.
 \tag{27}
\]
Then
\[
 0\le h,\ell\le q-1,
 \tag{28}
\]
and
\[
 r=n-m=hp+a,
 \tag{29}
\]
\[
 s=m-1-r=2m-n-1=\ell p+(p-1-a).
 \tag{30}
\]
By (3),
\[
 b_r\equiv b_ab_h\pmod p,
 \qquad
 b_s\equiv b_{p-1-a}b_\ell\pmod p.
 \tag{31}
\]
The prime reflection
\[
 b_{p-1-a}\equiv b_a\pmod p
 \tag{32}
\]
follows directly from (4)--(5): for \(k<p\), \((k!)^2\) is a \(p\)-unit and
\(u_k(p-1-a)\equiv u_k(-1-a)=u_k(a)\pmod p\); extend both finite sums to \(k<p\) and square.

Thus
\[
 \boxed{
 (b_r,b_s)\equiv b_a(b_h,b_{q-1-h})\pmod p.}
 \tag{33}
\]
The scalar defect is therefore
\[
 \boxed{
 b_s-b_r\equiv b_a(b_{q-1-h}-b_h)\pmod p.}
 \tag{34}
\]
For \(q=1\), \(h=\ell=0\), recovering the familiar prime reflection. For \(q\ge2\), the lower block generally differs.

More importantly,
\[
 \boxed{
 p\mid b_r,\ p\mid b_s
 \iff
 [p\mid b_a]
 \ \text{or}\
 [p\mid b_h\ \&\ p\mid b_{q-1-h}].}
 \tag{35}
\]
This is the exact common-zero decomposition.

Prime by prime, for fixed \(m\),
\[
 \operatorname{rad}\gcd(m,b_r,b_s)
 =\prod_{\substack{p\mid m:\\
 p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\ \text{or}\\
 (p\mid b_{h_p}\ \&\ p\mid b_{\ell_p})}}p,
 \tag{36}
\]
where
\[
 h_p=\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor-\frac mp,
 \qquad
 \ell_p=\frac mp-1-h_p.
\]
For \(p>\sqrt n\), \(p^2>m\), so this squarefree description is already the full \(p\)-part of the local carrier.

## 6. Resultant interpretation: ordinary elimination is zero, saturation is exact

Fix \(p\mid m\) and abbreviate in \(\mathbb F_p\)
\[
 x=b_a,\qquad y=b_h,\qquad z=b_\ell.
\]
The two endpoint equations are \(xy=0\), \(xz=0\), with ideal
\[
 I=(xy,xz)=x(y,z)=(x)\cap(y,z).
 \tag{37}
\]
The two components are exactly the primary branch \(x=0\) and the secondary branch \(y=z=0\).

An ordinary eliminant retaining only \(x\) cannot be nonzero:
\[
 I\cap\mathbb F_p[x]=(0),
 \tag{38}
\]
because the secondary component \(y=z=0\) projects onto every \(x\). Thus a raw scalar resultant is structurally degenerate.

The correct operation is saturation:
\[
 \boxed{I:(y,z)^\infty=(x).}
 \tag{39}
\]
Once the secondary component is excluded, the target is exactly \(x=0\). The next section performs this saturation arithmetically for all high-prime multiples at once.

## 7. Global saturation theorem for C_n

The omission \(r\ne s\) is equivalent to
\[
 r=s\iff 2n+1=3m.
 \tag{40}
\]
Define
\[
 \mathcal A_n(p):=
 \left\{q\ge1:\frac n2<qp\le n,\ 3qp\ne2n+1\right\}.
 \tag{41}
\]
Fix \(P\) with \(\sqrt n<P<n\), put
\[
 J=\left\lceil\frac nP\right\rceil,
 \qquad
 E_n(P)=\operatorname{rad}\!\left(\operatorname{lcm}_{0\le j<J}b_j\right),
 \tag{42}
\]
and define the primary high-prime carrier
\[
 D_n(P):=
 \prod_{\substack{P<p\le n\\p\text{ prime}\\
 \mathcal A_n(p)\ne\varnothing\\
 p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}p.
 \tag{43}
\]
Let \(C_n^{>P}\) be the part of \(C_n\) supported on primes \(p>P\). Since \(P>\sqrt n\), it is squarefree.

### Theorem

\[
 \boxed{
 \frac{C_n^{>P}}{\gcd(C_n^{>P},E_n(P))}
 =
 \frac{D_n(P)}{\gcd(D_n(P),E_n(P))}.}
 \tag{44}
\]
Equivalently, for every \(p>P\) with \(p\nmid E_n(P)\),
\[
 \boxed{p\mid C_n\iff p\mid D_n(P).}
 \tag{45}
\]

**Proof.** Suppose \(p>P\), \(p\nmid E_n(P)\), and \(p\mid C_n\). Some admissible \(m=qp\) contributes \(p\). Since \(p>P\),
\[
 q\le n/p<n/P\le J,
\]
so \(q\le J-1\), and (28) gives \(h,\ell<J\). Since \(p\nmid E_n(P)\), neither \(b_h\) nor \(b_\ell\) vanishes mod \(p\). The secondary alternative in (35) is impossible; hence \(p\mid b_a\), \(a=n\bmod p\). The same \(q\) proves \(\mathcal A_n(p)\ne\varnothing\), so \(p\mid D_n(P)\).

Conversely, if \(p\mid D_n(P)\), choose \(q\in\mathcal A_n(p)\) and \(m=qp\). Then \(p\mid b_a\), and (33) gives \(p\mid b_r,b_s\), so \(p\mid C_n\). This converse does not need \(p\nmid E_n(P)\). Primewise comparison proves (44). \(\square\)

This is the direct lcm control requested in the question. A secondary high prime attached to any \(m\) divides
\[
 \gcd(b_h,b_{q-1-h}),\qquad h,q-1-h<J,
\]
so the lcm of **all** such secondary factors divides the one shared factor in (42). We do not multiply a separate certificate for each \(m\).

### Height and nonvanishing of the certificate

All \(b_j>0\), so \(E_n(P)\) is a concrete nonzero integer. Also, for \(0\le k\le j\),
\[
 \binom jk^2\le4^j,\qquad
 \binom{j+k}{k}^2\le16^j,
\]
hence
\[
 b_j\le(j+1)64^j.
 \tag{46}
\]
Therefore
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \log E_n(P)
 &\le\log\operatorname{lcm}_{j<J}b_j\\
 &\le\sum_{j<J}\log b_j\\
 &\le\sum_{j<J}\bigl(\log(j+1)+j\log64\bigr)\\
 &=O(J^2).
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{47}
\]
Thus
\[
 \boxed{\log E_n(P)=O((n/P+1)^2).}
 \tag{48}
\]
This proves exact target preservation outside the exceptional support, nonzero certification, and sublinear aggregate height after optimizing \(P\).

### Low-prime floor and optimization

Every local gcd divides \(m\), so
\[
 C_n\mid L_n:=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n).
 \tag{49}
\]
The \(p\le P\) part has logarithm
\[
 \sum_{p\le P}\left\lfloor\log_p n\right\rfloor\log p
 =\sum_{k\ge1}\vartheta(\min(P,n^{1/k})).
 \tag{50}
\]
Using only \(\vartheta(x)=O(x)\), for \(P\ge\sqrt n\),
\[
 \log (L_n)_{p\le P}
 =O(P+\sqrt n+n^{1/3}\log n).
 \tag{51}
\]
From (44),
\[
 \left|\log C_n^{>P}-\log D_n(P)\right|
 \le\log E_n(P).
 \tag{52}
\]
Hence
\[
 \boxed{
 \log C_n=\log D_n(P)
 +O\!\left(P+\sqrt n+n^{1/3}\log n+(n/P)^2\right).}
 \tag{53}
\]
Balancing \(P\) with \((n/P)^2\) gives \(P=n^{2/3}\), and therefore
\[
 \boxed{
 \log C_n=\log D_n(n^{2/3})+O(n^{2/3}).}
 \tag{54}
\]
The structural content is local and stronger than simply restating a known carrier equivalence: (33) explains why composite multiples split into exactly two components; (35) identifies the contaminating component; (42) gives one explicit arithmetic saturation for every \(m\); and (47) proves its aggregate height is sublinear.

A narrower exceptional factor can be formed from the lcm of only the actual pairwise gcds \(\gcd(b_h,b_{q-1-h})\). That lcm still divides \(\operatorname{lcm}_{j<J}b_j\), so (47) remains valid. The uniform factor (42) is simpler.

## 8. First divided quotient: exact formula and target counterexample

For prime \(p\), \(0\le r\le p-1\), put \(s=p-1-r\). Prime reflection makes
\[
 Q_{p,r}:=\frac{b_{p-1-r}-b_r}{p}\in\mathbb Z.
 \tag{55}
\]
For \(k<p\), \(u_k(x)\) has \(p\)-integral coefficients. Taylor expansion in \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}\) gives
\[
 u_k(-1-r+p)\equiv u_k(-1-r)+pu_k'(-1-r)\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Differentiating (5),
\[
 u_k'(-1-x)=-u_k'(x).
\]
Therefore
\[
 u_k(p-1-r)^2-u_k(r)^2
 \equiv-2p\,u_k(r)u_k'(r)\pmod{p^2},
\]
and summing to \(k=p-1\) yields
\[
 \boxed{
 Q_{p,r}\equiv-2\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}u_k(r)u_k'(r)\pmod p.}
 \tag{56}
\]
Terms \(k>r\) vanish because \(u_k(r)=0\).

This quotient is **not** a target-selective second congruence. Take
\[
 p=73,\qquad n=75,\qquad m=73,\qquad r=2,\qquad s=70.
\]
Since
\[
 b_2=73,
\]
we have \(73\mid b_2\), and prime reflection gives \(73\mid b_{70}\). Thus
\[
 \gcd(73,b_2,b_{70})=73,
\]
so this is an actual carrier target.

At \(r=2\),
\[
 u_1(2)=6,\quad u_1'(2)=5,
\]
\[
 u_2(2)=6,\quad u_2'(2)=\frac{25}{2},
\]
and all \(k>2\) contribute zero. Therefore
\[
 Q_{73,2}
 \equiv-2\left(6\cdot5+6\cdot\frac{25}{2}\right)
 =-210\equiv9\pmod{73}.
 \tag{57}
\]
Hence
\[
 \boxed{
 73\mid b_2,b_{70}
 \quad\text{but}\quad
 73\nmid\frac{b_{70}-b_2}{73}.}
 \tag{58}
\]
So requiring the first divided quotient to vanish would delete a genuine target prime. The reason is conceptual: common vanishing of the endpoints is a mod-\(p\) condition, while dividing their difference by \(p\) asks for mod-\(p^2\) data that the target does not imply.

## 9. Exact negative scope

The preceding formulas rigorously rule out the following classes, and no more.

1. **Scalar p-unit composite reflection.** There is no universal integer scalar \(N(m,r)\) that remains a \(p\)-unit for every relevant \(p\mid m\) and forces \(m\mid N(m,r)(b_s-b_r)\). The family \(m=n=2p,r=0\) proves this for arbitrarily large primes.

2. **Generic integer-valued-polynomial normalization.** The characteristic-zero reflection gives (14), and \(L_d\) is the minimal universal finite-difference clearing in that generic class. For every composite multiple \(m=qp\), \(q\ge2\), this clearing contains \(p\).

3. **Unsaturated scalar resultant.** The exact common-zero ideal is \(I=(xy,xz)=(x)\cap(y,z)\), and its elimination to the primary coordinate is zero. The secondary component must be saturated away.

4. **First divided quotient.** Even for \(m=p\), where reflection holds exactly mod \(p\), the quotient \((b_{p-1-r}-b_r)/p\) need not vanish on a genuine target; (58) is a carrier-admissible counterexample.

These arguments do **not** rule out structured identities that retain the Lucas block coordinates. Equation (33) says exactly what state such an identity must retain.

## 10. Smallest viable interface and next arithmetic object

After the exact saturation (44), every high prime \(p>P\) outside a factor of logarithmic height \(O((n/P)^2)\) satisfies
\[
 p\mid C_n
 \iff
 \mathcal A_n(p)\ne\varnothing
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 p\mid b_{n\bmod p}.
 \tag{59}
\]
At \(P=n^{2/3}\), all discarded mass is \(O(n^{2/3})\). This is the smallest clean mod-\(p\) interface produced by the symmetric-index attack: **one low digit, no composite-multiplier state**.

If one goes one level beyond mod \(p\), the appropriate object is not the divided endpoint difference but the Lucas-lift defect
\[
 \Delta_p(a,h):=
 \frac{b_{a+ph}-b_ab_h}{p}\in\mathbb Z,
 \tag{60}
\]
whose integrality follows from Apéry--Lucas. A first-order refinement should retain the explicit primary factor \(b_a\) and compare
\[
 \Delta_p(a,h),\qquad
 \Delta_p(p-1-a,q-1-h),
 \tag{61}
\]
rather than divide \(b_s-b_r\) first. The latter destroys the primary factor and imposes an illegitimate mod-\(p^2\) condition, as (58) demonstrates.

The live interface left by this attack is therefore
\[
 \boxed{
 \text{control }p>n^{2/3}\text{ with }p\mid b_{n\bmod p},
 \text{ or build a saturated Lucas-lift identity retaining }b_a.}
 \tag{62}
\]
The recurrence/Casoratian can still be useful **inside** this primary-digit problem, for example as a nonvanishing certificate for a block-state eliminant. A scalar symmetric difference or an invertible transfer across a composite \(p\)-block should not be pursued further.

## 11. Compact audit checklist

```text
Characteristic-zero reflection:
    u_k(-1-x)=u_k(x)                                          YES
    b_s-b_r = m * rational divided difference                 YES

Universal integer clearing:
    m | L_{2 max(r,s)} (b_s-b_r)                              YES
    log L_{2 max(r,s)} may be Theta(n)                        BAD

Raw composite reflection:
    p | m => b_s=b_r (mod p)                                  FALSE
    counterexample for every p>=3: n=m=2p, r=0, s=2p-1

Exact p|m block identity:
    (b_r,b_s)=b_a(b_h,b_{q-1-h}) (mod p)                     YES

Common-zero decomposition:
    primary:   p | b_a
    secondary: p | b_h and p | b_{q-1-h}                     EXACT

Ordinary resultant in primary coordinate:
    elimination ideal = 0                                     DEGENERATE

Saturation:
    (xy,xz):(y,z)^infty=(x)                                  YES
    all high-p secondary failures supported on E_n(P)         YES
    log E_n(n^(2/3))=O(n^(2/3))                               SUBLINEAR

First divided quotient:
    Q_{p,r}=(b_{p-1-r}-b_r)/p
    Q=-2 sum u_k(r)u'_k(r) (mod p)                            EXACT

```

The strongest conclusion from this line is therefore not a full proof of \(\log C_n=o(n)\), but a rigorous localization: **the composite-multiple branch itself costs only \(e^{O(n^{2/3})}\)** after a concrete nonzero saturation, while scalar reflection/divided-difference attempts that try to do better without retaining the Lucas block state are provably non-target-preserving.
