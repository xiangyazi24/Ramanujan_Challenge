# Direct symmetric-index attack on the Apéry carrier

## Verdict

There is a useful exact symmetric-index identity, but it is **not** a scalar composite-modulus reflection. The correct mod-p state has two branches.

For a prime \(p\mid m\), write \(m=qp\), \(n=tp+a\) with \(0\le a<p\), and put
\[
 h=t-q,\qquad \ell=q-1-h=2q-t-1.
\]
For every admissible top-half \(m\) one has \(0\le h,\ell\le q-1\), and the exact Apéry--Lucas/reflection calculation gives
\[
 \boxed{(b_r,b_s)\equiv b_a\,(b_h,b_\ell)\pmod p,}
 \qquad r=n-m,\quad s=m-1-r.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \boxed{p\mid b_r,\ p\mid b_s
 \iff
 \bigl(p\mid b_a\bigr)
 \ \text{or}\ 
 \bigl(p\mid b_h\ \text{and}\ p\mid b_\ell\bigr).}
 \tag{1}
\]
The first alternative is the desired one-digit target; the second is the precise composite-multiple contamination.

This has a genuinely useful global consequence. For \(P>\sqrt n\), every secondary-branch prime \(p>P\) divides an Apéry number with index \(<n/P\). Hence **all** secondary-branch primes occurring in the lcm over all \(m\) are supported on the single nonzero arithmetic factor
\[
 E_n(P):=\operatorname{rad}\!\left(\operatorname{lcm}_{0\le j<J} b_j\right),
 \qquad J=\left\lceil\frac nP\right\rceil,
 \tag{2}
\]
and
\[
 \log E_n(P)=O(J^2)=O((n/P)^2).
 \tag{3}
\]
After removing this factor, the high-prime part of \(C_n\) is **exactly** the primary one-digit carrier. Combining (3) with the low-prime lcm bound \(O(P+\sqrt n+n^{1/3}\log n)\) and choosing \(P=n^{2/3}\) gives an \(O(n^{2/3})=o(n)\) total loss. Thus composite multiples of a high prime can be saturated away at sublinear height; they are not an additional all-index obstruction.

What fails is equally sharp:

* \(b_{m-1-r}-b_r\) is not generally divisible by \(m\), or even by a chosen prime \(p\mid m\). In fact there is an infinite counterexample family for arbitrarily large \(p\).
* A characteristic-zero reflection exists termwise, but its natural universal integer-valued-polynomial clearing contains the target \(p\) for every genuinely composite witness \(m=qp\), \(q\ge2\).
* The first divided quotient after prime reflection is not a consequence of the target condition. There is an admissible target example at \(p=73\) where both endpoint Apéry numbers vanish mod \(73\), but the divided quotient is \(9\pmod{73}\).
* The ordinary resultant/elimination ideal is forced to be zero because the two endpoint equations have the common primary factor \(b_a\) and a secondary component. Saturation is necessary, and (2) is an arithmetic saturation certificate of sublinear aggregate height.

So the smallest viable interface left by this attack is the **primary digit condition**
\[
 p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p},
 \tag{4}
\]
with the top-half admissibility of a multiple \(qp\). A further attack must retain the Lucas block state (or a first Lucas-lift defect); a scalar normalized endpoint difference cannot do it.

## 2. Exact characteristic-zero reflection and its denominators

Set
\[
 u_k(x):=\binom{x}{k}\binom{x+k}{k}
 =\frac{F_k(x)}{(k!)^2},
 \qquad
 F_k(x):=\prod_{j=-k+1}^{k}(x+j).
 \tag{8}
\]
Then \(u_k\in\mathbb Q[x]\) is integer-valued on every integer, has degree \(2k\), and
\[
 F_k(-1-x)=F_k(x),\qquad
 u_k(-1-x)=u_k(x).
 \tag{9}
\]
For \(T\ge0\), define the finite reflection polynomial
\[
 B_T(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{T}u_k(x)^2.
 \tag{10}
\]
It satisfies the **characteristic-zero identity**
\[
 B_T(-1-x)=B_T(x).
 \tag{11}
\]
If \(r,s\ge0\) and \(T=\max(r,s)\), then the terms with \(k>r\) or \(k>s\) vanish at the corresponding nonnegative integer endpoint, so
\[
 B_T(r)=b_r,\qquad B_T(s)=b_s.
 \tag{12}
\]
Now put \(s=m-1-r=(-1-r)+m\). Equation (11) gives the exact divided-difference representation
\[
 b_s-b_r
 =B_T((-1-r)+m)-B_T(-1-r).
 \tag{13}
\]
More explicitly, since
\[
 H_k(m,r):=
 \frac{F_k(m-1-r)-F_k(-1-r)}{m}
 \in\mathbb Z[m,r],
 \tag{14}
\]
we have
\[
 \boxed{
 b_s-b_r
 =m\sum_{k=0}^{T}
 \frac{H_k(m,r)\bigl(u_k(s)+u_k(r)\bigr)}{(k!)^2}.}
 \tag{15}
\]
This is an exact characteristic-zero reflection formula. It also exhibits the denominator obstruction: the most naive clearing gives
\[
 m\mid (T!)^2(b_s-b_r).
 \tag{16}
\]
There is a substantially better universal clearing, but it is still fatal for target preservation in the composite case.

### 2.1 Optimal universal clearing for integer-valued finite differences

Let
\[
 L_d=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,d),\qquad L_0=1.
\]

**Lemma.** If \(f\in\mathbb Q[x]\) is integer-valued on \(\mathbb Z\) and \(\deg f\le d\), then for all integers \(x\) and all positive integers \(m\),
\[
 \boxed{m\mid L_d\bigl(f(x+m)-f(x)\bigr).}
 \tag{17}
\]
Moreover \(L_d\) is the minimal positive universal multiplier valid for **all** integer-valued polynomials of degree at most \(d\).

**Proof.** Every integer-valued polynomial of degree at most \(d\) has a Newton expansion
\[
 f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_j\binom{x}{j},\qquad c_j\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Vandermonde gives
\[
 \binom{x+m}{j}-\binom{x}{j}
 =\sum_{i=1}^{j}\binom{x}{j-i}\binom{m}{i},
\]
so
\[
 \frac{L_d}{m}
 \left(\binom{x+m}{j}-\binom{x}{j}\right)
 =\sum_{i=1}^{j}
 \binom{x}{j-i}\frac{L_d}{i}\binom{m-1}{i-1}\in\mathbb Z.
\]
This proves (17). Conversely, if a universal multiplier \(M\) works, take
\(f(x)=\binom{x}{j}\), \(x=0\), \(m=j\). The finite difference is \(1\), so \(j\mid M\) for every \(j\le d\); hence \(L_d\mid M\).  \(\square\)

Apply this lemma to \(u_k\), at \(x=-1-r\). By (9),
\[
 m\mid L_{2k}\bigl(u_k(s)-u_k(r)\bigr).
\]
Multiplying by the integer \(u_k(s)+u_k(r)\), nesting \(L_{2k}\mid L_{2T}\), and summing gives
\[
 \boxed{m\mid L_{2T}(b_s-b_r),\qquad T=\max(r,s).}
 \tag{18}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 \boxed{
 \frac{m}{\gcd(m,L_{2T})}\mid b_s-b_r.}
 \tag{19}
\]
This is a genuine composite-modulus reflection congruence, but it is exactly too weak at the primes relevant to the carrier.

If \(m=qp\) with \(q\ge2\), then \(r+s=m-1\), so
\[
 T\ge\left\lceil\frac{m-1}{2}\right\rceil\ge p.
\]
Therefore \(p\mid L_{2T}\). The universal clearing in (18) has swallowed the target prime. Since
\[
 \log L_{2T}=\psi(2T)=\Theta(T),
 \tag{20}
\]
it also has linear logarithmic height when \(T=\Theta(n)\). Thus the generic integer-valued-polynomial reflection route cannot produce the needed sublinear global certificate.

The minimality statement above should be read precisely: it rules out improving (18) **using only the generic class of integer-valued polynomials of bounded degree**. It does not rule out special cancellation among the Apéry summands. The next subsection shows, however, that no special cancellation can turn the raw difference into a universal \(p\)-unit scalar reflection.

## 3. Raw composite reflection is false for arbitrarily large target primes

There is an infinite exact counterexample family. Let \(p\ge3\) be prime and take
\[
 n=m=2p,\qquad r=0,\qquad s=2p-1.
\]
This is an admissible top-half pair. By Apéry--Lucas and prime reflection,
\[
 b_{2p-1}\equiv b_1b_{p-1}\equiv 5b_0\equiv5\pmod p.
\]
Hence
\[
 \boxed{b_{2p-1}-b_0\equiv4\pmod p.}
 \tag{21}
\]
So \(p\mid m\) but \(p\nmid b_s-b_r\), for every prime \(p\ge3\).

In particular, suppose a scalar normalization \(N(m,r)\in\mathbb Z\) is intended to force
\[
 m\mid N(m,r)(b_s-b_r)
 \tag{22}
\]
for all top-half reflected pairs while remaining a \(p\)-unit whenever \(p\mid m\), so that reduction modulo \(p\) preserves the target. Taking \(m=2p,r=0\) in (21) forces
\[
 p\mid N(2p,0)
\]
for every \(p\ge3\), contradicting the required \(p\)-unit property. Thus:

\[
 \boxed{\text{There is no universal target-preserving scalar normalization of }b_s-b_r.}
 \tag{23}
\]

For the first numerical member, \(p=3\), \(m=n=6\):

```text
b_0 = 1
b_5 = 819005
b_5 - b_0 = 819004 = 4 (mod 6) = 1 (mod 3).
```

A second small top-half example is \(n=7,m=6,r=1,s=4\), where
\(b_4-b_1=32996\equiv2\pmod6\).

## 4. Recurrence reflection, Casoratian, and why the transfer state must break at a p-block

The middle recurrence coefficient factors as
\[
 P(j)=(2j+1)(17j^2+17j+5),
\]
and obeys
\[
 P(-j-1)=-P(j).
 \tag{24}
\]
Consequently, if \(y_j\) solves (5) on a range where the recurrence is interpreted, then \(z_j=y_{-j-1}\) also solves it. The bilateral extension
\[
 B_{-1-j}=b_j\qquad(j\ge0)
 \tag{25}
\]
is therefore a reflected solution. The important point is that the equations at \(j=-1\) and \(j=0\) are singular: they impose consistency inside the two halves but do not identify the free scale on one half with the other. Thus characteristic-zero reflection across \(-1/2\) does not create translation-periodicity by \(m\).

The one-step transfer matrix is
\[
 v_{j+1}=T_jv_j,
 \qquad
 v_j=\binom{y_j}{y_{j-1}},
 \qquad
 T_j=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 P(j)/(j+1)^3&-j^3/(j+1)^3\\
 1&0
 \end{pmatrix},
 \tag{26}
\]
with
\[
 \det T_j=\left(\frac{j}{j+1}\right)^3.
 \tag{27}
\]
The integral-cleared form is
\[
 \widetilde T_j=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 P(j)&-j^3\\
 (j+1)^3&0
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \widetilde T_jv_j=(j+1)^3v_{j+1},
 \tag{28}
\]
and
\[
 \det\widetilde T_j=j^3(j+1)^3.
 \tag{29}
\]
Modulo \(p\), (28) is singular at \(j\equiv0,-1\pmod p\). In particular, at \(j=p-1\) the recurrence gives a constraint on the previous block but does not determine \(y_p\); the next block starts with a new state. This is exactly the block-state freedom that appears in the Lucas factorization below. Any approach that insists on an invertible two-state transfer across the boundary must introduce a factor of \(p\).

The companion solution currently used in `proof.tex` makes the same issue visible from the determinant side. With \(a_0=0,a_1=6\),
\[
 \boxed{a_jb_{j-1}-a_{j-1}b_j=\frac6{j^3}.}
 \tag{30}
\]
For example \(a_2=351/4\). The known universal integral clearing is
\[
 d_j a_j\in\mathbb Z,
 \qquad d_j=L_j^3,
 \tag{31}
\]
whose logarithmic height is \(3\psi(j)=\Theta(j)\). Thus the Casoratian is an excellent nonzero **adjacent** certificate, but naively transporting the rational companion to an endpoint of size \(\Theta(n)\) and clearing all denominators has linear height. More importantly, (29) shows why reducing such a transfer through a \(p\)-block cannot be done by a \(p\)-unit integral matrix.

For \(1\le r<s\), an exact transfer can also be written from the fundamental matrix
\[
 F_j=\begin{pmatrix}a_j&b_j\\a_{j-1}&b_{j-1}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \det F_j=\frac6{j^3},
\]
as \(F_sF_r^{-1}\). This is a valid characteristic-zero identity, but its entries contain the rational companion values. It does not remove the mod-\(p\) block component described next.

## 5. The exact symmetric-index identity modulo every prime divisor of m

This is the key local statement.

Let \(p\mid m\), write
\[
 m=qp,\qquad n=tp+a,\qquad 0\le a<p.
 \tag{32}
\]
Because \(m\le n<2m\),
\[
 q\le t\le2q-1.
\]
Set
\[
 h=t-q,\qquad \ell=2q-t-1=q-1-h.
 \tag{33}
\]
Then
\[
 0\le h,\ell\le q-1,
 \tag{34}
\]
and the two reflected indices have the base-\(p\) forms
\[
 r=n-m=hp+a,
 \tag{35}
\]
\[
 s=m-1-r=2m-n-1=\ell p+(p-1-a).
 \tag{36}
\]
The one-step form of Apéry--Lucas gives
\[
 b_r\equiv b_a b_h\pmod p,
 \qquad
 b_s\equiv b_{p-1-a}b_\ell\pmod p.
 \tag{37}
\]
It remains only to note the prime reflection
\[
 b_{p-1-a}\equiv b_a\pmod p.
 \tag{38}
\]
For completeness, (38) follows directly from (8)--(9): for \(k<p\), \((k!)^2\) is a \(p\)-unit and
\[
 u_k(p-1-a)\equiv u_k(-1-a)=u_k(a)\pmod p.
\]
Extend both finite Apéry sums to \(0\le k<p\); all terms beyond the ordinary endpoint vanish exactly. Squaring and summing proves (38).

Combining (37)--(38),
\[
 \boxed{
 (b_r,b_s)\equiv
 b_a\bigl(b_h,b_{q-1-h}\bigr)\pmod p.}
 \tag{39}
\]
In particular, the defect of scalar reflection is exactly
\[
 \boxed{
 b_s-b_r\equiv
 b_a\bigl(b_{q-1-h}-b_h\bigr)\pmod p.}
 \tag{40}
\]
For \(q=1\), (34) forces \(h=\ell=0\), and (40) recovers the usual prime reflection. For \(q\ge2\), the lower block need not be symmetric at equal values, and (21) is the case \(q=2,h=0,\ell=1,a=0\).

Most importantly, (39) gives the common-zero classification
\[
 \boxed{
 p\mid b_r,\ p\mid b_s
 \iff
 \bigl[p\mid b_a\bigr]
 \ \text{or}\ 
 \bigl[p\mid b_h\ \&\ p\mid b_{q-1-h}\bigr].}
 \tag{41}
\]
This is exact, not heuristic.

Equivalently, for a fixed \(m\) one can write the squarefree part of the local gcd prime by prime as
\[
 \operatorname{rad}\gcd(m,b_r,b_s)
 =\prod_{p\mid m\atop
 \left(p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\right)\ \text{or}\
 \left(p\mid b_{h_p},\ p\mid b_{\ell_p}\right)}p,
 \tag{42}
\]
where
\[
 h_p=\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor-\frac mp,
 \qquad
 \ell_p=\frac mp-1-h_p.
\]
For the high primes \(p>\sqrt n\) relevant below, \(p^2>m\), so there is no hidden \(p\)-power in \(m\): (42) already describes the entire \(p\)-part of the local carrier.

## 6. Resultant/elimination interpretation: ordinary elimination must vanish; saturation is the right operation

Fix \(p\mid m\) and abbreviate in \(\mathbb F_p\)
\[
 x=b_a,\qquad y=b_h,\qquad z=b_\ell.
\]
The two endpoint equations are
\[
 xy=0,\qquad xz=0.
\]
Their ideal is
\[
 I=(xy,xz)=x(y,z)=(x)\cap(y,z).
 \tag{43}
\]
This decomposition is the algebraic form of (41):

* primary component: \(x=0\);
* secondary component: \(y=z=0\).

An ordinary scalar resultant/eliminant that tries to retain only \(x\) cannot work:
\[
 I\cap\mathbb F_p[x]=(0).
 \tag{44}
\]
Indeed, the secondary component \(y=z=0\) projects onto every value of \(x\). Thus any nonzero polynomial consequence in \(x\) alone would incorrectly delete genuine solutions of the two endpoint equations.

The correct algebraic operation is saturation by the secondary component:
\[
 \boxed{I:(y,z)^\infty=(x).}
 \tag{45}
\]
After proving that \((y,z)\ne(0,0)\), the common-zero condition really does reduce to \(x=0\). The next section constructs a single **integer** exceptional factor whose prime support contains every failure of this saturation for all high-prime multiples \(m\), and whose logarithmic height is \(o(n)\).

This also precisely delimits what a useful resultant would have to do: it must be a *saturated* or block-state resultant. A raw resultant of the two endpoint equations is structurally degenerate.

## 7. Global saturation theorem for C_n

Let
\[
 \mathcal A_n(p):=
 \left\{q\ge1:
 \frac n2<qp\le n,\quad 3qp\ne2n+1
 \right\}.
 \tag{46}
\]
The last condition is exactly the omission \(r\ne s\), since
\[
 r=s\iff 2n+1=3m.
 \tag{47}
\]
Fix a threshold
\[
 \sqrt n<P<n
\]
and put
\[
 J=\left\lceil\frac nP\right\rceil,
 \qquad
 E_n(P)=\operatorname{rad}\!\left(
 \operatorname{lcm}_{0\le j<J}b_j
 \right).
 \tag{48}
\]
This certificate is nonzero because every \(b_j>0\).

Define the primary high-prime carrier
\[
 D_n(P):=
 \prod_{\substack{P<p\le n\\ p\ {m prime}\\
 \mathcal A_n(p)\ne\varnothing\\
 p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}p.
 \tag{49}
\]
Let \(C_n^{>P}\) denote the part of \(C_n\) supported on primes \(p>P\). Since \(P>\sqrt n\), every such prime occurs in every \(m\le n\) with exponent at most one; hence \(C_n^{>P}\) is squarefree.

### Theorem (exact target preservation outside one small exceptional factor)

\[
 \boxed{
 \frac{C_n^{>P}}{\gcd(C_n^{>P},E_n(P))}
 =
 \frac{D_n(P)}{\gcd(D_n(P),E_n(P))}.}
 \tag{50}
\]
Equivalently, for every prime \(p>P\) with \(p\nmid E_n(P)\),
\[
 \boxed{p\mid C_n\iff p\mid D_n(P).}
 \tag{51}
\]

**Proof.** Suppose first that \(p>P\), \(p\nmid E_n(P)\), and \(p\mid C_n\). There is an admissible \(m=qp\) contributing this \(p\). Because \(m\le n\),
\[
 q\le\frac np<\frac nP\le J.
\]
Since \(q\) is integral and the first inequality is strict at \(p>P\), in fact \(q\le J-1\). By (34),
\[
 0\le h,\ell\le q-1<J.
\]
The assumption \(p\nmid E_n(P)\) therefore implies
\[
 p\nmid b_h,\qquad p\nmid b_\ell.
\]
The secondary branch in (41) is impossible, so \(p\mid b_a\), where \(a=n\bmod p\). The same \(q\) shows \(\mathcal A_n(p)\ne\varnothing\), hence \(p\mid D_n(P)\).

Conversely, if \(p\mid D_n(P)\), choose \(q\in\mathcal A_n(p)\) and put \(m=qp\). Since \(p\mid b_a\), (39) gives
\[
 p\mid b_r,\qquad p\mid b_s.
\]
Thus \(p\mid\gcd(m,b_r,b_s)\), hence \(p\mid C_n\). This converse does not even require \(p\nmid E_n(P)\). The primewise equivalence outside \(E_n(P)\) proves (50). \(\square\)

A more local way to state the same fact is that, for a fixed admissible \(m\), every high prime in
\(\gcd(m,b_r,b_s)\) is either a primary prime \(p\mid b_{n\bmod p}\), or divides the small-index common factor
\(\gcd(b_h,b_{q-1-h})\). Taking the lcm over all \(m\) does **not** require multiplying a new certificate for each \(m\): every secondary factor divides the one shared lcm in (48).

### 7.1 Height of the saturation certificate

A crude but sufficient Apéry bound is
\[
 \binom{j}{k}^2\le4^j,
 \qquad
 \binom{j+k}{k}^2\le16^j
 \quad(0\le k\le j),
\]
so
\[
 b_j\le(j+1)64^j.
 \tag{52}
\]
Therefore
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \log E_n(P)
 &\le\log\operatorname{lcm}_{j<J}b_j\\
 &\le\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\log b_j\\
 &\le\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\bigl(\log(j+1)+j\log64\bigr)\\
 &=O(J^2).
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{53}
\]
Thus
\[
 \boxed{\log E_n(P)=O((n/P+1)^2).}
 \tag{54}
\]
This proves all three properties needed from the exceptional certificate:

1. **target preservation:** (50)--(51) are exact outside its prime support;
2. **nonzero certificate:** \(E_n(P)\ge1\) is a concrete positive integer;
3. **sublinear aggregate height:** at the optimized threshold below, \(\log E_n(P)=O(n^{2/3})=o(n)\).

### 7.2 The low-prime floor and the optimized n^(2/3) threshold

Every local term \(\gcd(m,b_r,b_s)\) divides \(m\), so
\[
 C_n\mid L_n:=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n).
 \tag{55}
\]
The contribution to \(\log L_n\) from primes \(p\le P\) is
\[
 \sum_{p\le P}\left\lfloor\log_p n\right\rfloor\log p
 =\sum_{k\ge1}\vartheta\bigl(\min(P,n^{1/k})\bigr).
 \tag{56}
\]
Using only the classical upper bound \(\vartheta(x)=O(x)\), for \(P\ge\sqrt n\),
\[
 \log (L_n)_{p\le P}
 =O\!\left(P+\sqrt n+n^{1/3}\log n\right).
 \tag{57}
\]
From (50), since both high-prime carriers are squarefree,
\[
 \left|\log C_n^{>P}-\log D_n(P)\right|
 \le\log E_n(P).
 \tag{58}
\]
Combining (54), (57), and (58),
\[
 \boxed{
 \log C_n
 =\log D_n(P)
 +O\!\left(
 P+\sqrt n+n^{1/3}\log n+(n/P)^2
 \right).}
 \tag{59}
\]
The two leading structural costs are \(P\) and \((n/P)^2\). Balancing them gives
\[
 P=n^{2/3}.
\]
Hence
\[
 \boxed{
 \log C_n
 =\log D_n(n^{2/3})+O(n^{2/3}).}
 \tag{60}
\]
This is not merely another statement that \(C_n\) is equivalent to the original conjectural carrier. Equation (39) identifies **why** composite multiples do not add a new high-prime obstruction, (41) identifies the only contaminating component, and (48)--(54) give an explicit arithmetic saturation whose aggregate logarithmic height is sublinear.

One can make the exceptional factor narrower by taking the lcm only of the actual lower-pair gcds
\[
 \gcd(b_h,b_{q-1-h})
\]
that occur in (41). Its lcm still divides \(\operatorname{lcm}_{j<J}b_j\), so the same \(O(J^2)\) height bound holds. The uniform \(E_n(P)\) in (48) is simpler and already optimal at the exponent level for this argument.

## 8. The first divided quotient is not a target-selective second congruence

For the true prime reflection, let \(0\le r\le p-1\) and \(s=p-1-r\). Since \(p\mid b_s-b_r\), define
\[
 Q_{p,r}:=\frac{b_{p-1-r}-b_r}{p}\in\mathbb Z.
 \tag{61}
\]
There is an exact first-order formula modulo \(p\). For every \(k<p\), \(u_k(x)\) has \(p\)-integral coefficients. Taylor expansion in \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}\) gives
\[
 u_k(-1-r+p)
 \equiv u_k(-1-r)+p u_k'(-1-r)\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Differentiating \(u_k(-1-x)=u_k(x)\) yields
\[
 u_k'(-1-x)=-u_k'(x).
\]
Therefore
\[
 u_k(p-1-r)^2-u_k(r)^2
 \equiv-2p\,u_k(r)u_k'(r)\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Extending the finite sums to \(k<p\) gives
\[
 \boxed{
 Q_{p,r}\equiv
 -2\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}u_k(r)u_k'(r)\pmod p.}
 \tag{62}
\]
Terms \(k>r\) vanish because \(u_k(r)=0\).

Crucially, \(p\mid b_r\) does **not** force the right-hand side of (62) to vanish. There is an exact admissible large-prime counterexample:
\[
 p=73,\qquad n=75,\qquad m=73,\qquad r=2,\qquad s=70.
\]
Here
\[
 b_2=73,
\]
so \(73\mid b_2\), and prime reflection gives \(73\mid b_{70}\). Hence the local carrier really contains \(73\):
\[
 \gcd(73,b_2,b_{70})=73.
\]
But (62) gives a nonzero first quotient. At \(r=2\),
\[
 u_1(2)=6,\quad u_1'(2)=5,
\]
\[
 u_2(2)=6,\quad u_2'(2)=\frac{25}{2},
\]
and all \(k>2\) contribute zero. Thus
\[
 Q_{73,2}
 \equiv-2\left(6\cdot5+6\cdot\frac{25}{2}\right)
 =-210
 \equiv9\pmod{73}.
 \tag{63}
\]
So
\[
 \boxed{
 73\mid b_2,b_{70}
 \quad\text{but}\quad
 73\nmid\frac{b_{70}-b_2}{73}.}
 \tag{64}
\]
This directly refutes the proposal that the first divided quotient supplies a second congruence that every target prime must satisfy. Requiring \(Q_{p,r}\equiv0\pmod p\) would delete a genuine carrier prime.

The same obstruction is conceptual: the condition \(b_r\equiv b_s\equiv0\pmod p\) is a **first-order mod-\(p\)** condition. Dividing their difference by \(p\) asks for mod-\(p^2\) information that is not implied by the target.

## 9. Precise negative scope

The computations above rigorously rule out the following class of strategies.

### 9.1 Scalar p-unit composite reflection

There is no universal integer scalar \(N(m,r)\) which is a \(p\)-unit for every relevant \(p\mid m\) and makes
\[
 m\mid N(m,r)(b_{m-1-r}-b_r)
\]
for all admissible pairs. The family \(m=n=2p,r=0\) proves this for arbitrarily large \(p\).

### 9.2 Generic integer-valued-polynomial normalization

The exact characteristic-zero reflection of the summands yields (18). Within the generic class of integer-valued polynomial finite differences, \(L_d\) is the minimal universal clearing. For every composite multiple \(m=qp\), \(q\ge2\), the relevant \(L_{2T}\) contains \(p\). Hence this class cannot preserve the high-prime target.

### 9.3 Unsaturated scalar resultant

The exact mod-\(p\) common-zero ideal is \(I=(xy,xz)=(x)\cap(y,z)\), and its elimination to the primary coordinate is zero. A nonzero scalar resultant cannot remove the secondary component without saturation.

### 9.4 First divided quotient

Even for \(m=p\), where ordinary reflection is exact mod \(p\), the quotient \((b_{p-1-r}-b_r)/p\) need not vanish on a genuine target; (64) is an explicit carrier-admissible counterexample.

These statements do **not** rule out a more structured identity that retains the Lucas block coordinates. In fact (39) shows exactly what such an identity must retain.

## 10. Smallest viable interface left by the attack

After the exact saturation (50), every high prime \(p>P\) outside a factor of logarithmic height \(O((n/P)^2)\) satisfies
\[
 p\mid C_n
 \iff
 \mathcal A_n(p)\ne\varnothing
 \ \text{and}\ 
 p\mid b_{n\bmod p}.
 \tag{65}
\]
At \(P=n^{2/3}\), everything discarded in reaching (65) has total logarithmic mass \(O(n^{2/3})\). This is the smallest clean mod-\(p\) interface produced by the symmetric-index attack: **one low digit, no composite multiplier state**.

If one wants to go beyond mod \(p\), the appropriate next object is not the divided endpoint difference but the Lucas-lift defect
\[
 \Delta_p(a,h):=
 \frac{b_{a+ph}-b_ab_h}{p}\in\mathbb Z,
 \tag{66}
\]
whose integrality is exactly (7). A first-order refinement should compare the two block defects
\[
 \Delta_p(a,h),\qquad
 \Delta_p(p-1-a,q-1-h),
 \tag{67}
\]
while keeping the explicit primary factor \(b_a\). Dividing the endpoint difference first destroys that factor and, as (64) shows, imposes an illegitimate mod-\(p^2\) condition on genuine targets.

Thus the recommended next arithmetic interface is:

\[
 \boxed{
 \text{control the primes }p>n^{2/3}
 \text{ with }p\mid b_{n\bmod p},
 \text{ or derive a saturated Lucas-lift identity retaining }b_a.}
 \tag{68}
\]

The recurrence/Casoratian route can still be useful **inside this primary digit problem** (for example to certify nonvanishing of an eliminant in the digit variable), but a transfer across a composite \(p\)-block or a scalar symmetric difference should not be pursued further.

    (b_r,b_s) = b_a (b_h,b_{q-1-h}) (mod p)                  [YES]

    primary:   p | b_a

Ordinary resultant in primary coordinate:
    elimination ideal = 0                                     [DEGENERATE]

Saturation:
    (xy,xz):(y,z)^infty = (x)                                [YES]
