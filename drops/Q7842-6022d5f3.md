# Verdict

**No: neither Janson, the Lovász Local Lemma, nor a standard Stein–Chen dependency-graph argument can prove \((PP3)\) or \((HM)_3\) from \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\), multiplicative Sidonicity, and reflection.** There are three separate obstructions.

1. The proposed graph—joining \(p,q\) when they have at least one common hit—is **not a probabilistic dependency graph**. A missing edge usually means that the two events are disjoint, hence negatively correlated, not independent.
2. At \(X=512\), even that overlap graph is almost complete on the active primes:
   
   
   \[
   \boxed{\Delta_{\mathrm{overlap}}(512)=29.}
   \]
   
   There are \(75\) window primes, but only \(30\) active primes. On those \(30\) vertices, \(\Delta=29=30-1\). The genuine exact-dependence graph is the complete graph \(K_{30}\).
3. More fundamentally, the repository's anchored-rainbow-star construction already satisfies \(Z(p)=2\), reflection, all the listed spacing conditions, and—because every active set has two points—multiplicative Sidonicity. Yet it violates \((HM)_3\) by a factor \(X^{1-o(1)}\). Thus the proposed local hypotheses are logically insufficient, independently of any proof technology.

The numerical sub-Poisson behavior is real and valuable, but it is evidence for an additional **cross-prime link-discrepancy theorem**. It is not a consequence of the currently known primewise combinatorics.

# 2. The proposed graph is not a dependency graph

Let \(U\) be uniform on \(I\), and write

\[
I_p=1_{A_p}(U),\qquad
\alpha_p=|A_p|,\qquad
J_{p,q}=|A_p\cap A_q|.
\]

Exact pairwise independence is equivalent to

\[
\boxed{M J_{p,q}=\alpha_p\alpha_q.}
\tag{2.1}
\]

The audit checked (2.1) as an integer identity. It fails for all \(435\) pairs of active primes. Hence every ordinary dependency graph must contain every active pair: its active part is \(K_{30}\).

The user's overlap rule is even more visibly invalid as a dependency rule. If \(J_{p,q}=0\) while both primes are active, then

\[
\Pr(I_p=I_q=1)=0
\quad\text{but}\quad
\Pr(I_p=1)\Pr(I_q=1)>0.
\]

So a missing overlap edge means dependence, not independence.

Nor does this automatically define a lopsided-LLL graph. For disjoint positive-probability events,

\[
\Pr(A_p\mid \overline{A_q})
=\frac{\Pr(A_p)}{1-\Pr(A_q)}
>\Pr(A_p),
\]

which has the wrong sign for the usual negative-dependency condition used to omit \(q\) from \(p\)'s lopsided neighborhood.

The source of the issue is structural: all \(I_p\) are functions of the same random integer \(U\). They are not functions of disjoint blocks of independent trials. CRT would make residues independent under a uniform measure on a full product period, but the sampling interval has length \(X^2\), while products of three window primes have size \(\asymp X^3\).

# 3. Even a valid bound \(\Delta=o(\#\mathcal P_X)\) would not imply HM3

At every integer \(m\), the set of primes hitting \(m\) forms a clique in the overlap graph. Therefore

\[
K_X(m)\le \Delta+1.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Using the proved second factorial moment,

\[
\sum_{m<M}(K_X(m))_2\le5M\lambda_X^2,
\]

one gets only

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{m<M}(K_X(m))_3
&=\sum_m(K_X(m))_2(K_X(m)-2)\\
&\le (\Delta+1)\sum_m(K_X(m))_2\\
&\le 5(\Delta+1)M\lambda_X^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.2}
\]

To recover

\[
\sum_m(K_X(m))_3
\ll M\lambda_X^3X^{o(1)},
\]

(3.2) would require essentially

\[
\boxed{\Delta+1\ll\lambda_XX^{o(1)},}
\tag{3.3}
\]

not merely

\[
\Delta=o(\#\mathcal P_X).
\]

Condition (3.3) is already an atom-scale statement. When \(\lambda_X\) is small, it is dramatically stronger than sublinear graph degree.

There is also a general probabilistic warning: convergence in distribution, or even total-variation convergence, to a Poisson law does **not** by itself imply convergence of third factorial moments. A mass of probability \(X^{-2}\) at a value \(K\asymp X/\log X\) is invisible in total variation but contributes

\[
X^{-2}\left(\frac X{\log X}\right)^3
\asymp\frac X{(\log X)^3}
\]

to the normalized third moment. This is exactly the rare-rainbow-star phenomenon. Uniform integrability/tail control is the missing ingredient.

# 4. Why Janson does not close the problem

Classical Janson inequalities require a product probability space or a valid dependency/dissociation structure. Neither is supplied here by the graph \(J_{p,q}>0\).

Even abstracting from that defect, a maximum-degree condition is not enough. A local-dependence Poisson theorem uses weighted quantities of the form

\[
 b_1=\sum_p\sum_{q\in B_p}\Pr(A_p)\Pr(A_q),
 \qquad
 b_2=\sum_p\sum_{\substack{q\in B_p\\q\ne p}}\Pr(A_p\cap A_q),
\]

plus a conditional-dependence term when the neighborhoods are not genuine dependency neighborhoods. From \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\) alone,

\[
\Pr(A_p)\ll X^{-1/3}.
\]

Thus even the easy part would naturally require a scale such as

\[
\Delta X^{-1/3}=o(1),
\]

i.e. \(\Delta=o(X^{1/3})\), not merely

\[
\Delta=o(X/\log X).
\]

The intersection term \(b_2\) is not controlled by the cardinality bound at the relative precision needed for \((HM)_3\). Controlling its pair-conditioned extension to a third prime is precisely the Palm problem.

At \(X=512\), the true active dependency degree is \(29\), while \(X^{1/3}=8\). This is a finite-scale comparison rather than an asymptotic disproof, but it shows that the hoped-for sparse local-dependence regime is not what the actual graph displays.

# 5. Why the Lovász Local Lemma does not close the problem

The LLL is designed to show that

\[
\Pr\Bigl(\bigcap_p\overline{A_p}\Bigr)>0
\]

under small-event/sparse-dependency hypotheses. It is not a factorial-moment estimate.

Using only \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\), a symmetric-LLL expression has worst-case scale

\[
 e\,\max_p\Pr(A_p)(\Delta+1)
 \ll X^{-1/3}(\Delta+1).
\]

The true graph may have \(\Delta\asymp X/\log X\), for which this grows like \(X^{2/3}/\log X\). More importantly, even if the inequality happened to hold because the actual \(Z(p)\)'s are tiny, it would prove merely the existence of integers with no hits—something already obvious from the \(K\)-histogram—not the suppression of rare integers with three or more hits.

The lopsided version does not repair this: as observed above, an absent overlap edge can make conditioning on the complement increase the probability of the other event.

# 6. Why standard Stein–Chen does not close the problem

For an ordinary dependency graph, the Arratia–Goldstein–Gordon bound controls total variation by the same \(b_1+b_2\) quantities above. The only automatically valid graph here is complete on the active primes. With that graph the estimate retains essentially the whole pair mass and supplies no new arithmetic cancellation.

Choosing the smaller overlap graph does not help, because it is not a dependency graph; the omitted conditional term is then uncontrolled.

A specially designed size-bias or exchangeable-pair coupling is not logically excluded. But to make it useful one would have to prove that conditioning on

\[
I_p=I_q=1
\]

does not overload the remaining primes. That assertion is exactly the pair-Palm contraction, merely rewritten in Stein–Chen language.

Finally, total-variation Poisson approximation is too weak for the desired factorial moment without a separate uniform-integrability estimate, as the rare-star calculation in Section 3 shows.

# 7. Primewise Sidon and reflection are provably insufficient

The repository already contains the required countermodel in [`hm3_result.tex`](https://github.com/xiangyazi24/Ramanujan_Challenge/blob/main/problems/3.2/hm3_result.tex).

Choose one anchor \(m_0\asymp X^2\). For all but \(O(1)\) window primes, put

\[
a_p=m_0\bmod p,
\qquad
Z_p^*=\{a_p,p-1-a_p\}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then:

- \(Z^*(p)=2\ll p^{2/3}\);
- reflection is built in;
- the two residues can be chosen distinct, nonzero, nonconsecutive, and away from the endpoints;
- every active prime hits the same \(m_0\);
- the universal second-moment bound still holds;
- even the row codegree of two distinct integers is \(O(1)\).

It also satisfies the empirical Sidon hypothesis. A nondegenerate two-point set is multiplicatively Sidon: its only two off-diagonal ordered ratios are reciprocal. They cannot coincide here. Equality would force the ratio to be \(1\) (the two points equal) or \(-1\); the latter, with the second point \(-1-a_p\), would give \(a_p=1+a_p\), impossible.

If \(L\sim X/\log X\) primes are active, then

\[
K_X^*(m_0)=L,
\qquad
\lambda_X^*\asymp\frac1{\log X},
\]

and therefore

\[
\sum_{m<X^2}(K_X^*(m))_3
\ge(L)_3
\gg\frac{X^3}{(\log X)^3},
\]

whereas

\[
X^2(\lambda_X^*)^3
\asymp\frac{X^2}{(\log X)^3}.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
(A)+(B)+(C)\not\Longrightarrow(PP3)\text{ or }(HM)_3.}
\tag{7.2}
\]

The finite statement \(K_X\le5\) for tested \(X\) does not change the logical conclusion. It is not an asymptotic hypothesis. Even if a uniform constant bound were proved, combining it with HM2 yields only

\[
F_3\ll M\lambda_X^2,
\]

leaving a factor \(1/\lambda_X\) relative to HM3. Near-Poisson cumulants through order eight are likewise strong evidence, but at finitely many scales they cannot supply the missing uniform tail theorem.

# 8. What a genuinely combinatorial proof would need

A purely combinatorial proof is still possible in principle, but it must introduce a **cross-prime incidence statement** not implied by the primewise data.

For ordered \(p\ne q\), define

\[
J_{p,q}=|A_p\cap A_q|,
\]

\[
T_{p,q}
=\sum_{m\in A_p\cap A_q}
  \sum_{\ell\ne p,q}1_{A_\ell}(m),
\]

and let

\[
\mu_{p,q}^{\rm fin}
=\sum_{\ell\ne p,q}\frac{|A_\ell|}{M}.
\]

The exact missing combinatorial assertion is

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\ne q}
\bigl(T_{p,q}-J_{p,q}\mu_{p,q}^{\rm fin}\bigr)_+
\ll X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^3.
}
\tag{PP3}
\]

By the pair-Palm characterization proved in `hm3_result.tex`, this is equivalent to \((HM)_3\), up to the already-controlled baseline.

A stronger, potentially attackable version is the absolute link-discrepancy bound

\[
\sum_{p\ne q}
\left|
T_{p,q}-J_{p,q}\mu_{p,q}^{\rm fin}
\right|
\ll X^{2+o(1)}\lambda_X^3.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Graph degree, primewise multiplicative energy, and reflection do not imply (8.1). They concern respectively the two-section of the incidence hypergraph, one-prime ratio collisions, and one-prime involution. Equation (8.1) controls the **links of actual pair-CRT witnesses across a third characteristic**.

That is the sharp strategic conclusion:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The surviving problem is not local dependence; it is cross-prime link expansion.}
}
\]

The empirical Sidon, Palm, ARD, and cumulant data strongly predict this expansion, but a theorem must constrain which reflection orbit the actual Apéry sequence selects in each characteristic. None of \(Z(p)\ll p^{2/3}\), multiplicative Sidonicity, reflection, or a finite maximum-\(K\) census contains that information.
