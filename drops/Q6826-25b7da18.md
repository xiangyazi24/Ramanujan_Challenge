# Cross-prime Apéry zero count: rigorous audit and exact stopping point

## Verdict first

Let

\[
Z_p=\{1\le r<p:p\mid b_r\},\qquad
S(X)=\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|,
\]

where

\[
b_r=\sum_{k=0}^r\binom rk^2\binom{r+k}{k}^2.
\]

I do **not** obtain an unconditional exponent smaller than \(5/3\). The currently banked estimate

\[
|Z_p|\ll p^{2/3}
\]

therefore still gives the best unconditional bound available from the audited toolkit:

\[
\boxed{S(X)\ll \frac{X^{5/3}}{\log X}.}
\]

The main rigorous contribution below is an exact cross-prime reduction. It isolates a single family-average root estimate for the growing gap polynomials \(N_h\). Any sublinear saving over their degree, on average over \(h\) and \(p\), gives a genuine exponent improvement. In particular, if for some \(0\le\theta<1\)

\[
\sum_{h\le H}\sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p)
 \ll \frac{P H^{1+\theta}}{\log P},
\tag{AGR_\theta}
\]

where \(ho_h(p)=\#\{x\in\mathbb F_p:N_h(x)=0\}\), uniformly at the required polynomial range of \(H\), then

\[
\boxed{
S(X)\ll \frac{X^{\,2-1/(2+\theta)}}{\log X}.
}
\]

Thus every \(	heta<1\) beats \(5/3\), and the ideal bounded-orbit scale \(	heta=0\) gives \(X^{3/2}/\log X\). The trivial degree estimate is exactly \(	heta=1\), which recovers \(5/3\).

The stopping point is not fixed-prime root counting. It is a **uniform prime-aspect theorem for a polynomially growing family of number fields/gap polynomials**. Fixed-\(h\) Chebotarev or the prime ideal theorem is insufficient because its constants are not uniform when \(h\asymp X^c\). Characteristic-zero coprimality and resultants do not bypass this: after the required saturation they only control stars or triples of collision edges, while the extremal \(p^{2/3}\) configuration can be a scattered matching. The actual \(p=73\) collision is exactly why unsaturated resultant statements are invalid.

Everything below is unconditional unless explicitly marked as a diagnostic conditional statement.

## 1. Banked gap-polynomial input

Write

\[
P(n)=34n^3+51n^2+27n+5.
\]

The Apéry recurrence is

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}.
\]

The repository proves the following facts, which I use as inputs.

For \(h\ge1\), there is an integer polynomial \(N_h(X)\) with

\[
N_1=1,\qquad N_2(X)=P(X+1),
\]

and recurrence

\[
N_{h+1}(X)=P(X+h)N_h(X)-(X+h)^6N_{h-1}(X).
\tag{1.1}
\]

It has exact degree

\[
\deg N_h=3(h-1).
\tag{1.2}
\]

If \(p\ge7\), \(2\le h\le p\), and

\[
0\le r<r+h<p,\qquad p\mid b_r,\qquad p\mid b_{r+h},
\]

then

\[
N_h(r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{1.3}
\]

Moreover \(N_h\) is not the zero polynomial modulo \(p\) in this range. Hence

\[
\#\{x\in\mathbb F_p:N_h(x)=0\}\le3(h-1).
\tag{1.4}
\]

The no-consecutive-zero theorem gives no gap \(h=1\). The reflection identity

\[
b_{p-1-r}\equiv b_r\pmod p
\tag{1.5}
\]

is also banked.

The existing fixed-prime estimate follows by partitioning the index interval into blocks and charging every additional zero in a block to a zero pair of gap \(h<H\):

\[
|Z_p|\le \frac pH+O(1)+\sum_{2\le h<H}3(h-1)
 \ll \frac pH+H^2.
\]

Taking \(H\asymp p^{1/3}\) yields \(|Z_p|\ll p^{2/3}\).

## 2. The sharp cross-prime gap-sieve inequality

For a dyadic prime interval define

\[
S(P,2P)=\sum_{P<p\le2P}|Z_p|.
\]

For \(h<p\), put

\[
\rho_h(p)=\#\{x\in\mathbb F_p:N_h(x)=0\}.
\]

### Theorem 2.1 (dyadic gap sieve)

For \(P\ge10\) and \(2\le H\le P/2\),

\[
\boxed{
S(P,2P)
\ll \frac{P^2}{H\log P}
  +\sum_{2\le h<H}\sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p).
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

#### Proof

Fix \(p\in(P,2P]\). Partition \(\{1,\ldots,p-1\}\) into consecutive intervals of length at most \(H\). If a block contains \(m\) members of \(Z_p\), then

\[
m\le1+\binom m2.
\]

Summing over blocks gives

\[
|Z_p|\le \left\lceil\frac pH\right\rceil
 +\#\{(r,s)\in Z_p^2:r<s,\ s-r<H,\ r,s\text{ in one block}\}.
\]

Dropping the condition that the two roots lie in the same block only enlarges the second term. Hence

\[
|Z_p|\le \left\lceil\frac pH\right\rceil
 +\sum_{1\le h<H}C_p(h),
\]

where

\[
C_p(h)=\#\{r:1\le r<r+h<p,\ r,r+h\in Z_p\}.
\]

Now \(C_p(1)=0\), and by (1.3),

\[
C_p(h)\le\rho_h(p)\qquad(2\le h<H).
\]

Summing over \(P<p\le2P\), and using the Chebyshev bound

\[
\pi(2P)-\pi(P)\ll P/\log P,
\]

gives (2.1). \(\square\)

### Corollary 2.2 (the current exponent is exactly the degree-scale result)

Using \(ho_h(p)\le3(h-1)\),

\[
S(P,2P)
\ll \frac{P^2}{H\log P}+\frac{P H^2}{\log P}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Taking \(H\asymp P^{1/3}\) gives

\[
S(P,2P)\ll\frac{P^{5/3}}{\log P}.
\]

Dyadic summation gives \(S(X)\ll X^{5/3}/\log X\).

This derivation is useful because it shows that the exponent \(5/3\) is not a mysterious artifact: it is exactly the balance

\[
\text{block cost }P^2/H
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{degree cost }P H^2.
\]

### Theorem 2.3 (the exact exponent-improvement criterion)

Assume that for some \(0\le\theta\le1\), uniformly for the relevant \(P,H\),

\[
\sum_{2\le h<H}\sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p)
 \ll \frac{P H^{1+\theta}}{\log P}.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Then

\[
S(P,2P)
\ll \frac{P^{\,2-1/(2+\theta)}}{\log P}.
\tag{2.4}
\]

Consequently

\[
S(X)
\ll \frac{X^{\,2-1/(2+\theta)}}{\log X}.
\tag{2.5}
\]

#### Proof

Insert (2.3) into (2.1):

\[
S(P,2P)\ll \frac{P^2}{H\log P}
 +\frac{P H^{1+\theta}}{\log P}.
\]

Balance the two terms by taking

\[
H\asymp P^{1/(2+\theta)}.
\]

Both terms are then

\[
\asymp \frac{P^{\,2-1/(2+\theta)}}{\log P}.
\]

Dyadic summation proves (2.5). \(\square\)

For \(	heta=1\), the exponent is \(5/3\). For every \(	heta<1\), it is strictly smaller. More quantitatively, if \(	heta=1-\varepsilon\), then

\[
2-\frac1{3-\varepsilon}
=\frac53-\frac{\varepsilon}{9}+O(\varepsilon^2).
\]

Thus even a very small power saving over the total degree of the growing family gives a genuine cross-prime exponent gain.

## 3. Why fixed-height Chebotarev does not prove Theorem 2.3

Factor the squarefree polynomial \(N_h\) over \(\mathbb Q\) as

\[
N_h=f_{h,1}\cdots f_{h,\nu_h},
\]

with distinct irreducible factors. For fixed \(h\), let

\[
K_{h,j}=\mathbb Q(\alpha_{h,j}),
\]

where \(\alpha_{h,j}\) is a root of \(f_{h,j}\).

Outside finitely many primes dividing leading coefficients, discriminants, or the indices of the orders \(\mathbb Z[\alpha_{h,j}]\), a root of \(f_{h,j}\) modulo \(p\) is the same as a degree-one prime ideal of \(K_{h,j}\) over \(p\). The prime ideal theorem therefore gives, for every fixed \(h\),

\[
\sum_{p\le X}\rho_h(p)
=(\nu_h+o_h(1))\operatorname{Li}(X).
\tag{3.1}
\]

Thus the expected prime average is controlled by the number \(
u_h\) of characteristic-zero irreducible factors, not by the degree \(3(h-1)\). If one had both

\[
\sum_{h\le H}\nu_h\ll H^{1+\theta},\qquad \theta<1,
\tag{3.2}
\]

and a prime-ideal theorem uniform for all \(h\le H\) with

\[
H\asymp P^{1/(2+\theta)},
\]

then Theorem 2.3 would give the desired improvement.

Neither ingredient is presently available in the required all-height, polynomially growing range.

1. The repository contains strong finite-height Galois and irreducibility certificates, and structural results such as squarefreeness, but no proved all-\(h\) bound of the form (3.2) with \(	heta<1\).

2. Even all-\(h\) irreducibility by itself would not suffice. Formula (3.1) is a fixed-number-field theorem. Its error and exceptional-prime threshold depend on the degree and discriminant of \(K_{h,j}\). Here the degree is \(O(h)\), the coefficient heights and discriminants grow with \(h\), and the needed range is \(h=P^c\), not \(h\ll(\log P)^A\). Standard unconditional effective prime-ideal theorems do not give a uniform main term in that regime.

This is the exact distinction between a valid fixed-height Frobenius statement and the missing growing-family theorem. No conjectural independence is being inserted.

## 4. Reversing the sums: exact identity and quantitative failure

There is an exact reversal:

\[
\boxed{
S(X)=\sum_{1\le r<X}
 \#\{p:\ r<p\le X,\ p\mid b_r\}.
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The elementary bound

\[
\binom rk\le2^r,
\qquad
\binom{r+k}{k}\le2^{r+k}\le4^r
\]

gives

\[
b_r\le(r+1)64^r.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Hence, for \(r\ge2\),

\[
\#\{p>r:p\mid b_r\}
\le\frac{\log b_r}{\log(r+1)}
\ll\frac r{\log r}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Summing gives only

\[
S(X)\ll\sum_{r<X}\frac r{\log r}
\ll\frac{X^2}{\log X},
\tag{4.4}
\]

which is much weaker than \(X^{5/3}/\log X\).

### Reflection does not repair the exponent

By (1.5), the non-central roots pair under

\[
r\longleftrightarrow p-1-r.
\]

Therefore

\[
S(X)=2\sum_{r<X/2}
\#\{p\le X:p>2r+1,\ p\mid b_r\}+C(X),
\tag{4.5}
\]

where \(C(X)\le\pi(X)\) counts possible central roots \(r=(p-1)/2\).

The stronger inequality \(p>2r+1\) changes only constants in the height argument. It still gives \(O(X^2/\log X)\). The shell \(r\asymp p/2\) remains of the same order as \(p\), so reflection does not turn the problem into a small-index problem.

### A sharp dyadic hybrid

Let

\[
I(R,P)=\#\{(r,p):R<r\le2R,\ P<p\le2P,\ r<p,\ p\mid b_r\}.
\]

The height bound gives

\[
I(R,P)\ll\frac{R^2}{\log P}.
\tag{4.6}
\]

On the other hand, the gap-polynomial argument localizes to any index interval of length \(R\). If \(J\subset[1,p-1]\) has length \(R\), then

\[
|Z_p\cap J|\ll \frac RH+H^2,
\]

so with \(H\asymp R^{1/3}\),

\[
|Z_p\cap J|\ll R^{2/3}.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Thus

\[
I(R,P)\ll\frac{P R^{2/3}}{\log P}.
\tag{4.8}
\]

Combining,

\[
\boxed{
I(R,P)\ll\frac1{\log P}
\min\{R^2,\,P R^{2/3}\}.
}
\tag{4.9}
\]

The crossover is \(R=P^{3/4}\). For the large-index shells \(R\asymp P\), which remain after reflection as \(R\asymp P/2\), (4.9) is

\[
I(R,P)\ll\frac{P^{5/3}}{\log P}.
\]

Hence the reversed-sum/height hybrid reproduces the same obstruction in the final macroscopic shell. It gives no exponent saving.

## 5. GCD and gap-polynomial structure across two indices

The recurrence gives a useful but insufficient large-prime gcd lemma.

### Lemma 5.1 (large common primes are controlled by one gap value)

Let \(r\ge0\), \(h\ge2\), and let \(q>r+h\) be prime. If

\[
q\mid b_r,
\qquad
q\mid b_{r+h},
\]

then

\[
q\mid N_h(r).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Moreover

\[
0<N_h(r)\le \exp\bigl(C h\log(r+h+2)\bigr)
\tag{5.2}
\]

for an absolute constant \(C\). Consequently

\[
\#\{q>r+h:q\text{ prime},\ q\mid b_r,\ q\mid b_{r+h}\}
\ll h.
\tag{5.3}
\]

#### Proof

The divisibility (5.1) is (1.3), since all recurrence denominators are nonzero modulo \(q\).

For positivity, start the recurrence at the artificial initial state

\[
u_0=0,\qquad u_1=1,
\]

at indices \(r,r+1\). If \(n\ge r+1\) and \(u_n\ge u_{n-1}>0\), then

\[
\begin{aligned}
(n+1)^3u_{n+1}
&=P(n)u_n-n^3u_{n-1}\\
&\ge(P(n)-n^3)u_n.
\end{aligned}
\]

But

\[
P(n)-n^3-(n+1)^3=4(2n+1)^3>0.
\]

Thus \(u_{n+1}>u_n>0\). The rational coefficient carrying \(u_1\) to \(u_h\) is \(N_h(r)\) divided by a positive product of cubes, so \(N_h(r)>0\).

For the upper bound, apply (1.1) at the integer \(r\). For \(j\le h\),

\[
|P(r+j)|+(r+j)^6\ll(r+h+2)^6.
\]

Induction gives

\[
\max\{N_j(r),N_{j-1}(r)\}
\le \bigl(C_1(r+h+2)^6\bigr)^j,
\]

which implies (5.2).

If \(t\) distinct primes \(q>r+h\) divide \(N_h(r)\), then

\[
(r+h+1)^t\le N_h(r).
\]

Taking logarithms and using (5.2) gives \(t\ll h\). \(\square\)

### Quantitative consequence: still no gain

Reverse the close-pair sum in Theorem 2.1. For \(h\le H\), \(r<2P\), Lemma 5.1 allows at most \(O(h)\) primes \(p\in(P,2P]\) with

\[
p\mid b_r,\qquad p\mid b_{r+h},\qquad r+h<p.
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\sum_{h\le H}C_p(h)
\ll P\sum_{h\le H}h
\ll P H^2.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Inserted into the block argument, this gives

\[
S(P,2P)
\ll\frac{P^2}{H\log P}+P H^2.
\tag{5.5}
\]

The optimum is \(H\asymp(P/\log P)^{1/3}\), yielding

\[
S(P,2P)
\ll\frac{P^{5/3}}{(\log P)^{2/3}},
\tag{5.6}
\]

which is weaker by a logarithm than the fixed-prime degree argument. Thus the best elementary gcd/height information is quantitatively on the wrong side.

This is an exact stopping point for the two-index gcd route: one would need an average \(o(h)\) bound for the number of large common prime divisors of \(b_r,b_{r+h}\), uniformly over \(r,h\), with enough saving to survive the \(P H\) choices of \((r,h)\). No such theorem is known for this holonomic sequence.

## 6. Resultants and saturation: what they do and do not control

Suppose the same root \(r\) starts two collision edges of gaps \(h<k\):

\[
p\mid b_r,\quad p\mid b_{r+h},\quad p\mid b_{r+k}.
\]

Then physically

\[
N_h(r)\equiv N_k(r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

If two characteristic-zero polynomials are coprime, a common root modulo \(p\) forces \(p\) to divide their resultant. However, for the Apéry gap polynomials this statement must be made only after removing the cut-edge factors coming from recurrence denominators.

The warning in the question is real, not cosmetic. At \(p=73\), since \(b_2=73\), one has

\[
\gcd_{\mathbb F_{73}}(N_3,N_4)=X+3,
\]

even though the relevant characteristic-zero shifted-coprimality statement has no genuine physical common root. The root \(X=-3\equiv70\pmod{73}\) lies on a cut edge: \(70+4\not<73\). Equivalently, \(X+3\) divides the denominator/cut polynomial that must be saturated away.

Thus the valid implication is of the form

\[
\text{physical common root}
\Longrightarrow
p\mid\operatorname{Res}(N_h,N_k)_{\mathrm{sat}},
\]

not the raw resultant statement.

Even granting perfect all-pairs characteristic-zero coprimality and complete control of all saturated resultants, this does not prove an exponent improvement. It only controls configurations in which one zero is incident to at least two short collision edges, or shifted variants that create a three-zero pattern.

The extremal configuration behind the \(p^{2/3}\) bound can avoid every such event. At the critical scale

\[
H\asymp p^{1/3},
\]

the total degree capacity is

\[
\sum_{h\le H}3h\asymp H^2\asymp p^{2/3}.
\]

One may distribute the close zero pairs among the gaps so that each starting zero is used only once: a scattered matching. Such a configuration saturates the degree budget but contains no star and no triple. Resultants between two different gap polynomials never fire.

Therefore the resultant route has two independent limitations:

1. **Arithmetic:** raw reduction of characteristic-zero coprimality is false without saturation; \(p=73\) is an explicit example.
2. **Combinatorial:** saturated pairwise resultants control common-base or triple configurations, while the degree extremizer is a matching.

This is why characteristic-zero all-pairs coprimality, although valuable, does not by itself lower \(S(X)\).

## 7. Product formulas and global height budgets

### 7.1 The product of the Apéry numbers

From (4.2),

\[
\log\prod_{r<X}b_r\ll\sum_{r<X}r\ll X^2.
\]

Every incidence \(p\mid b_r\), \(r<p\le X\), contributes at least one factor \(p\). Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p\le X}|Z_p|\log p
\le\sum_{r<X}\log b_r
\ll X^2.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

For primes \(p>Y\), this gives

\[
\sum_{Y<p\le X}|Z_p|
\ll\frac{X^2}{\log Y}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Taking \(Y\) as large as \(X\) still gives only \(X^2/\log X\). The product budget is too large by a factor \(X^{1/3}\).

### 7.2 Products of one gap polynomial

For fixed \(h\), Lemma 5.1 gives

\[
\log|N_h(r)|\ll h\log(P+h)
\qquad(0\le r<2P).
\]

Thus

\[
\log\prod_{0\le r<2P}N_h(r)
\ll P h\log P.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Every root \(x\in\mathbb F_p\) of \(N_h\), with \(P<p\le2P\), has a representative \(0\le x<p<2P\), so it contributes a factor \(p\) to the product in (7.3). Therefore

\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p)\log p
\ll P h\log P,
\]

and hence

\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p)\ll P h.
\tag{7.4}
\]

This is weaker by a factor \(\log P\) than the trivial degree-per-prime estimate

\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p)
\ll \frac{P h}{\log P}.
\]

So the product formula does not even reproduce the best known logarithm, let alone an exponent saving.

## 8. Primitive divisors and known holonomic-sequence theorems

The available primitive-divisor and prime-factor results point in the wrong direction for this problem.

### 8.1 Primitive-divisor logic

A primitive divisor theorem says that a term \(b_r\) possesses a prime not dividing earlier terms. This is a **lower bound** on the supply of prime divisors. Our problem requires an upper bound on

\[
\#\{(r,p):r<p\le X,\ p\mid b_r\}.
\]

Even a primitive prime divisor for every \(r\) would add at least one incidence per row and would not prevent a prime from dividing many other rows.

Classical Zsigmondy, Lucas, and Lehmer primitive-divisor theorems concern constant-coefficient linear recurrences, usually with a divisibility property. The Apéry numbers satisfy a recurrence with polynomial coefficients and do not form a divisibility sequence. Those theorems do not apply.

### 8.2 Luca's binary holonomic theorem

Florian Luca, *Prime divisors of binary holonomic sequences*, Advances in Applied Mathematics 40 (2008), proves that a nondegenerate order-two holonomic sequence has at least \(c\log N\) distinct prime divisors in the product of its first \(N\) nonzero terms, under the paper's hypotheses.

This again is a lower bound on the number of distinct primes appearing somewhere. It supplies no upper bound for the number of large primes dividing a fixed term and no upper bound for the multiplicity with which one prime divides different terms.

### 8.3 Luca--Shparlinski on Apéry numbers

F. Luca and I. E. Shparlinski, *Arithmetic properties of Apéry numbers*, Journal of the London Mathematical Society 78 (2008), prove density-one lower bounds for the number of prime factors and for the largest prime factor of \(b_n\). Those results show that typical terms have nontrivial prime divisors. They do not bound the reversed incidence count in (4.1).

### 8.4 Residue-class and Lucas-congruence results

The work of Garaev--Luca--Shparlinski on Apéry numbers in residue classes, Malik--Straub on divisibility of sporadic Apéry-like sequences, and Delaygue on \(p\)-adic valuations and \(p\)-Lucas properties gives powerful congruence and coverage information. It does not provide an upper bound for the number of coefficient zeros among \(0\le r<p\), averaged over \(p\).

The 2026 Caruso--Fürnsinn--Vargas-Montoya--Zudilin theorem on Galois groups of truncated Apéry-like series modulo primes concerns the algebraic structure of the generating polynomial/series reduction. It does not turn the coefficient-zero set \(Z_p\) into a bounded-degree Frobenius class whose average can presently be controlled uniformly in \(p\).

### Literature conclusion

No known theorem on prime divisors of holonomic sequences supplies either of the two missing upper-bound inputs:

\[
\sum_{h\le H}\nu_h\ll H^{1+\theta},\quad \theta<1,
\]

for the characteristic-zero gap polynomials, together with a prime-ideal theorem uniform for \(h=P^c\); or a direct upper bound for the large-prime common-divisor incidence of \(b_r,b_{r+h}\) that saves a power of \(h\).

## 9. Large sieve and determinant methods

### 9.1 Why the ordinary large sieve does not see \(S(X)\)

The large sieve controls a fixed set of integers whose reductions modulo many primes occupy restricted residue sets. Here the set being counted changes with the prime:

\[
Z_p=\{r<p:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\}.
\]

There is no fixed subset \(A\subset[1,X]\) that is known to lie in a small set of residue classes modulo every \(p\). The full interval \([1,X]\) is being tested against a different arithmetic predicate for each prime.

A dual large-sieve expression with coefficients \(c_r\) contains terms

\[
\sum_{p\le X}\sum_{a\in Z_p}
\left|\sum_{r\le X\atop r\equiv a\, (p)}c_r\right|^2.
\]

In the decisive range \(p\asymp X\), each residue class contains at most one \(r<X\), so the expression degenerates to a weighted incidence sum and the standard \(X+Q^2\) large-sieve constant is of order \(X^2\). It gives no saving.

### 9.2 Determinant method

The points \((r,p)\) with \(p\mid b_r\) do not lie on a fixed bounded-degree algebraic curve in \((r,p)\). The integer \(b_r\) has exponential index complexity. Passing to a close pair replaces it by

\[
p\mid N_h(r),
\]

but the degree is \(3(h-1)\), and the required \(h\) grows as a power of \(P\). A determinant method applied separately to each \(h\) therefore reproduces a degree-dependent estimate; summing over \(h\) returns the \(H^2\) term in (2.2).

A successful determinant/large-sieve theorem would have to exploit the recurrence in \(h\) as a genuine low-conductor family structure. No such bounded-conductor two-variable algebraic family is currently available; \(h\) is the length of a transfer-matrix product, not an algebraic parameter of fixed degree.

## 10. The sharpest rigorous partial statement

The strongest clean unconditional reduction is the following.

### Theorem 10.1 ([CROSS-PRIME-GAP-ROOT])

For \(P\ge10\) and \(2\le H\le P/2\),

\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}|Z_p|
\ll \frac{P^2}{H\log P}
 +\mathcal R(P,H),
\]

where

\[
\mathcal R(P,H)
=\sum_{2\le h<H}\sum_{P<p\le2P}
\#\{x\in\mathbb F_p:N_h(x)=0\}.
\]

The banked degree theorem gives

\[
\mathcal R(P,H)\ll \frac{P H^2}{\log P}.
\]

Any estimate

\[
\mathcal R(P,H)
\ll \frac{P H^{2-\delta}}{\log P}
\qquad(\delta>0)
\tag{10.1}
\]

uniformly at

\[
H\asymp P^{1/(3-\delta)}
\]

implies

\[
\boxed{
S(X)\ll
\frac{X^{\,2-1/(3-\delta)}}{\log X}
=\frac{X^{\,5/3-\delta/9+O(\delta^2)}}{\log X}.
}
\tag{10.2}
\]

This is, in my judgment, the sharpest currently useful scalar target. It asks for no independence of the coefficient zeros \(b_r\bmod p\), no pointwise improvement for every prime, and no control of nonzero fibers. It asks only for a power saving over the **aggregate degree count of the moving gap-polynomial family**.

Equivalently, one can target the number-field statement:

1. prove that the total number of irreducible factors satisfies
   
   \[
   \sum_{h\le H}\nu_h\ll H^{2-\delta};
   \]

2. prove a prime-ideal theorem uniform enough that, for \(h\le P^{1/(3-\delta)}\),
   
   \[
   \sum_{P<p\le2P}\rho_h(p)
   \ll \nu_h\frac P{\log P}
   \]

   after summing over \(h\).

The first is a characteristic-zero monodromy/factorization problem. The second is a growing-conductor prime-aspect problem. Both are genuinely absent; neither follows from finite-height Galois certificates or from fixed-\(h\) Chebotarev.

---

## 11. Precise final verdict

**No unconditional exponent improvement is proved.** After auditing the requested routes:

- Reversing the sums plus the exponential height of \(b_r\) gives only \(X^2/\log X\).
- Reflection reduces to \(p>2r+1\) but leaves a macroscopic shell \(r\asymp p\), where the \(5/3\) bound is unchanged.
- Local interval bounds combined with reversed heights give the exact hybrid (4.9); its largest shell is still \(P^{5/3}/\log P\).
- Primitive-divisor and holonomic prime-factor theorems are lower-bound results and do not control cross-prime multiplicity.
- The large-prime gcd lemma (5.3) gives \(O(h)\) common primes for a fixed pair \((r,r+h)\), but summing it gives (5.6), weaker than the current bound.
- Global and gap-polynomial product formulas lose, rather than gain, a logarithm.
- Raw resultant transfer is invalid without saturation; \(p=73\) gives the explicit cut-edge collision. Saturated resultants still miss the scattered-matching extremizer.
- Standard large-sieve and determinant methods see either a changing prime-dependent set or a polynomial family whose degree grows with \(h\); they reproduce the degree barrier.
- Fixed-height prime-ideal/Chebotarev averages are qualitatively favorable but quantitatively nonuniform in the polynomial range of \(h\) needed for an exponent gain.

Therefore the rigorous status is

\[
\boxed{
S(X)\ll X^{5/3}/\log X
\quad\text{remains the best unconditional bound from the audited machinery.}
}
\]

The exact next theorem is [CROSS-PRIME-GAP-ROOT], namely a bound

\[
\mathcal R(P,H)\ll P H^{2-\delta}/\log P
\]

for some fixed \(\delta>0\) in the growing range \(H=P^{1/(3-\delta)}\). Proving that single estimate would give the first genuine exponent improvement, with the explicit exponent in (10.2).
