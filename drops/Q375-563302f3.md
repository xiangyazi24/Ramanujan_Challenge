# Q375 — Poisson concentration for the Apéry large-prime count via Chen–Stein

## Executive verdict

Write

\[
\mathcal P_N:=\{p\text{ prime}:\sqrt N<p\le N\},
\qquad
B_N(n):=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}\mathbf 1_{p\mid b_n}.
\]

The proposed Chen–Stein shortcut does **not** work as stated.

1. For a fixed integer \(n\), the quantities \(X_p(n)=\mathbf1_{p\mid b_n}\) are deterministic. There is no random variable to approximate. Chen–Stein becomes meaningful only after choosing a random \(n\), normally uniform on \(\{0,\ldots,N\}\).

2. After that randomization, all \(X_p\) are functions of the same random integer. No sparse dependency graph is presently known. The only unconditional exact dependency graph is the complete graph, and its Chen–Stein bound contains the entire unresolved pair count.

3. The indicator used in the question is incomplete. Since \(p>\sqrt N\), an integer \(n\le N\) has two base-\(p\) digits,

   \[
   n=a_pp+r_p,\qquad 0\le a_p,r_p<p.
   \]

   The Apéry Lucas congruence gives

   \[
   p\mid b_n
   \iff a_p\in Z_p\ \text{or}\ r_p\in Z_p,
   \]

   not merely \(r_p=n\bmod p\in Z_p\). The suffix-only formula is valid in the top window \(p>n/2\), where \(a_p=1\) and \(b_1=5\), but not in the full fixed window \((\sqrt N,N]\).

4. The averaged second moment is exactly

   \[
   \sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
   =\sum_p|E_p|+2\sum_{p<q}|E_p\cap E_q|,
   \qquad E_p:=\{n\le N:p\mid b_n\}.
   \]

   Thus proving \(O(N)\) is precisely the two-prime incidence theorem; Chen–Stein does not remove it.

5. A pointwise covariance bound alone cannot imply an exponential tail. Even exact pairwise independence can coexist with a rare event on which the whole sum is huge. Pairwise covariance controls only the variance.

6. One-prime discrepancy of each \(Z_p\) is not enough. What is needed is **cross-prime CRT/block discrepancy** for the whole family \((Z_p)_p\), including the three channels involving the leading digit.

7. Even total-variation convergence of the empirical law of \(B_N(n)\) to a Poisson distribution does not control the maximum or the second moment. A single anchored integer can be invisible in total variation and still contribute \(\asymp N^2/\log^2N\) to the energy.

The correct conclusion is:

> **Poisson behavior is a consequence of sufficiently strong cross-prime incidence estimates, not a substitute for them. For P3.2, attack the first two factorial moments directly.**

## 0. Numerical normalization

The notation must retain the scale parameter:

\[
B_N(n)=\#\{p\in(\sqrt N,N]:p\mid b_n\},
\qquad 0\le n\le N.
\]

The distribution changes when either the prime window or the sampled set of integers changes.

For \(Y\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\),

\[
\mathbb EY^2=\lambda+\lambda^2.
\]

Thus \(\lambda=0.6\) gives \(\mathbb EY^2=0.96\). If, on the same sample, one instead observes

\[
\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2\approx1.6
\]

and \(\operatorname{Var}(B_N)/\mathbb EB_N\approx1\), then the compatible Poisson mean is the positive solution of

\[
m+m^2=1.6,
\]

namely \(m\approx0.86\), not \(0.6\). The quoted numbers may come from different windows, but they should not be combined as one fitted law.

The repository’s large scan `problems/3.2/bn_bigscan_report.md` concerns the **top-half** count \(n/2<p\le n\). Its global mean through two million is about \(0.053\), with variance/mean about \(0.999\), and its Lucas event is suffix-only because the leading digit is \(1\). That experiment is excellent evidence for Poisson behavior in the top-half model, but it is not the same random variable as the fixed window \(\sqrt N<p\le N\).

## 1. Fixed \(n\) versus random \(n\)

### 1.1 Fixed \(n\)

For a fixed integer \(n\),

\[
X_p(n):=\mathbf1_{p\mid b_n}
\]

is a constant in \(\{0,1\}\). Hence

\[
B_N(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}X_p(n)
\]

is a deterministic integer. Statements such as

\[
\Pr(B_N(n)\ge T),
\qquad
d_{\rm TV}(B_N(n),\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda))
\]

are undefined unless an additional probability space is introduced.

Randomizing the prime \(p\) does not turn \(B_N(n)\) into a sum of Bernoulli trials; it produces a different random variable whose mean is \(B_N(n)/|\mathcal P_N|\).

### 1.2 The natural probability space

Take

\[
U_N\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
\]

and define

\[
X_p:=\mathbf1_{p\mid b_{U_N}},
\qquad
B_N:=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}X_p.
\]

This random variable is exactly the empirical distribution of the deterministic values \(B_N(n)\):

\[
\Pr(B_N=t)
=\frac1{N+1}\#\{0\le n\le N:B_N(n)=t\}.
\]

Chen–Stein may now be discussed. Every conclusion, however, is initially an average-over-\(n\) conclusion.

A local randomization, for example choosing \(n\) uniformly in an interval \([m,m+H]\), likewise yields an averaged statement on that interval. It does not give the value at the fixed point \(m\).

## 2. The exact Lucas event: two digits, not one

Let

\[
Z_p:=\{0\le r<p:b_r\equiv0\pmod p\},
\qquad z_p:=|Z_p|.
\]

For \(p>\sqrt N\) and \(n\le N\), write

\[
n=a_pp+r_p,
\qquad
0\le a_p,r_p<p.
\]

The Apéry Lucas congruence gives

\[
b_n\equiv b_{a_p}b_{r_p}\pmod p.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
X_p(n)=1
\iff
r_p\in Z_p\quad\text{or}\quad a_p\in Z_p.
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

Define the two digit events

\[
E_p^{(0)}:=\{n\le N:n\bmod p\in Z_p\},
\]

\[
E_p^{(1)}:=\{n\le N:\lfloor n/p\rfloor\in Z_p\}.
\]

Then

\[
E_p=E_p^{(0)}\cup E_p^{(1)}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

The question’s indicator \(1_{n\bmod p\in Z_p}\) is only \(1_{E_p^{(0)}}\).

### Exact one-prime counts

Write

\[
N=Q_pp+s_p,
\qquad 0\le s_p<p,
\]

and put

\[
\begin{aligned}
t_p&:=|Z_p\cap\{0,\ldots,Q_p-1\}|,\\
u_p&:=|Z_p\cap\{0,\ldots,s_p\}|,\\
\delta_p&:=\mathbf1_{Q_p\in Z_p}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Then

\[
|E_p^{(0)}|=Q_pz_p+u_p,
\tag{2.3}
\]

\[
|E_p^{(1)}|=pt_p+(s_p+1)\delta_p,
\tag{2.4}
\]

and

\[
|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_p^{(1)}|=z_pt_p+u_p\delta_p.
\tag{2.5}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
|E_p|
=Q_pz_p+u_p+(p-z_p)t_p
 +\delta_p((s_p+1)-u_p).
}
\tag{2.6}
\]

Thus even the marginal probability is not generally

\[
\frac{z_p}{p}+O(1/N).
\]

A low zero digit \(a\in Z_p\cap[0,Q_p]\) contributes an interval of roughly \(p\) integers. Controlling the mean requires, in addition to the suffix intensity, a weighted low-root estimate such as

\[
\frac1N\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_N}
 p\,|Z_p\cap[0,\lfloor N/p\rfloor]|=O(1).
\tag{2.7}
\]

In the top-half window \(p>n/2\), one has \(a_p=1\), and \(1\notin Z_p\) for \(p\ge7\) because \(b_1=5\). Only in that narrower situation does (2.1) reduce to the suffix event.

## 3. The exact first and second moments

Put

\[
\mu_p:=\Pr(X_p=1)=\frac{|E_p|}{N+1},
\]

\[
\mu_{p,q}:=\Pr(X_p=X_q=1)
=rac{|E_p\cap E_q|}{N+1},
\]

and

\[
\lambda_N:=\mathbb EB_N=\sum_p\mu_p.
\]

Since \(X_p^2=X_p\),

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb EB_N^2
=\lambda_N+2\sum_{p<q}\mu_{p,q}.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
=\sum_p|E_p|+2\sum_{p<q}|E_p\cap E_q|.
}
\tag{3.2}
\]

The second falling factorial satisfies

\[
\mathbb E(B_N)_2=2\sum_{p<q}\mu_{p,q}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N)
\]

is equivalent to

\[
\sum_p|E_p|=O(N),
\qquad
\sum_{p<q}|E_p\cap E_q|=O(N).
\tag{3.4}
\]

This is exactly the pair-count theorem. A Poisson theorem is unnecessary at second order.

If \(\lambda_N=O(1)\), a useful sufficient condition is bounded aggregate positive covariance:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}
(\mu_{p,q}-\mu_p\mu_q)_+=O(1).
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

Indeed,

\[
\sum_{p<q}\mu_p\mu_q\le\frac12\lambda_N^2.
\]

For an actual Poisson second-factorial-moment asymptotic one would want

\[
\lambda_N\to\lambda,
\qquad
\sum_p\mu_p^2\to0,
\qquad
\sum_{p\ne q}(\mu_{p,q}-\mu_p\mu_q)\to0.
\tag{3.6}
\]

Even (3.6) proves only the first two factorial moments, not a full Poisson law.

### The all-\(n\) consequence of an \(O(N)\) energy theorem

The deduction in the question is valid once the energy theorem is genuinely proved:

\[
\max_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
\le\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
\ll N.
\]

Hence

\[
\max_{n\le N}B_N(n)\ll\sqrt N
=o(N/\log N).
\]

The error is not in this deduction. The error is treating empirical or total-variation Poisson behavior as though it already implied the required energy bound.

## 4. What Chen–Stein would actually require

A standard local-dependence version of the Arratia–Goldstein–Gordon theorem uses neighborhoods \(\mathcal N_p\) and quantities of the schematic form

\[
\begin{aligned}
b_1&=\sum_p\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_p}\mu_p\mu_q,\\
b_2&=\sum_p\sum_{\substack{q\in\mathcal N_p\\q\ne p}}\mu_{p,q},\\
b_3&=\sum_p
\mathbb E\left|
\mathbb E[X_p-\mu_p\mid
\sigma(X_q:q\notin\mathcal N_p)]
\right|.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The total-variation error is bounded by an explicit multiple of \(b_1+b_2+b_3\).

In a true dependency graph, \(X_p\) is jointly independent of all variables outside \(\mathcal N_p\), so \(b_3=0\). This is much stronger than small pairwise covariance.

Here every \(X_p\) is a function of the same random integer \(U_N\). No prime-local independent random inputs have been identified. Consequently:

- the only unconditional exact dependency graph is the complete graph;
- for the complete graph, \(b_2\) is essentially \(\mathbb E(B_N)_2\), the unresolved pair moment;
- choosing singleton neighborhoods merely transfers all nonlocal dependence to \(b_3\);
- declaring the neighborhoods empty would assert mutual independence, which is false and unproved.

An Apéry-specific Kubilius model, Stein coupling, or conditional-independence theorem is possible in principle. But proving it would require quantitative control of the same CRT and digit-block incidences that occur in (3.2). It would not be a shortcut.

## 5. The CRT calculation and its scale

First consider only the suffix/suffix channel. For \(p\ne q\) in \(\mathcal P_N\), one has \(pq>N\). For every

\[
(a,b)\in Z_p\times Z_q,
\]

let \(c_{p,q}(a,b)\in[0,pq)\) be the CRT representative satisfying

\[
c_{p,q}(a,b)\equiv a\pmod p,
\qquad
c_{p,q}(a,b)\equiv b\pmod q.
\]

Then exactly

\[
A_{p,q}^{00}
:=|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(0)}|
=\sum_{a\in Z_p}\sum_{b\in Z_q}
\mathbf1_{c_{p,q}(a,b)\le N}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The random-CRT benchmark is

\[
\frac{(N+1)z_pz_q}{pq}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

For one pair, the trivial discrepancy bound is

\[
\left|
A_{p,q}^{00}-\frac{(N+1)z_pz_q}{pq}
\right|\le z_pz_q.
\tag{5.3}
\]

After normalization this is the \(O(z_pz_q/N)\) error from the question. It is not globally small. If \(z_p\) has bounded average, then

\[
\frac1N\sum_{p<q}z_pz_q
\asymp
\frac1N\left(\frac N{\log N}\right)^2
=rac N{\log^2N},
\tag{5.4}
\]

whereas a bounded normalized second moment requires \(O(1)\).

Equivalently, in the unnormalized energy, the accumulated trivial error is

\[
O(N^2/\log^2N),
\]

which is precisely the anchored-star scale that must be beaten.

Thus one needs a genuinely aggregate theorem, for example

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}A_{p,q}^{00}=O(N),
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

or the weaker one-sided discrepancy estimate

\[
\sum_{p<q}
\left(
A_{p,q}^{00}-\frac{(N+1)z_pz_q}{pq}
\right)_+=O(N).
\tag{5.6}
\]

For \(p,q\asymp N\) and singleton zero sets, an individual joint probability is either \(0\) or about \(1/N\), whereas the independent benchmark is about \(1/N^2\). Pointwise approximate independence is therefore the wrong formulation. The desired phenomenon is that only the correct sparse proportion of prime pairs possess a low CRT representative.

## 6. Pairwise covariance does not give Poisson concentration

Let

\[
M_N:=|\mathcal P_N|\asymp N/\log N.
\]

If

\[
|\operatorname{Cov}(X_p,X_q)|\le\delta_N
\qquad(p\ne q),
\]

then

\[
\operatorname{Var}(B_N)
\le\lambda_N+2\binom{M_N}{2}\delta_N
\ll\lambda_N+M_N^2\delta_N.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Chebyshev gives only

\[
\Pr(B_N\ge T)
\le
\frac{\lambda_N+M_N^2\delta_N}{(T-\lambda_N)^2}
\qquad(T>\lambda_N).
\tag{6.2}
\]

This is a polynomial tail, not an exponential one.

### What scale of \(\delta_N\) controls the second moment?

If \(\lambda_N=O(1)\), then a uniform bound

\[
\delta_N=O(M_N^{-2})
=O\left(\frac{\log^2N}{N^2}\right)
\tag{6.3}
\]

is sufficient for a Poisson-scale \(O(1)\) normalized second moment.

For the weaker P3.2 target

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
=o(N^2/\log^2N),
\]

i.e.

\[
\mathbb EB_N^2=o(N/\log^2N),
\]

the uniform covariance hypothesis would need only

\[
\delta_N=o(1/N).
\tag{6.4}
\]

But neither (6.3) nor (6.4) produces an exponential tail from pairwise information alone.

### Exact pairwise independence can still have a huge upper tail

Let \(m\) be prime, let \(U,V\) be independent uniform variables in \(\mathbf F_m\), and for each \(t\in\mathbf F_m\) put

\[
X_t:=\mathbf1_{Ut+V=0}.
\]

Each \(X_t\) has mean \(1/m\), and every distinct pair is exactly independent. Nevertheless

\[
W_m:=\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_m}X_t
=
\begin{cases}
1,&U\ne0,\\
0,&U=0,\ V\ne0,\\
m,&U=V=0.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus

\[
\Pr(W_m=m)=1/m^2,
\]

which is vastly larger than \(e^{-cm}\), despite zero pairwise covariance. Also \(W_m\) does not converge to \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\).

Therefore there is **no value of \(\delta\)** for which the hypothesis

\[
|\operatorname{Cov}(X_p,X_q)|\le\delta
\]

alone implies a Poisson or Chernoff upper tail. One needs higher-order structure such as:

- mutual independence;
- negative association;
- a bounded-degree dependency graph;
- a suitable martingale/mgf condition;
- or Poisson-scale factorial-moment bounds of all orders up to the tail threshold.

For example, if for every \(1\le k\le T\) one could prove

\[
\mathbb E(B_N)_k\le C^k,
\tag{6.5}
\]

then

\[
\Pr(B_N\ge T)
\le\frac{C^T}{(T)_T}
=rac{C^T}{T!},
\tag{6.6}
\]

which has the desired Poisson-type decay. The case \(k=2\) alone is only a variance theorem.

## 7. What “spreading of \(Z_p\)” would have to mean

A one-prime discrepancy estimate might say that for intervals \(I\subset[0,p)\),

\[
\#(Z_p\cap I)
=\frac{z_p|I|}{p}+O(D_p).
\tag{7.1}
\]

Such an estimate is useful for:

- marginal suffix counts;
- the low-root term \(Z_p\cap[0,N/p]\);
- some mixed digit-channel estimates.

It does **not** by itself control (5.1). The suffix/suffix problem asks whether the CRT image of

\[
Z_p\times Z_q
\]

is equidistributed in the initial segment \([0,N]\subset\mathbf Z/(pq)\mathbf Z\). This is a two-prime statement.

The decisive obstruction is horizontal alignment. The abstract choice

\[
Z_p^*:=\{N\bmod p\}
\]

has cardinality one for every prime and absolute interval discrepancy at most one, yet all CRT classes meet at the same integer \(N\). Consequently

\[
B_N(N)=M_N\asymp N/\log N
\]

and

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
\ge B_N(N)^2
\asymp N^2/\log^2N.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Thus no property imposed separately on each \(Z_p\)—bounded size, reflection, no adjacent roots, or one-dimensional discrepancy—can rule out the star.

### The four digit channels

Replace the OR in (2.2) by the dominating sum

\[
Y_p:=\mathbf1_{E_p^{(0)}}+\mathbf1_{E_p^{(1)}}.
\]

Then

\[
X_p\le Y_p\le2X_p,
\]

so

\[
\sum_nB_N(n)^2=O(N)
\iff
\sum_n\left(\sum_pY_p(n)\right)^2=O(N).
\tag{7.3}
\]

The pair energy splits into four nonnegative channels:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{p<q}
\left(
|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(0)}|
+|E_p^{(0)}\cap E_q^{(1)}|
+|E_p^{(1)}\cap E_q^{(0)}|
+|E_p^{(1)}\cap E_q^{(1)}|
\right).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

They are:

1. suffix/suffix — sparse CRT representatives;
2. suffix/leading — a residue class modulo \(p\) crossing a root-indexed \(q\)-block;
3. leading/suffix — the symmetric mixed channel;
4. leading/leading — overlaps of intervals
   \([ap,(a+1)p-1]\) and \([bq,(b+1)q-1]\).

The last overlap can have length comparable to \(\min(p,q)\), so it is qualitatively different from the suffix/suffix event, where each pair of residues has at most one representative below \(N\).

Therefore “the zero sets are spread out” helps only if upgraded to a theorem controlling these cross-prime CRT and block incidences in aggregate.

A convenient sufficient formulation is

\[
\sum_p\mu_p=O(1)
\tag{7.5}
\]

and

\[
\sum_{p<q}
(\mu_{p,q}-\mu_p\mu_q)_+=O(1).
\tag{7.6}
\]

This is the exact horizontal anti-anchoring condition needed at second order.

## 8. Why total-variation Poisson convergence does not control energy or maxima

Let \(Y_N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\). Modify it with probability \(1/N\) by assigning the value

\[
L_N:=\left\lfloor\frac N{\log N}\right\rfloor.
\]

Call the result \(W_N\). Then

\[
d_{\rm TV}(W_N,Y_N)\le1/N\to0,
\]

and even

\[
\mathbb EW_N=\lambda+O(1/\log N)\to\lambda.
\]

But

\[
\mathbb EW_N^2
\ge\frac{L_N^2}{N}
\asymprac N{\log^2N}\to\infty.
\tag{8.1}
\]

This is exactly what one bad integer does to the empirical Apéry distribution. Moving one of the \(N+1\) sample points changes total variation by at most \(1/(N+1)\), while a value

\[
B_N(n_0)\asymp N/\log N
\]

contributes

\[
\asymp N^2/\log^2N
\]

to the unnormalized second moment.

Since

\[
0\le B_N(n)\le M_N\asymp N/\log N,
\]

a TV error \(\varepsilon_N\) controls an arbitrary second-moment discrepancy only at the scale

\[
O(\varepsilon_NM_N^2).
\]

To deduce a bounded normalized second moment from TV alone would require roughly

\[
\varepsilon_N
=O(M_N^{-2})
=O(\log^2N/N^2),
\tag{8.2}
\]

not merely \(o(1)\).

Thus a qualitative Chen–Stein theorem is still insufficient for the energy target unless accompanied by uniform integrability or a strong quantitative tail bound.

## 9. The all-\(n\) maximum problem

Suppose a genuine Poisson approximation gives

\[
\Pr(B_N\ge T)
\le
\Pr(\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_N)\ge T)+\varepsilon_N.
\tag{9.1}
\]

To conclude that **no** integer \(n\le N\) has \(B_N(n)\ge T\), an empirical union bound would need

\[
(N+1)
\left(
\Pr(\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_N)\ge T)+\varepsilon_N
\right)<1.
\tag{9.2}
\]

For bounded \(\lambda_N\), the Poisson tail becomes \(o(1/N)\) at approximately

\[
T\sim\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}.
\]

But (9.2) also requires

\[
\varepsilon_N=o(1/N).
\tag{9.3}
\]

Ordinary Chen–Stein convergence \(\varepsilon_N\to0\) is nowhere near enough.

There is no “uniform Chen–Stein for fixed \(n\)” that avoids this issue. At a fixed point the variables are deterministic. One can prove Poisson approximation uniformly for many probability spaces—for example, uniformly over large intervals—but an isolated bad point can remain invisible until the sampling interval has length one, at which point there is no probabilistic approximation left.

### What a Poisson model predicts for the maximum

If the \(N\) values behaved like independent Poisson variables of bounded mean, then

\[
\max_{n\le N}B_N(n)
\]

would grow around \(\log N/\log\log N\), not remain bounded. This is still

\[
o(N/\log N),
\]

so it would be more than enough for P3.2. But proving that maximum requires tail control at probability scale \(1/N\), not convergence of the bulk histogram.

### Density-one versus all-\(n\)

An energy estimate

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2\le CN
\]

gives

\[
\#\{n\le N:B_N(n)>T\}
\le CN/T^2.
\tag{9.4}
\]

This is strong density-one information for every \(T(N)\to\infty\), and simultaneously the deterministic maximum bound \(B_N(n)\le\sqrt{CN}\). A qualitative Poisson law gives the former bulk information but not the latter moment bound.

## 10. The right P3.2 targets

For the direct second-moment route, define

```text
LPD1(N):
  sum_{sqrt(N) < p <= N}
    #{0 <= n <= N : p divides b_n}
  = O(N).
```

After Lucas, this includes both the suffix mass and the weighted low-leading-digit mass in (2.7).

For \(i,j\in\{0,1\}\), define

```text
LPD2_ij(N):
  sum_{sqrt(N) < p < q <= N}
    #{0 <= n <= N : digit channel i hits at p
                     and digit channel j hits at q}
  = O(N).
```

The four estimates \(LPD2_{00},LPD2_{01},LPD2_{10},LPD2_{11}\), together with \(LPD1\), imply

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2=O(N).
\]

For the original GCD theorem this is stronger than necessary. It suffices to prove

\[
\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
=o(N^2/\log^2N).
\tag{10.1}
\]

Thus any fixed power saving over the anchored-star energy scale would close the route.

If a full Poisson theorem is later desired, the natural number-theoretic method is factorial moments. For every fixed \(k\), one would seek

\[
\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\in\mathcal P_N\\\text{distinct}}}
\frac1{N+1}
\left|
E_{p_1}\cap\cdots\cap E_{p_k}
\right|
\longrightarrow\lambda^k.
\tag{10.2}
\]

For P3.2, only \(k=1,2\) and upper bounds are needed. Proving (10.2) for all fixed \(k\) would be substantial overkill.

## 11. Direct answers

### 1. Can Chen–Stein be applied for a fixed \(n\)?

No. For fixed \(n\), all \(X_p(n)\) and \(B_N(n)\) are deterministic. Chen–Stein applies after randomizing \(n\), typically uniformly in \([0,N]\), and then describes the empirical distribution over \(n\). It gives no pointwise statement without an additional argument.

### 2. Is the averaged second moment the right alternative?

Yes. It is the exact direct target:

\[
\frac1{N+1}\sum_{n\le N}B_N(n)^2
=\sum_p\mu_p+2\sum_{p<q}\mu_{p,q}.
\]

Proving it bounded is equivalent to bounding the one-prime incidence and the two-prime pair count. Chen–Stein’s \(b_2\) term is not a substitute for that pair bound; it is essentially the pair bound itself.

### 3. Does a uniform covariance bound imply an exponential tail, and what \(\delta\) is needed?

No exponential tail follows from pairwise covariance alone, for any \(\delta\), even \(\delta=0\). The finite-field construction in Section 6 has exact pairwise independence and a rare event on which the sum equals the number of variables.

If \(M_N\asymp N/\log N\), then

\[
\delta_N=O(1/M_N^2)
=O(\log^2N/N^2)
\]

is sufficient only for a bounded variance/second moment. The weaker P3.2 energy target would follow from \(\delta_N=o(1/N)\), assuming bounded mean. Exponential concentration requires higher-order or mgf information.

### 4. Does spreading of \(Z_p\) help?

Yes, but one-dimensional spread of each individual \(Z_p\) is insufficient. One needs aggregate two-prime discrepancy for CRT images and block overlaps. The relevant statement is a horizontal anti-alignment theorem such as (5.5), (5.6), or bounded aggregate positive covariance (7.6), together with control of all four Lucas digit channels.

### 5. Is there a uniform Chen–Stein theorem controlling \(\max_nB_N(n)\)?

Not in the sense needed here. A Poisson approximation for random \(n\) controls the bulk empirical law. To control the maximum by a union bound, the approximation error must be \(o(1/N)\) at the relevant tail, and to infer a bounded second moment from TV alone it must be roughly \(O(\log^2N/N^2)\). Rare aligned integers are otherwise invisible. The deterministic all-\(n\) problem requires a moment-sensitive or direct anti-anchoring theorem.

---

## Final conclusion

\[
\boxed{
\text{Chen–Stein does not bypass Apéry pair dispersion.}
}
\]

The rigorous route is:

1. randomize \(n\) only to formulate averages;
2. use the two-digit Lucas decomposition;
3. prove the one-prime mass bound;
4. prove aggregate bounds for the four two-prime channels;
5. deduce the second moment and hence the deterministic maximum bound.

The observed Poisson law is valuable evidence that these arithmetic incidence estimates are true. It is not, by itself, an input from which they follow.
