# P3.2 Route-A audit: the reflection–Casoratian prime is a forced ambient factor

## Executive verdict

Let \(p=n-r>n/2\) be an odd top-half candidate, and put

\[
 s=p-1-r=n-1-2r.
\]

Suppose first that \(r\ne s\), and set

\[
 m=\min(r,s),\qquad M=\max(r,s),\qquad h=M-m=|s-r|,
\]

\[
 \varepsilon=\operatorname{sgn}(s-r),\qquad
 Q_{m,M}=\frac{M!}{m!}=\prod_{j=m+1}^{M}j.
\]

For the Apéry companion normalized by \((a_0,a_1)=(0,6)\), the exact answer is

\[
 \boxed{
 D_{r,s}:=a_s b_r-a_r b_s
 =\varepsilon\,\frac{6N_h(m)}{Q_{m,M}^{\,3}},
 }
 \tag{V.1}
\]

where

\[
 N_0(x)=0,\qquad N_1(x)=1,
\]

\[
 N_{j+1}(x)=P(x+j)N_j(x)-(x+j)^6N_{j-1}(x),
 \qquad
 P(x)=34x^3+51x^2+27x+5.
 \tag{V.2}
\]

The decisive specialization is

\[
 r+s=p-1,\qquad h\equiv0\pmod2,\qquad p=2m+h+1.
 \tag{V.3}
\]

The exact reflection symmetry of the gap continuant is

\[
 N_h(-x-h-1)=(-1)^{h-1}N_h(x).
 \tag{V.4}
\]

Hence, for even \(h>0\),

\[
 \boxed{
 N_h(x)=(2x+h+1)K_h(x),\qquad K_h(x)\in\mathbb Z[x].
 }
 \tag{V.5}
\]

At the reflected pair,

\[
 \boxed{N_h(m)=pK_h(m).}
 \tag{V.6}
\]

This factor \(p\) is present for **every** odd top-half prime \(p\), whether or not \(p\mid b_r\). It is the forced parity factor of the reflected continuant, equivalently the zero top-left entry of the universal anti-diagonal middle transfer. The proposed target divisibility is therefore not new arithmetic information.

The exact least denominator clearing is also explicit. Put

\[
 g_{m,h}=\gcd\!\left(Q_{m,M}^{\,3},\,6N_h(m)\right).
\]

Then \(D_{r,s}\) in lowest terms is

\[
 \boxed{
 D_{r,s}
 =\frac{\varepsilon\,6N_h(m)/g_{m,h}}
        {Q_{m,M}^{\,3}/g_{m,h}},
 \qquad
 \operatorname{den}(D_{r,s})
 =\frac{Q_{m,M}^{\,3}}{g_{m,h}}.
 }
 \tag{V.7}
\]

Since \(M<p\), the denominator is a \(p\)-unit. Using (V.6),

\[
 g_{m,h}=\gcd\!\left(Q_{m,M}^{\,3},\,6K_h(m)\right)
\]

and

\[
 \boxed{
 \operatorname{num}(D_{r,s})
 =\varepsilon\,p\,
   \frac{6K_h(m)}{g_{m,h}}.
 }
 \tag{V.8}
\]

Thus the primitive numerator contains exactly the ambient linear factor \(p\), plus a quotient for which target badness gives no further divisibility. In particular, dividing out the factor \(p=2m+h+1\) destroys the only guaranteed \(p\)-divisibility.

This completely blocks the proposed height argument:

* grouping by \(h=|3p-(2n+1)|\) puts at most two primes in each group and merely reindexes the same ambient factors;
* taking products or lcms over all candidate groups has logarithmic height at least
  
  \[
  \vartheta(n)-\vartheta(n/2)-O(\log n)
  =\frac n2+o(n);
  \]
* taking products or lcms only over actual targets is circular: their unavoidable factor is already the target radical;
* interval denominator lcms do not combine the separate numerator divisibilities, and a macroscopic interval lcm itself has linear logarithmic height;
* a long-gap primitive numerator is itself exponentially large:
  
  \[
  \log|\operatorname{num}(D_{r,s})|
  \ge h\log(17+12\sqrt2)-O(\log n).
  \]

Therefore the reflection–Casoratian does **not** prove that the top-half target radical is \(\exp(o(n))\). More strongly, after saturation by its forced linear factor, it supplies no target-selective divisor at all.

The central case \(r=s=(p-1)/2\) gives \(D_{r,s}=0\), so it cannot be used as a nonzero height carrier. For fixed \(n\) there is at most one such prime, \(p=(2n+1)/3\), and its logarithmic contribution is \(O(\log n)\); it may be removed at the outset.

## 1. Exact Wronskian and telescoping formula

Both \(a_j\) and \(b_j\) satisfy

\[
 (j+1)^3u_{j+1}=P(j)u_j-j^3u_{j-1},
 \qquad
 P(j)=34j^3+51j^2+27j+5.
 \tag{1.1}
\]

Define

\[
 W_j=a_jb_{j+1}-a_{j+1}b_j.
\]

Substitution of (1.1) gives

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 W_j
 &=\frac{j^3}{(j+1)^3}
   \left(a_{j-1}b_j-a_jb_{j-1}\right)\\
 &=\frac{j^3}{(j+1)^3}W_{j-1}.
 \end{aligned}
\]

Since

\[
 W_0=a_0b_1-a_1b_0=-6,
\]

we obtain

\[
 \boxed{
 a_jb_{j+1}-a_{j+1}b_j=-\frac6{(j+1)^3}.
 }
 \tag{1.2}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
 a_{j+1}b_j-a_jb_{j+1}=\frac6{(j+1)^3}.
 \tag{1.3}
\]

As all \(b_j>0\), division gives the exact ratio increment

\[
 \boxed{
 \frac{a_{j+1}}{b_{j+1}}-\frac{a_j}{b_j}
 =\frac{6}{(j+1)^3b_jb_{j+1}}.
 }
 \tag{1.4}
\]

For integers \(0\le m<M\), summing (1.4) from \(j=m\) to \(M-1\) yields

\[
 \boxed{
 a_Mb_m-a_mb_M
 =6b_mb_M\sum_{j=m}^{M-1}
   \frac1{(j+1)^3b_jb_{j+1}}.
 }
 \tag{1.5}
\]

In particular this determinant is strictly positive for \(m<M\). Formula (1.5) is the exact telescoping form requested in the question.

## 2. Exact continuant/Casoratian normalization

Fix \(m\ge0\), and write

\[
 C_h(m)=a_{m+h}b_m-a_mb_{m+h}.
\]

The recurrence in the upper index gives

\[
 (m+h)^3C_h(m)
 =P(m+h-1)C_{h-1}(m)
  -(m+h-1)^3C_{h-2}(m).
 \tag{2.1}
\]

Put

\[
 Q_h(m)=\prod_{j=1}^{h}(m+j)=\frac{(m+h)!}{m!},
\]

and

\[
 E_h(m)=\frac{Q_h(m)^3}{6}C_h(m).
\]

Multiplying (2.1) by \(Q_{h-1}(m)^3/6\) gives

\[
 E_h(m)
 =P(m+h-1)E_{h-1}(m)
  -(m+h-1)^6E_{h-2}(m).
 \tag{2.2}
\]

The initial values are

\[
 E_0(m)=0,
\]

and, by (1.3),

\[
 E_1(m)
 =\frac{(m+1)^3}{6}
   \left(a_{m+1}b_m-a_mb_{m+1}\right)
 =1.
\]

Thus \(E_h(m)=N_h(m)\), where \(N_h\) is defined by (V.2). Therefore

\[
 \boxed{
 N_h(m)
 =\frac{((m+h)!/m!)^3}{6}
   \left(a_{m+h}b_m-a_mb_{m+h}\right).
 }
 \tag{2.3}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
 \boxed{
 a_{m+h}b_m-a_mb_{m+h}
 =\frac{6N_h(m)}{((m+h)!/m!)^3}.
 }
 \tag{2.4}
\]

Combining (1.5) and (2.3) also gives the positive continuant formula

\[
 \boxed{
 N_h(m)
 =\left(\frac{(m+h)!}{m!}\right)^3
   b_mb_{m+h}
   \sum_{j=m}^{m+h-1}
   \frac1{(j+1)^3b_jb_{j+1}}.
 }
 \tag{2.5}
\]

The sign in (V.1) is now immediate: if \(s>r\), use \(m=r,M=s\); if \(s<r\), reverse the determinant.

## 3. Reflection forces the candidate prime before badness is imposed

The polynomial \(P\) is odd about \(-1/2\):

\[
 P(-1-x)=-P(x).
 \tag{3.1}
\]

Indeed,

\[
 P(x)=(2x+1)(17x^2+17x+5),
\]

and the quadratic factor is invariant under \(x\mapsto-1-x\).

The continuant has the exact reflection law

\[
 \boxed{
 N_h(-x-h-1)=(-1)^{h-1}N_h(x).
 }
 \tag{3.2}
\]

This follows either by induction from (V.2), or by reversing the tridiagonal determinant in Section 5 below and using (3.1).

Now specialize to

\[
 r+s=p-1,\qquad m=\min(r,s),\qquad M=m+h.
\]

Because \(p\) is odd, \(r+s\) is even, so \(r\) and \(s\) have the same parity and \(h\) is even. Moreover

\[
 p=m+M+1=2m+h+1.
 \tag{3.3}
\]

For even \(h>0\), equation (3.2) says that \(N_h\) is odd around the half-integer \(-(h+1)/2\). Therefore \(2x+h+1\) divides \(N_h(x)\) in \(\mathbb Q[x]\). Since \(2x+h+1\) is primitive and \(N_h\in\mathbb Z[x]\), Gauss's lemma gives an integral quotient:

\[
 N_h(x)=(2x+h+1)K_h(x),\qquad K_h(x)\in\mathbb Z[x].
 \tag{3.4}
\]

Evaluating at \(x=m\) gives

\[
 N_h(m)=pK_h(m).
 \tag{3.5}
\]

There is also a useful one-line congruence proof. Since

\[
 m-p=-m-h-1,
\]

(3.2) gives

\[
 N_h(m-p)=-N_h(m).
\]

But \(N_h\in\mathbb Z[x]\), so

\[
 N_h(m-p)\equiv N_h(m)\pmod p.
\]

Hence \(2N_h(m)\equiv0\pmod p\), and \(p\mid N_h(m)\).

No use was made of \(p\mid b_r\). Thus the divisibility of the Casoratian numerator by \(p\) is an ambient reflection identity, not an Apéry-zero certificate.

This exactly matches the matrix result in the repository: the transfer across the symmetric middle interval is universally anti-diagonal. Its top-left entry vanishes for every reflected pair, before the distinguished Apéry solution is inserted.

## 4. Smallest denominator clearing and primitive numerator

Let

\[
 Q=Q_{m,M}=\frac{M!}{m!},
 \qquad
 N=N_h(m).
\]

From (V.1),

\[
 D_{r,s}=\varepsilon\frac{6N}{Q^3}.
\]

Let

\[
 g=\gcd(Q^3,6N).
\]

Then

\[
 \gcd\left(\frac{6N}{g},\frac{Q^3}{g}\right)=1,
\]

so the reduced fraction is

\[
 D_{r,s}
 =\frac{\varepsilon\,6N/g}{Q^3/g}.
 \tag{4.1}
\]

Consequently

\[
 \boxed{
 q_{m,M}:=\frac{Q^3}{\gcd(Q^3,6N_h(m))}
 }
 \tag{4.2}
\]

is the **least positive integer** such that \(q_{m,M}D_{r,s}\in\mathbb Z\). Any denominator clearing is a multiple of \(q_{m,M}\).

At a reflected pair, \(N=pK_h(m)\) and \(p>M\), so \(p\nmid Q\). Therefore

\[
 \gcd(Q^3,6pK_h(m))=\gcd(Q^3,6K_h(m)),
 \tag{4.3}
\]

and

\[
 \boxed{
 D_{r,s}
 =\frac{\varepsilon\,p\,6K_h(m)/g}
        {Q^3/g},
 \qquad
 g=\gcd(Q^3,6K_h(m)).
 }
 \tag{4.4}
\]

This proves two points exactly:

1. \(p\) survives in the reduced numerator, because the reduced denominator is a \(p\)-unit.
2. The only possible additional target information would be
   
   \[
   p\mid K_h(m).
   \tag{4.5}
   \]
   
   Reflection and \(p\mid b_r\) do not force (4.5).

The standard Apéry denominator theorem gives another, coarser clearing. If

\[
 \ell_M=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,M),
\]

then \(\ell_M^3a_j\in\mathbb Z\) for \(j\le M\), hence

\[
 q_{m,M}\mid \ell_M^3.
 \tag{4.6}
\]

Combining (4.2) and (4.6), the exact denominator divides both the factorial-product clearing \(Q^3\) and the global lcm clearing \(\ell_M^3\). Neither statement changes the primitive factorization (4.4).

### 4.1 Exact criterion for an interval-lcm clearing

Let

\[
 L_{m,M}=\operatorname{lcm}(m+1,m+2,\ldots,M).
\]

Since \(L_{m,M}\mid Q\), equation (2.4) gives the exact equivalence

\[
 \boxed{
 L_{m,M}^3D_{r,s}\in\mathbb Z
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \left(\frac{Q}{L_{m,M}}\right)^3
 \mid 6N_h(m).
 }
 \tag{4.7}
\]

Thus replacing \(Q^3\) by the interval lcm is not a formal cancellation: it is precisely the additional divisibility assertion in the right side of (4.7). The path-matching expansion does not supply that divisibility term by term. The exact minimal clearing remains (4.2), which already incorporates every genuine cancellation.

Even if one grants (4.7) for a chosen family of intervals, it does not improve selectivity: \(p\nmid Q/L_{m,M}\), so the factor \(p\) in \(N_h(m)\) remains the same universal factor.

## 5. The matching expansion explains, rather than removes, the factor

Let \(J_{h-1}(x)\) be the tridiagonal matrix indexed by \(j=1,\ldots,h-1\), with

\[
 J_{jj}=P(x+j),
\]

and

\[
 J_{j,j+1}=J_{j+1,j}=-(x+j+1)^3.
\]

Then

\[
 \boxed{N_h(x)=\det J_{h-1}(x).}
 \tag{5.1}
\]

Expanding the determinant over matchings of the path on \(1,\ldots,h-1\) gives

\[
 \boxed{
 N_h(x)
 =\sum_{\mathcal M}(-1)^{|\mathcal M|}
   \prod_{(j,j+1)\in\mathcal M}(x+j+1)^6
   \prod_{j\notin V(\mathcal M)}P(x+j).
 }
 \tag{5.2}
\]

Reverse the path by \(j\mapsto h-j\), and simultaneously send \(x\mapsto-x-h-1\). The edge weights are preserved up to the harmless even sixth power, while every unmatched vertex factor changes sign by \(P(-1-t)=-P(t)\). This is exactly the involution behind (3.2).

For even \(h\), paired matching weights cancel at the center

\[
 x=-\frac{h+1}{2},
\]

and the cancellation produces the factor \(2x+h+1\). At the integer reflected evaluation \(x=m\), that factor is \(p\). Thus the matching model identifies the \(p\)-factor as a geometric symmetry factor of the path determinant. It does not attach the factor to the condition \(p\mid b_m\).

### 5.1 Why a different matching of endpoints does not help

Suppose \(p\mid b_u\), with \(u+1<p\). From (1.2),

\[
 a_ub_{u+1}\equiv-\frac6{(u+1)^3}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
 \tag{5.3}
\]

Hence both \(a_u\) and \(b_{u+1}\) are \(p\)-units. If \(v<p\) is not another zero of \(b\) modulo \(p\), then

\[
 a_vb_u-a_ub_v\equiv-a_ub_v\not\equiv0\pmod p.
 \tag{5.4}
\]

Therefore one cannot pair a target index for \(p\) with an arbitrary target index belonging to another prime and retain either divisibility. A Casoratian edge carries \(p\) only when both endpoints are zero in the **same characteristic**. The guaranteed second endpoint is the reflected one, and that edge has just been shown to be universally divisible by \(p\).

So the matching alternatives are exhaustive:

* pair \(r\) with its own reflection \(s\): obtain the universal factor \(p\);
* pair indices belonging to different characteristics: no forced divisibility;
* use an additional same-\(p\) zero beyond reflection: this is new zero-fibre information, not supplied by the proposed construction.

## 6. There is no hidden second \(p\)-factor

The quotient \(K_h(m)=N_h(m)/p\) need not vanish modulo \(p\) on a target.

### 6.1 Small symbolic example

Take

\[
 n=6,\qquad p=5,\qquad r=1,\qquad s=3.
\]

Here \(b_1=5\), so \(p\mid b_r\). We have \(m=1,h=2\), and

\[
 N_2(x)=P(x+1)
 =(2x+3)(17x^2+51x+39).
\]

Thus

\[
 N_2(1)=5\cdot107=535,
\]

and

\[
 D_{1,3}
 =\frac{6\cdot535}{(2\cdot3)^3}
 =\frac{535}{36}.
\]

The reduced numerator is \(535=5\cdot107\), with \(5\nmid107\).

### 6.2 Noncentral \(p=17\) target

This is not a \(p=5\) artefact. Take

\[
 n=20,\qquad p=17,\qquad r=3,\qquad s=13.
\]

The lower value is especially divisible:

\[
 b_3=1445=5\cdot17^2.
\]

Here \(m=3,h=10\). Applying (V.2) modulo \(17^2=289\) gives

```text
N_0(3),...,N_10(3)
 = 0, 1, 215, 110, 58, 11, 6, 186, 130, 287, 34   (mod 289).
```

Therefore

\[
 N_{10}(3)\equiv34=2\cdot17\pmod{17^2},
\]

so

\[
 \boxed{
 K_{10}(3)=\frac{N_{10}(3)}{17}\equiv2\pmod{17}.
 }
 \tag{6.1}
\]

Because the denominator in (4.4) is a \(17\)-unit,

\[
 v_{17}\!\left(\operatorname{num}(D_{3,13})\right)=1.
\]

Thus even \(17^2\mid b_3\) does not create a second factor \(17\) in the reflected Casoratian numerator. The target condition contributes no residual divisibility after the ambient factor is removed.

## 7. Grouping by the gap \(h=|n-1-3r|\)

For fixed \(n\), write

\[
 h_p=|s-r|=|n-1-3r|=|3p-(2n+1)|.
 \tag{7.1}
\]

For a prescribed \(h\), the only possible primes are

\[
 \boxed{
 p_\pm(h)=\frac{2n+1\pm h}{3}.
 }
 \tag{7.2}
\]

Hence each gap group contains at most two candidates. There is no large multiplicity to compress.

Let

\[
 \mathcal P_n^*
 =\left\{p:\frac n2<p\le n,\ p\text{ odd prime},\ 3p\ne2n+1\right\}.
\]

For each \(p\in\mathcal P_n^*\), let \(
u_p\) be the primitive numerator in (4.4). Then

\[
 p\mid\nu_p
\]

for every \(p\in\mathcal P_n^*\), independently of target status. Since these primes are distinct,

\[
 \operatorname{lcm}_{p\in\mathcal P_n^*}|\nu_p|
 \quad\text{is divisible by}\quad
 \prod_{p\in\mathcal P_n^*}p.
 \tag{7.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \log\operatorname{lcm}_{p\in\mathcal P_n^*}|\nu_p|
 &\ge\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_n^*}\log p\\
 &=\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(n/2)-O(\log n)\\
 &=\frac n2+o(n).
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{7.4}
\]

The \(O(\log n)\) removes the possible central prime and the finite small-prime exceptions.

Grouping by \(h\) merely rewrites the same identity. If

\[
 \mathcal P_n(h)=\{p\in\mathcal P_n^*:h_p=h\},
\]

then

\[
 |\mathcal P_n(h)|\le2
\]

and

\[
 \sum_h\log\prod_{p\in\mathcal P_n(h)}p
 =\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_n^*}\log p
 =\frac n2+o(n).
 \tag{7.5}
\]

An lcm within each two-element group can save at most a constant-factor duplication of unrelated cofactors. It cannot remove either distinct prime \(p_+(h)\) or \(p_-(h)\).

### 7.1 What happens if one groups only actual targets

Let

\[
 T_n=\left\{p:\frac n2<p\le n,\ p\mid b_{n-p}\right\}
\]

and let

\[
 R_n^{\mathrm{top}}=\prod_{p\in T_n}p.
\]

For noncentral targets, (4.4) says

\[
 \nu_p=p\mu_p,
 \qquad
 \mu_p=\varepsilon\frac{6K_{h_p}(m_p)}{g_{m_p,h_p}}.
 \tag{7.6}
\]

No divisibility \(p\mid\mu_p\) follows. Consequently the reflection–Casoratian gives only

\[
 \boxed{
 R_n^{\mathrm{top}}
 \mid\operatorname{lcm}_{p\in T_n}|\nu_p|,
 }
 \tag{7.7}
\]

while the reverse height inequality is tautological:

\[
 \log\operatorname{lcm}_{p\in T_n}|\nu_p|
 \ge\log R_n^{\mathrm{top}}.
 \tag{7.8}
\]

Using only target groups therefore assumes the unknown support set and constructs an integer whose unavoidable height is already the quantity to be bounded. Using all groups avoids target dependence but incurs the linear ambient cost (7.4).

This is the exact saturation dichotomy.

## 8. Common denominators and nested intervals do not combine numerator divisibility

For a candidate \(r\), the interval between the reflected indices is

\[
 I_r=(m,M].
\]

The two branches are

\[
 r\le\frac{n-1}{3}:
 \quad
 I_r=(r,n-1-2r],
 \quad
 h=n-1-3r,
 \tag{8.1}
\]

and

\[
 r\ge\frac{n-1}{3}:
 \quad
 I_r=(n-1-2r,r],
 \quad
 h=3r-n+1.
 \tag{8.2}
\]

Within each branch these intervals are nested. This makes it tempting to clear all denominators by one lcm associated with the largest interval.

That observation concerns only denominators. After a common clearing \(C\), one obtains a list of distinct integers

\[
 C D_{r_p,s_p},\qquad p\in T_n.
\]

The fact that \(p\) divides its own integer does not imply that \(p\) divides any of the other integers, their sum, or their gcd. A common denominator does not turn coordinatewise divisibility into simultaneous divisibility of one small integer.

The only automatic one-integer constructions are:

* the product, whose logarithmic height adds all coordinate heights;
* the lcm, whose logarithmic height is at least the target radical by (7.8);
* a CRT-weighted sum, whose coefficients must carry complementary prime products unless a new cross-characteristic relation is supplied.

For the last point, write \(P=\prod_i p_i\). If the only known congruence is \(p_i\mid A_i\), then a sum

\[
 E=\sum_i c_iA_i
\]

is guaranteed to vanish modulo every \(p_j\) only if the off-diagonal terms are killed modulo \(p_j\). In the absence of additional relations this requires

\[
 \frac{P}{p_i}\mid c_i.
\]

Then \(P\mid E\), so every nonzero such \(E\) has

\[
 \log|E|\ge\log P.
\]

This is the same complementary-cofactor saturation already encountered in the multi-target determinant routes. Reflection provides no cross-characteristic relation that would lower these coefficients.

## 9. Quantitative denominator and Archimedean height audit

There are three different denominator scales; none gives an \(o(n)\) global carrier.

### 9.1 Raw factorial-product clearing

The exact continuant clearing is

\[
 Q^3=\left(\frac{M!}{m!}\right)^3.
\]

Its logarithm is

\[
 \log Q^3
 =3\bigl(\log M!-\log m!\bigr).
 \tag{9.1}
\]

If \(m,M\asymp n\) and \(h=M-m\asymp n\), Stirling's formula gives

\[
 \log Q^3=\Theta(n\log n).
\]

This clearing is much too large.

### 9.2 Global lcm clearing

The standard companion integrality uses

\[
 \ell_M^3,
 \qquad
 \ell_M=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,M).
\]

The prime number theorem gives

\[
 \log\ell_M^3=3\psi(M)=3M+o(M).
 \tag{9.2}
\]

This improves \(n\log n\) to linear height, but not to \(o(n)\).

### 9.3 Interval lcm clearing

For

\[
 L_{m,M}=\operatorname{lcm}(m+1,\ldots,M),
\]

every prime \(q\in(m,M]\) divides \(L_{m,M}\). Therefore

\[
 \log L_{m,M}
 \ge\vartheta(M)-\vartheta(m).
 \tag{9.3}
\]

For a macroscopic gap \(h=M-m\ge\delta n\),

\[
 \boxed{
 \log L_{m,M}\ge h+o(n)\ge\delta n+o(n).
 }
 \tag{9.4}
\]

Thus even the optimistic interval-lcm multiplier has linear logarithmic height on a long reflected interval. If (4.7) holds and one forms the cleared positive integer

\[
 C_{m,M}=L_{m,M}^3D_{m,M},
\]

then its multiplier alone contributes at least \(3h+o(n)\).

The exact primitive denominator (4.2) can be smaller because of the gcd with \(6N_h(m)\). There is no justification for replacing (4.2) by a lower bound of order \(L_{m,M}^3\) in every individual case. That possible cancellation does not rescue the route, because the primitive numerator itself is already tall for a long gap.

### 9.4 The primitive numerator is linearly tall for a long gap

Let

\[
 \lambda=17+12\sqrt2.
\]

The standard Apéry asymptotic is

\[
 b_j\asymp\lambda^j(j+1)^{-3/2}.
 \tag{9.5}
\]

From the first term of the positive telescoping sum (1.5),

\[
 D_{m,M}
 \ge\frac{6b_M}{(m+1)^3b_{m+1}}.
 \tag{9.6}
\]

Using (9.5),

\[
 \log D_{m,M}
 \ge h\log\lambda-O(\log n).
 \tag{9.7}
\]

Conversely, the ratio of the \(j\)-th summand in (1.5) to the first decays geometrically like \(\lambda^{-2(j-m)}\), up to polynomial factors. Hence

\[
 \boxed{
 \log D_{m,M}=h\log\lambda+O(\log n).
 }
 \tag{9.8}
\]

Let \(q_{m,M}\) be the exact denominator (4.2) and \(
u_{m,M}\) the primitive numerator. Since \(D_{m,M}>0\),

\[
 |\nu_{m,M}|=q_{m,M}D_{m,M}\ge D_{m,M}.
\]

Therefore

\[
 \boxed{
 \log|\nu_{m,M}|
 \ge h\log\lambda-O(\log n).
 }
 \tag{9.9}
\]

For \(h\ge\delta n\), one reflected primitive numerator already has positive linear logarithmic height, even under the impossible best-case assumption \(q_{m,M}=1\). Thus denominator cancellation cannot turn a long-gap reflected Casoratian into a subexponential-height carrier.

For short gaps, the location of the primes already gives the only available saving. If \(h_p\le H\), then by (7.2) all such primes lie in an interval of length \(2H/3+O(1)\), so the elementary bound is

\[
 \sum_{\substack{n/2<p\le n\\h_p\le H}}\log p
 \le\left(\frac{2H}{3}+2\right)\log n.
 \tag{9.10}
\]

A Brun–Titchmarsh estimate improves this to \(O(H\log n/\log(2H))\). Hence a genuinely sublinear maximum gap would already make the corresponding ambient prime band harmless. The unresolved situation is a broad collection of gaps, and the reflection factor supplies no restriction on which of those ambient primes are targets.

## 10. Final no-go theorem for the proposed Route A

Let \(T_n^*=T_n\setminus\{(2n+1)/3\}\), omitting the possible central target, and for \(p\in T_n^*\) let \(
u_p\) be the reduced numerator of \(D_{r,s}\).

Then the exact reflection–Casoratian theorem is

\[
 \boxed{
 \nu_p=p\mu_p,
 \qquad
 p\nmid\operatorname{den}(D_{r,s}),
 }
 \tag{10.1}
\]

where the factor \(p\) occurs for every top-half candidate and where target badness does not imply \(p\mid\mu_p\).

Consequently:

1. **Target-adaptive product/lcm.**
   
   \[
   R_n^{\mathrm{top},*}
   \mid\operatorname{lcm}_{p\in T_n^*}|\nu_p|,
   \]
   
   but the right side has logarithm at least \(\log R_n^{\mathrm{top},*}\). This is tautological.

2. **Target-independent product/lcm.** Taking all top-half candidates gives
   
   \[
   \log\operatorname{lcm}_{p\in\mathcal P_n^*}|\nu_p|
   \ge\frac n2+o(n).
   \]
   
   Thus the universal carrier has linear height.

3. **Gap grouping.** Each \(h\) contains at most two candidates, and summing the forced factors over all \(h\) recovers the same top-half primorial. No asymptotic compression occurs.

4. **Matching.** Cross-prime endpoint matchings do not preserve divisibility; the only guaranteed same-prime edge is reflection, whose factor is universal.

5. **Denominator sharing.** A common interval or global lcm clears several rational coordinates but does not produce one integer divisible by all their distinct primes. Product, lcm, or CRT recombination restores at least the full target product.

6. **Saturation.** Dividing \(N_h(m)\) by the forced factor \(p=2m+h+1\) leaves \(K_h(m)\), which can be a \(p\)-unit at an actual target, even when \(p^2\mid b_m\).

Therefore no inequality of the form

\[
 \log R_n^{\mathrm{top}}=o(n)
\]

follows from the reflection–Casoratian, its path-matching expansion, or denominator lcm grouping. The obstruction is not merely that a standard Casoratian is “too large.” The exact algebra shows that its entire guaranteed \(p\)-divisibility is the ambient reflection factor.

A successful reopening would need genuinely new information after saturation, for example a theorem forcing \(p\mid K_{h_p}(m_p)\) on a sufficiently large target subfamily, or a cross-characteristic relation coupling several \(\mu_p\)'s into one nonzero subexponential-height integer. Neither is supplied by the current Route-A identities, and the explicit target examples above rule out any universal second-factor statement.
