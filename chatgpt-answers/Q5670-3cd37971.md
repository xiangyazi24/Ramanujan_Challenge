# Strict normalization audit for the Apéry finite \({}_4F_3\) Gauss sum

## Verdict

Let \(q\) be an odd prime, let \(\omega\) be a generator of \(\widehat{\mathbf F_q^\times}\), and put \(A=\omega^M\), where \(0<M<q-1\). With the standard convention

\[
g_q(k)=\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_q}\omega^k(x)\,\zeta_q^x,
\qquad g_q(0)=g_q(\varepsilon)=-1,
\]

the displayed expression

\[
T(q,M)=
-\frac{1}{(q-1)g_q(-M)^2g_q(M)^2}
\sum_{\nu=0}^{q-2}
 g_q(\nu-M)^2g_q(\nu+M)^2g_q(-\nu)^4
\tag{V.1}
\]

is **exactly** McCarthy's starred finite-field hypergeometric function

\[
\boxed{
T(q,M)=
{}_4F_3\left(
\begin{matrix}A,A,\bar A,\bar A\\
\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon
\end{matrix}\biggm|1
\right)^{\star}_{q}.}
\tag{V.2}
\]

There is no missing factor of \(q\), \(q-1\), or a sign, and no exceptional-\(\nu\) correction is to be added.

At the prime compatible with the chosen Teichmüller character, \(T(q,M)\) is integral and

\[
\boxed{T(q,M)\equiv b_M\pmod{\mathfrak p_\omega}.}
\tag{V.3}
\]

Since \(0<M<q-1\), the residue index requested in the question is simply \(r=M\). Thus the claimed reduction is **true**.

Two terminology/normalization corrections are essential:

1. The function in (V.2) is well-poised in McCarthy's sense **if and only if**
   \(A^2=\varepsilon\), hence, under \(0<M<q-1\), if and only if
   \(M=(q-1)/2\). For general \(M\), it is a finite \({}_4F_3^\star\), but it is not well-poised.
2. If \({}_4F_3^G\) denotes Greene's normalization, then
   \[
   \boxed{T(q,M)=-q^3\,{}_4F_3^G\left(
   \begin{matrix}A,A,\bar A,\bar A\\
   \varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon
   \end{matrix}\biggm|1\right).}
   \tag{V.4}
   \]
   Therefore (V.1) must not be called the unscaled Greene value.

The proof below tracks the trivial-character terms, the cyclotomic field, the Teichmüller prime, and every valuation and sign.

## 2. McCarthy's exact definition

We use the conventions of Dermot McCarthy, *Transformations of Well-Poised Hypergeometric Functions over Finite Fields*, Finite Fields Appl. **18** (2012), 1133–1147:

- multiplicative characters, including \(\varepsilon\), are extended to zero by \(\chi(0)=0\);
- \(g(\chi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_q}\chi(x)\theta(x)\), for one nontrivial additive character \(\theta\);
- consequently \(g(\varepsilon)=-1\).

McCarthy's Definition 1.4 is

\[
{}_{n+1}F_n\left(
\begin{matrix}A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_n\\
B_1,\ldots,B_n
\end{matrix}\biggm|x\right)^\star_q
=\frac1{q-1}\sum_\chi
\prod_{i=0}^n\frac{g(A_i\chi)}{g(A_i)}
\prod_{j=1}^n\frac{g(\overline{B_j\chi})}{g(\overline{B_j})}
 g(\bar\chi)\chi(-1)^{n+1}\chi(x).
\tag{2.1}
\]

Take

\[
(A_0,A_1,A_2,A_3)=(A,A,\bar A,\bar A),
\qquad
(B_1,B_2,B_3)=(\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon),
\qquad x=1.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Write \(\chi=\omega^\nu\). The three lower-character ratios give

\[
\prod_{j=1}^{3}
\frac{g(\bar\chi)}{g(\varepsilon)}
=\frac{g(-\nu)^3}{(-1)^3}
=-g(-\nu)^3.
\tag{2.3}
\]

The extra \(g(\bar\chi)\) supplies the fourth power. Also

\[
\chi(-1)^{4}=1,\qquad \chi(1)=1.
\tag{2.4}
\]

Substitution in (2.1) gives exactly (V.1). In particular, the minus sign is not optional: it is the product of the three factors \(g(\varepsilon)^{-1}=-1\).

### 2.1 Independence of the additive character

Replace \(\theta(x)\) by \(\theta(cx)\), \(c\in\mathbf F_q^\times\). Then

\[
g_c(k)=\omega^{-k}(c)g(k).
\tag{2.5}
\]

The exponent of \(\omega(c)\) in one numerator term of (V.1) is

\[
-2(\nu-M)-2(\nu+M)-4(-\nu)=0,
\tag{2.6}
\]

and the denominator exponent is also zero. Hence \(T(q,M)\) is independent of the chosen nontrivial additive character, as McCarthy's definition requires.

## 3. Why it is not generally well-poised

McCarthy calls (2.1) well-poised when \(x=\pm1\) and

\[
B_j=A_0\overline{A_j}
\qquad(1\le j\le n).
\tag{3.1}
\]

Choose \(A_0=A\). For the other three top characters \(A,\bar A,\bar A\), the required lower characters are

\[
\varepsilon,\ A^2,\ A^2.
\tag{3.2}
\]

They are all trivial exactly when \(A^2=\varepsilon\). The same conclusion holds if \(A_0=\bar A\). Since \(A=\omega^M\) and \(0<M<q-1\), this is equivalent to

\[
2M\equiv0\pmod{q-1}
\iff
M=\frac{q-1}{2}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

Thus the collaborator's word *well-poised* is correct only at the quadratic character.

## 4. Greene versus McCarthy normalization

McCarthy's Proposition 2.5 states that, when \(A_0\ne\varepsilon\) and \(A_i\ne B_i\) for \(1\le i\le n\),

\[
{}_{n+1}F_n^\star
=\left(\prod_{i=1}^n\binom{A_i}{B_i}^{-1}\right)
{}_{n+1}F_n^G.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Here every \(A_i\) is nontrivial and every \(B_i=\varepsilon\). Greene's finite binomial coefficient satisfies

\[
\binom{C}{\varepsilon}
=\frac1qJ(C,\varepsilon)
=-\frac1q
\qquad(C\ne\varepsilon).
\tag{4.2}
\]

Therefore

\[
\prod_{i=1}^{3}\binom{A_i}{\varepsilon}^{-1}
=(-q)^3=-q^3,
\tag{4.3}
\]

which proves (V.4). This is also consistent with McCarthy's Theorem 1.12 in the quadratic case: his starred value equals the modular coefficient \(\gamma(q)+q\), while the Greene formulation carries the extra factor \(-q^3\).

## 5. The field of definition and the Teichmüller prime

Fix a primitive root \(c\) of \(\mathbf F_q^\times\), choose complex roots of unity so that

\[
\omega(c)=\zeta_{q-1},
\qquad
\theta(x)=\zeta_q^x,
\tag{5.1}
\]

and put

\[
L=\mathbf Q(\zeta_q,\zeta_{q-1}),
\qquad
K=\mathbf Q(\zeta_{q-1}).
\tag{5.2}
\]

Every Gauss sum belongs to \(\mathcal O_L\), so (V.1) initially belongs to \(L\). If an automorphism over \(K\) sends \(\zeta_q\) to \(\zeta_q^a\), then

\[
g_q(k)\longmapsto\omega^{-k}(a)g_q(k).
\tag{5.3}
\]

The cancellation (2.6) shows that every summand and the denominator are fixed. Hence

\[
\boxed{T(q,M)\in K.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

Complex conjugation, followed by the reindexing \(\nu\mapsto-\nu\), fixes the expression, so in fact

\[
T(q,M)\in K^+.
\tag{5.5}
\]

The Teichmüller-compatible prime of \(K\) is

\[
\mathfrak p_c=(q,\zeta_{q-1}-c),
\qquad
\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak p_c\cong\mathbf F_q.
\tag{5.6}
\]

It is characterized by

\[
\omega(x)\equiv x\pmod{\mathfrak p_c}
\qquad(x\in\mathbf F_q^\times).
\tag{5.7}
\]

Let \(\mathfrak P\) be the prime of \(L\) above \(\mathfrak p_c\) for which \(\zeta_q\equiv1\). Reduction "modulo the Teichmüller prime" means reduction in

\[
\mathcal O_{K,\mathfrak p_c}\longrightarrow\mathbf F_q,
\tag{5.8}
\]

or equivalently after extension to \(\mathcal O_{L,\mathfrak P}\).

The raw expression gives

\[
T(q,M)\in \frac1{(q-1)q^2}\mathcal O_K.
\tag{5.9}
\]

Section 7 proves the stronger local statement

\[
\boxed{T(q,M)\in\mathcal O_{K,\mathfrak p_c}.}
\tag{5.10}
\]

An arbitrary prime above \(q\) is not interchangeable with (5.6): the compatibility \(\omega(x)\equiv x\) is part of the assertion. Replacing \(\omega\) by its inverse does not alter the final result, because the expression is invariant under \(M\mapsto q-1-M\) and \(b_M\equiv b_{q-1-M}\pmod q\).

## 6. Denominator and all exceptional characters

McCarthy's Gauss-product identity, his equation (3.3), is

\[
g(\chi)g(\bar\chi)=
\begin{cases}
\chi(-1)q,&\chi\ne\varepsilon,\
1,&\chi=\varepsilon.
\end{cases}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Since \(0<M<q-1\), \(A\) is nontrivial, and therefore

\[
g_q(M)^2g_q(-M)^2
=\bigl(g_q(M)g_q(-M)\bigr)^2
=q^2.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Thus

\[
T(q,M)=-\frac1{(q-1)q^2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{q-2}
 g_q(\nu-M)^2g_q(\nu+M)^2g_q(-\nu)^4.
\tag{6.3}
\]

The potentially exceptional summation indices are:

\[
\nu=0,\qquad \nu=M,\qquad \nu=q-1-M.
\tag{6.4}
\]

At these indices, respectively, \(g_q(-\nu)\), \(g_q(\nu-M)\), or \(g_q(\nu+M)\) is \(g_q(0)=-1\). No term is deleted and no \(q\)-correction is inserted. The Gross–Koblitz formula below includes the trivial character as the case of exponent zero and gives exactly \(-1\).

At the quadratic value \(M=(q-1)/2\), the index \(\nu=M\) makes both shifted top characters trivial. It is still handled by the same formula without an exception.

## 7. Gross–Koblitz/Stickelberger reduction

We use Gross and Koblitz, *Gauss sums and the \(p\)-adic \(\Gamma\)-function*, Ann. of Math. **109** (1979), 569–581, Theorem 1.7.

Choose \(\pi\) in the completion at \(\mathfrak P\) so that

\[
\pi^{q-1}=-q
\tag{7.1}
\]

and the associated primitive \(q\)-th root is the chosen \(\zeta_q\). For \(0\le a\le q-2\), Gross–Koblitz gives

\[
\boxed{
g_q(-a)=-\pi^a\Gamma_q\left(\frac{a}{q-1}\right).}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Because

\[
\frac{a}{q-1}\equiv-a\pmod q
\tag{7.3}
\]

and Morita's \(q\)-adic gamma function is continuous modulo \(q\), its elementary recurrence gives

\[
\Gamma_q\left(\frac a{q-1}\right)
\equiv\Gamma_q(-a)=\frac1{a!}\pmod q.
\tag{7.4}
\]

For \(a=0\), equations (7.2)–(7.4) read \(g_q(0)=-1\), exactly as required.

### 7.1 Valuation of each \(\nu\)-term

For \(0\le\nu\le q-2\), let \(\langle t\rangle\) denote the representative of \(t\mod q-1\) in \(\{0,\ldots,q-2\}\), and put

\[
a_\nu=\langle M-\nu\rangle,
\qquad
c_\nu=\langle -M-\nu\rangle.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Then

\[
g_q(\nu-M)=g_q(-a_\nu),
\qquad
g_q(\nu+M)=g_q(-c_\nu),
\qquad
g_q(-\nu)=g_q(-\nu).
\tag{7.6}
\]

The \(\pi\)-adic order of the numerator term is

\[
e_\nu=2a_\nu+2c_\nu+4\nu.
\tag{7.7}
\]

Set

\[
m=\min(M,q-1-M),
\qquad
M_+=\max(M,q-1-M).
\tag{7.8}
\]

A direct evaluation of the least residues gives the complete ledger

\[
\boxed{
e_\nu=
\begin{cases}
2(q-1),&0\le\nu\le m,\
4(q-1),&m<\nu\le M_+,\
6(q-1),&M_+<\nu\le q-2.
\end{cases}}
\tag{7.9}
\]

When \(M=(q-1)/2\), the middle interval is empty. Formula (7.9) includes every exceptional index in (6.4), because an exponent zero in (7.2) is legitimate.

Since \(q^2=\pi^{2(q-1)}\), after division by the denominator \(q^2\), only the terms

\[
0\le\nu\le m
\tag{7.10}
\]

survive modulo \(\mathfrak P\). All other terms contain at least one further positive power of \(\pi\). This proves the local integrality (5.10).

### 7.2 Leading coefficient of a surviving term

For \(0\le\nu\le m\), no wrap occurs in the two relevant residues:

\[
a_\nu=M-\nu,
\qquad
c_\nu=q-1-M-\nu.
\tag{7.11}
\]

Using (7.1), (7.2), and (7.4), and noting that all signs disappear under the even powers, gives

\[
\frac{g_q(\nu-M)^2g_q(\nu+M)^2g_q(-\nu)^4}{q^2}
\equiv
\frac{1}{(M-\nu)!^2(q-1-M-\nu)!^2(\nu!)^4}
\pmod{\mathfrak P}.
\tag{7.12}
\]

There is no residual sign from \(\pi^{2(q-1)}/q^2\), because it is exactly \(1\).

Finally,

\[
-\frac1{q-1}\equiv1\pmod q.
\tag{7.13}
\]

Combining (6.3), (7.10), (7.12), and (7.13),

\[
T(q,M)\equiv
\sum_{\nu=0}^{m}
\frac{1}{(M-\nu)!^2(q-1-M-\nu)!^2(\nu!)^4}
\pmod{\mathfrak p_c}.
\tag{7.14}
\]

## 8. Identification with the Apéry number

Wilson's theorem gives, whenever \(0\le M+\nu\le q-1\),

\[
\frac1{(q-1-M-\nu)!}
\equiv(-1)^{M+\nu+1}(M+\nu)!\pmod q.
\tag{8.1}
\]

The sign disappears after squaring, so each summand in (7.14) equals

\[
\frac{(M+\nu)!^2}{(M-\nu)!^2(\nu!)^4}
=\binom M\nu^2\binom{M+\nu}{\nu}^2.
\tag{8.2}
\]

If \(M\le(q-1)/2\), then \(m=M\), so (7.14) is the complete Apéry sum.

If \(M>(q-1)/2\), then \(m=q-1-M\). For every \(\nu\) with

\[
m<\nu\le M,
\tag{8.3}
\]

one has \(q\le M+\nu<2q\), so \(\binom{M+\nu}{\nu}\) is divisible by \(q\). Thus all omitted Apéry summands vanish modulo \(q\).

Therefore in both ranges

\[
\begin{aligned}
T(q,M)
&\equiv\sum_{\nu=0}^{M}
\binom M\nu^2\binom{M+\nu}{\nu}^2\
&=b_M
\pmod{\mathfrak p_c}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.4}
\]

This proves (V.3). It also proves the familiar reflection congruence \(b_M\equiv b_{q-1-M}\pmod q\) directly from the same finite sum.

## 9. Exact small-prime verification

The following checks use only the exact reduction (7.14) in \(\mathbf F_q\), together with the exact Apéry recurrence

\[
(n+1)^3b_{n+1}=P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1},
\qquad b_0=1,\ b_1=5.
\tag{9.1}
\]

No complex or floating-point Gauss sums occur.

For example:

```text
q=7, M=2:
  surviving Apéry terms mod 7 = [1, 36, 36] = [1,1,1]
  sum = 3

q=11, M=5:
  surviving terms mod 11 = [1,9,1,1,9,1]
  sum = 22 = 0

q=13, M=3:
  surviving terms mod 13 = [1,144,900,400] = [1,1,3,10]
  sum = 15 = 2

q=17, M=3:
  surviving terms mod 17 = [1,144,900,400] = [1,8,16,9]
  sum = 34 = 0
```

The full first-block residue sequences obtained independently from (7.14) and (9.1) are:

```text

[1, 5, 3, 3, 3, 5, 1]

q = 17, M = 0,...,16:
```

## Final yes/no answer

- **Is the displayed Gauss-sum expression correctly normalized?** **Yes**, as McCarthy's starred \({}_4F_3\).
- **Does it reduce to \(b_M\) at the compatible Teichmüller prime?** **Yes**, exactly as in (8.4).
- **Is it a general well-poised \({}_4F_3\)?** **No**; only when \(M=(q-1)/2\).
- **Is it Greene's unscaled \({}_4F_3\)?** **No**; it is \(-q^3\) times Greene's value.
