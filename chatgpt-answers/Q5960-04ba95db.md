# 10. Applicable theorems—and their exact limitations here

## Grothendieck algebraic de Rham finiteness

For a smooth algebraic variety, algebraic de Rham cohomology is finite-dimensional. Applied to (U=(mathbb G_m)^3setminus{F=0}), this proves that the meromorphic complex for (D_M) has finite-dimensional cohomology. Since the integer twist is gauge-equivalent to the ordinary complex on (U), no nonresonance hypothesis is needed for finiteness.

This theorem does not identify the cohomology with (R/K), does not prove the projected rank is two, and gives no small denominator bound.

Reference: A. Grothendieck, On the de Rham cohomology of algebraic varieties, Publ. Math. IHÉS 29 (1966), DOI 10.1007/BF02684807.

## Oaku–Takayama holonomic algorithm

There is a general Gröbner-basis algorithm for the de Rham cohomology of complements with rank-one local systems. It applies after embedding the torus complement as the affine complement of (xyzF=0), computing the localization (D)-module, and integrating it. This gives a theorem-level finite algorithm even when the divisor is singular and reducible.

It is the reliable fallback if the hand-built Koszul truncation does not stabilize transparently. It may be computationally expensive, and by itself it does not give the required arithmetic height bound; the fraction-free denominator audit remains necessary.

Reference: T. Oaku and N. Takayama, An algorithm for de Rham cohomology groups of the complement of an affine variety via D-module computation, arXiv:math/9801114.

## Griffiths–Dwork reduction

Classical Griffiths reduction applies cleanly to rational forms with poles on a smooth projective hypersurface, with a finite Jacobian ring. Its elementary reduction formula is the ancestor of (1).

It does not apply directly to the present raw divisor because (F=0) is reducible and the logarithmic critical ideal has four positive-dimensional components. One may use a generalized Griffiths–Dwork reduction only after either:

- passing to a toric/log resolution and including all boundary terms; or

- proving that the projectors define a derived tame direct summand with finite critical scheme.

Reference: P. Griffiths, On the periods of certain rational integrals I, II, Ann. of Math. 90 (1969), Part I, Part II.

## Brieskorn lattices for tame Laurent polynomials

For a convenient, Newton-nondegenerate Laurent polynomial with isolated critical points, the Gauss–Manin system and Brieskorn lattice are finite and have rank equal to the relevant Milnor/Newton number. In such a setting, a length-two critical scheme naturally produces a rank-two module.

The hypotheses fail globally here because of the four critical curves, and possibly at toric infinity. The theorem becomes applicable only to a proved projected direct summand after the curve and face modules are null-homotopic. Ideal-theoretic saturation alone is not enough.

Reference: A. Douai and C. Sabbah, Gauss-Manin systems, Brieskorn lattices and Frobenius structures (I), Ann. Inst. Fourier 53 (2003), DOI 10.5802/aif.1974.

## Logarithmic comparison

A logarithmic de Rham complex computes the meromorphic complement under hypotheses such as normal crossings, or for important classes of free divisors satisfying Jacobian linear-type/local quasi-homogeneity conditions. No such freeness or linear-type certificate has been established for the present reducible (F).

A log resolution always provides a normal-crossing model and a finite logarithmic complex, but the pushforward and residue bookkeeping must be retained. The four curve strata cannot simply be deleted from the raw complex.

References: F. J. Calderón-Moreno and L. Narváez-Macarro, On the logarithmic comparison theorem for integrable logarithmic connections, arXiv:math/0603003; L. Narváez-Macarro, Linearity conditions on the Jacobian ideal and logarithmic–meromorphic comparison for free divisors, arXiv:0804.2219.

## Dwork cohomology and algebraic (D)-modules

The (D)-module comparison theorems explain why a finite algebraic reduction exists beyond the smooth Griffiths setting. They do not turn a commutative ideal certificate into a de Rham certificate without computing the differential corrections.

Reference: A. Dimca, F. Maaref, C. Sabbah, and M. Saito, Dwork cohomology and algebraic D-modules, Math. Ann. 318 (2000), arXiv:math/9906078.
