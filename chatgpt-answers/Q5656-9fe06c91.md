# Hostile audit of the proposed p-adic Apéry lemma

## Executive verdict

Write

\[
P(m)=(2m+1)(17m^2+17m+5)
\]

and let

\[
(m+1)^3u_{m+1}=P(m)u_m-m^3u_{m-1}.
\]

The two claims are **true**, with the following qualifications and sharpenings.

1. For every \(n\ge 1\),
   \[
   H_n:=\gcd(A_n,b_n)\mid \gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1}),
   \qquad
   \gcd(H_n,C_n)=1.
   \]
   The second assertion is immediate from lowest terms. The first does **not** follow from the Casoratian alone until one also proves that \(2,3\nmid H_n\). The Casoratian initially gives divisibility only up to the factor \(6\). For the Apéry numbers, Gessel--Lucas at \(2\) and \(3\) gives \(2,3\nmid b_n\), so the missing small-prime step is valid.

   Prime by prime, the actual statement is stronger. If \(p^e\mid H_n\), then \(p\ge5\) and
   \[
   v_p(C_{n-1})\ge e+3v_p(n),
   \qquad
   v_p(C_{n+1})\ge e+3v_p(n+1).
   \]

2. Let \(p\ge7\), \(n=p+r\), \(0<r<p\), and suppose
   \[
   p\mid A_n,\qquad p\mid b_n.
   \]
   Then
   \[
   \boxed{v_p(b_n)=3.}
   \]
   This is an **equality**, not merely \(v_p(b_n)\ge3\). The proof proposed in the question is essentially correct, but the singular step must be written explicitly. One has
   \[
   p^3a_p\equiv6\pmod p,
   \qquad
   p^3a_{p+1}\equiv30\pmod p,
   \]
   so both \(a_p\) and \(a_{p+1}\) have valuation exactly \(-3\). The second congruence rules out the alleged possible cancellation at \(p+1\). The exclusion \(p\ge7\) is essential here: \(30\) is then a \(p\)-unit.

3. The same argument gives a substantially stronger theorem for **every** top-half target, without assuming \(p\mid A_n\). Put
   \[
   t=v_p(b_n)\ge1,
   \qquad
   \alpha=v_p(a_n).
   \]
   Then
   \[
   v_p(a_{n-1})=v_p(a_{n+1})=-3
   \]
   and
   \[
   \boxed{
   \alpha=
   \begin{cases}
   t-3,&t=1,2,\\
   \ge0,&t=3,\\
   0,&t\ge4.
   \end{cases}}
   \]
   Consequently
   \[
   \boxed{
   (v_p(C_{n-1}),v_p(C_n),v_p(C_{n+1}))
   =\bigl(3,\max(3-t,0),3\bigr).}
   \]

   Thus \(p\mid A_n\) can occur only when \(t=3\). It need not occur for every cubic target. It never occurs for a simple target, a double target, or a target of multiplicity at least four.

4. There is a new exact denominator-curvature carrier. Define
   \[
   \Delta_n=
   \frac{\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1})}
        {\gcd(C_{n-1},C_n,C_{n+1})}.
   \]
   Then, for every prime \(p\ge7\) with \(n/2<p\le n\),
   \[
   \boxed{
   v_p(\Delta_n)=
   \begin{cases}
   \min(v_p(b_n),3),&p\mid b_n,\\
   0,&p\nmid b_n.
   \end{cases}}
   \]
   In particular,
   \[
   \operatorname{rad}_{(n/2,n]}\Delta_n
   =\prod_{\substack{n/2<p\le n\\p\mid b_n}}p
   \]
   up to the irrelevant finite primes below \(7\).

   This is a proved strengthening of the proposed lemma: adjacent denominators characterize the **entire** top-half target set, not merely the much smaller intersection \(p\mid H_n\). It is nevertheless a reformulation, not a solution. The classical denominator bound gives only \(\log \Delta_n=O(n)\). Proving \(\log\operatorname{rad}_{(n/2,n]}\Delta_n=o(n)\) is exactly the original top-half problem in new coordinates.

The rest of this note proves these statements carefully and separates what is genuinely obtained from what remains open.

## 1. Exact Casoratian, including sign and index

Set

\[
W_n=a_nb_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_n
\qquad(n\ge1).
\]

At \(n=1\),

\[
W_1=a_1b_0-a_0b_1=6.
\]

Using the recurrence for both solutions,

\[
\begin{aligned}
W_{n+1}
&=a_{n+1}b_n-a_nb_{n+1}\\
&=\frac{P(n)a_n-n^3a_{n-1}}{(n+1)^3}b_n
 -a_n\frac{P(n)b_n-n^3b_{n-1}}{(n+1)^3}\\
&=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}
   (a_nb_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_n)\\
&=\frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3}W_n.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{a_nb_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_n=\frac6{n^3}.}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{a_{n+1}b_n-a_nb_{n+1}=\frac6{(n+1)^3}.}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Both formulas have positive sign. Reversing the order of the two terms changes the sign; this is the only sign convention issue.

## 2. Numerator and denominator conventions

Write

\[
a_n=\frac{A_n}{C_n},
\qquad
C_n>0,
\qquad
\gcd(A_n,C_n)=1.
\]

For every prime \(p\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
p\mid A_n&\Longleftrightarrow v_p(a_n)>0,\\
p\mid C_n&\Longleftrightarrow v_p(a_n)<0,\\
v_p(a_n)=0&\Longleftrightarrow p\nmid A_nC_n.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.1}
\]

In particular, the inference used in the proposed proof is valid:

\[
p\mid A_n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\nmid C_n.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Also

\[
\gcd(H_n,C_n)=1
\tag{2.3}
\]

is immediate because \(H_n\mid A_n\).

## 3. The small-prime repair needed for claim (1)

The Casoratian has numerator \(6\), so a proof of full divisibility by \(H_n\) must account for the primes \(2\) and \(3\).

The Apéry numbers satisfy the Gessel--Lucas congruence

\[
b_N\equiv\prod_i b_{d_i}\pmod p
\]

when \(N=\sum_i d_ip^i\). For \(p=2\),

\[
b_0\equiv b_1\equiv1\pmod2,
\]

so

\[
b_n\equiv1\pmod2.
\]

For \(p=3\),

\[
b_0\equiv1,\qquad b_1=5\equiv2,\qquad b_2=73\equiv1\pmod3,
\]

so every base-\(3\) digit factor is nonzero and

\[
3\nmid b_n.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{\gcd(b_n,6)=1,\qquad \gcd(H_n,6)=1.}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Without (3.1), the direct Casoratian argument would prove only the corresponding divisibility after deleting the possible \(2\)- and \(3\)-parts. This is the first gap in an abbreviated proof of claim (1).

## 4. Proof and sharpening of claim (1)

Let \(p^e\mid H_n\). By (3.1), \(p\ge5\). Since \(p^e\mid A_n\) and \(a_n\) is reduced,

\[
p\nmid C_n.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Clear denominators in (1.1):

\[
n^3\bigl(A_nb_{n-1}C_{n-1}-A_{n-1}b_nC_n\bigr)
=6C_nC_{n-1}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Both terms in the parenthesis are divisible by \(p^e\). Therefore

\[
e+3v_p(n)
\le v_p(6C_nC_{n-1})
=v_p(C_{n-1}),
\tag{4.3}
\]

where the last equality uses \(p\ge5\) and (4.1). Thus

\[
v_p(C_{n-1})\ge e+3v_p(n)\ge e.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Now clear denominators in (1.2):

\[
(n+1)^3
\bigl(A_{n+1}b_nC_n-A_nb_{n+1}C_{n+1}\bigr)
=6C_nC_{n+1}.
\tag{4.5}
\]

Again both terms in the parenthesis are divisible by \(p^e\), so

\[
v_p(C_{n+1})\ge e+3v_p(n+1)\ge e.
\tag{4.6}
\]

This proves

\[
\boxed{H_n\mid\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1}).}
\tag{4.7}
\]

Together with (2.3), claim (1) is proved.

A useful global repackaging is

\[
\boxed{
H_n\mid
\frac{\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1})}
     {\gcd(C_{n-1},C_n,C_{n+1})}.}
\tag{4.8}
\]

The quotient in (4.8) is exactly the positive denominator curvature introduced later.

## 5. Top-half preliminaries and the boundary cases

Let \(p\ge7\) and

\[
n=p+r,\qquad0\le r<p.
\]

Gessel--Lucas gives

\[
b_n=b_{p+r}\equiv b_1b_r=5b_r\pmod p.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Thus

\[
p\mid b_n\Longleftrightarrow p\mid b_r.
\tag{5.2}
\]

### 5.1 The cases \(r=0\) and \(r=1\)

If \(r=0\), then

\[
b_p\equiv5\pmod p,
\]

so \(p\nmid b_p\). Moreover, Section 6 below proves

\[
p^3a_p\equiv6\pmod p,
\]

so in lowest terms \(v_p(C_p)=3\) and \(p\nmid A_p\). The antecedent of claim (2) is impossible.

If \(r=1\), then

\[
b_{p+1}\equiv b_1^2=25\not\equiv0\pmod p
\]

for \(p\ge7\). Again the antecedent is impossible. Thus the \(r=1\) case of claim (2) is vacuous.

At the opposite boundary,

\[
b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p,
\]

so \(r=p-1\) is also not a target. Reflection gives

\[
b_{p-2}\equiv b_1=5\pmod p,
\]

so an actual top-half target has

\[
2\le r\le p-3.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Only the weaker fact \(1\le r\le p-2\) is needed for most of the proof.

### 5.2 No consecutive zeros

For \(0\le j\le p-2\), the Apéry sequence cannot have

\[
b_j\equiv b_{j+1}\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Indeed, the recurrence has invertible coefficients at every interior row below \(p\), so two consecutive zeros propagate backwards to \(b_0=0\), contradicting \(b_0=1\).

Consequently, if \(p\mid b_r\), then

\[
p\nmid b_{r-1},\qquad p\nmid b_{r+1}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

By Lucas,

\[
p\nmid b_{n-1},\qquad p\nmid b_{n+1}.
\tag{5.6}
\]

The first assertion in (5.6) is the exact unit input used in claim (2).

## 6. The singular row at \(p\): exact normalization and no cancellation

For \(0\le j\le p-1\), the recurrence rows below \(p\) have denominators prime to \(p\). Starting from \(a_0=0,a_1=6\), this gives

\[
a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{p-1}\in\mathbb Z_p.
\tag{6.1}
\]

The binomial formula gives the stronger endpoint congruence

\[
b_{p-1}\equiv1\pmod{p^2}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Indeed, the \(k=0\) term is \(1\), while for every \(1\le k\le p-1\),

\[
\binom{p-1+k}{k}
\]

contains a factor \(p\), and its square contains \(p^2\).

Apply the Casoratian at \(n=p\):

\[
a_pb_{p-1}-a_{p-1}b_p=\frac6{p^3}.
\]

Multiplying by \(p^3\) gives

\[
p^3a_pb_{p-1}-p^3a_{p-1}b_p=6.
\tag{6.3}
\]

By (6.1), the second term is divisible by \(p^3\) in \(\mathbb Z_p\). By (6.2), \(b_{p-1}\) is a unit congruent to \(1\). Hence

\[
\boxed{p^3a_p\equiv6\pmod p,}
\tag{6.4}
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{v_p(a_p)=-3.}
\tag{6.5}
\]

Now use the recurrence at its singular row \(m=p\):

\[
(p+1)^3a_{p+1}=P(p)a_p-p^3a_{p-1}.
\tag{6.6}
\]

Multiply by \(p^3\):

\[
(p+1)^3(p^3a_{p+1})
=P(p)(p^3a_p)-p^6a_{p-1}.
\tag{6.7}
\]

Modulo \(p\),

\[
P(p)\equiv P(0)=5,
\]

so (6.4) gives

\[
\boxed{p^3a_{p+1}\equiv30\pmod p.}
\tag{6.8}
\]

For \(p\ge7\), \(30\) is a unit. Therefore

\[
\boxed{v_p(a_{p+1})=-3.}
\tag{6.9}
\]

This is the exact audit of the possible cancellation at \(p+1\): there is none. In valuation language, the two terms on the right of (6.6) have valuations \(-3\) and at least \(3\), so they cannot cancel. Congruence (6.8) records the surviving leading unit.

The exclusion of \(p=5\) is not cosmetic: \(P(p)\equiv5\equiv0\pmod5\) and \(30\equiv0\pmod5\), so this unit argument does not apply there.

## 7. Invertible transport from \((p,p+1)\) to \((n-1,n)\)

For a solution \(u\), define the state

\[
U_m=\binom{u_{m-1}}{u_m}.
\]

Then

\[
U_{m+1}=M_mU_m,
\qquad
M_m=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
-\dfrac{m^3}{(m+1)^3}&\dfrac{P(m)}{(m+1)^3}
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det M_m=\frac{m^3}{(m+1)^3}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

For

\[
m=p+1,p+2,\dots,n-1
\]

with \(n=p+r<2p\), neither \(m\) nor \(m+1\) is divisible by \(p\). Hence

\[
M_m\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_p).
\tag{7.3}
\]

For a vector \(x=(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb Q_p^2\), put

\[
\mu(x)=\min(v_p(x_1),v_p(x_2)).
\]

Every matrix in \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_p)\) preserves \(\mu\): integrality gives \(\mu(Mx)\ge\mu(x)\), and applying the same argument to \(M^{-1}\) gives the reverse inequality.

Using (6.5) and (6.9),

\[
\mu(a_p,a_{p+1})=-3.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\min(v_p(a_{n-1}),v_p(a_n))=-3.}
\tag{7.4}
\]

For \(r=1\), the transport product is empty and (7.4) is simply the already known state \((a_p,a_{p+1})\). That case is nevertheless nontarget by Section 5.

## 8. Proof of claim (2): equality \(v_p(b_n)=3\)

Assume now

\[
p\mid A_n,\qquad p\mid b_n.
\]

Put

\[
t=v_p(b_n)\ge1.
\]

Since \(a_n=A_n/C_n\) is reduced,

\[
v_p(a_n)=v_p(A_n)\ge1.
\tag{8.1}
\]

By (5.6), \(b_{n-1}\) is a \(p\)-unit. Also \(p\nmid n\), since \(n=p+r\) with \(0<r<p\). Hence the right side of

\[
a_nb_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_n=\frac6{n^3}
\tag{8.2}
\]

is a \(p\)-unit.

The first term on the left has positive valuation by (8.1). Therefore the second term must itself be a unit:

\[
v_p(a_{n-1})+v_p(b_n)=0.
\]

Thus

\[
v_p(a_{n-1})=-t.
\tag{8.3}
\]

Combining (8.1), (8.3), and the transport identity (7.4),

\[
-t
=\min(v_p(a_{n-1}),v_p(a_n))
=-3.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{v_p(b_n)=t=3.}
\tag{8.4}
\]

Nothing in this argument gives merely a lower bound: the equality follows from invariance of the minimum valuation.

## 9. New proved lemma: the complete top-half valuation dictionary

The hypothesis \(p\mid A_n\) is not needed to determine almost all relevant valuations.

### Theorem 9.1

Let \(p\ge7\), \(n=p+r\), and suppose \(p\mid b_n\). Put

\[
t=v_p(b_n)\ge1,
\qquad
\alpha=v_p(a_n),
\qquad
\beta=v_p(a_{n-1}).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\beta=-3}
\tag{9.1}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=
\begin{cases}
t-3,&t=1,2,\\
\ge0,&t=3,\\
0,&t\ge4.
\end{cases}}
\tag{9.2}
\]

Moreover,

\[
\boxed{v_p(a_{n+1})=-3.}
\tag{9.3}
\]

#### Proof

By (7.4),

\[
\min(\alpha,\beta)=-3.
\tag{9.4}
\]

Suppose \(\beta>-3\). Then (9.4) forces \(\alpha=-3\). Since \(t\ge1\),

\[
v_p(a_{n-1}b_n)=\beta+t>-3,
\]

while \(a_nb_{n-1}\) has valuation \(-3\). Their difference would have valuation \(-3\), contradicting the unit right side of the Casoratian. Therefore \(\beta=-3\), proving (9.1).

Now the two terms in the Casoratian have valuations

\[
\alpha,\qquad t-3.
\]

Their difference has valuation \(0\).

- If \(t=1\) or \(2\), then \(t-3<0\). Cancellation of the negative part is possible only if
  \[
  \alpha=t-3.
  \]
- If \(t=3\), the second term is a unit. If \(\alpha<0\), the first term would dominate and the difference would have negative valuation. Hence \(\alpha\ge0\).
- If \(t\ge4\), the second term has positive valuation, so the first term must be a unit. Hence \(\alpha=0\).

This proves (9.2).

An actual target has \(r\ne p-1\), so \(p\nmid n+1\). The row \(M_n\) is therefore in \(\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_p)\). It preserves the minimum valuation between the states

\[
(a_{n-1},a_n),\qquad(a_n,a_{n+1}).
\]

In every case in (9.2), \(\alpha>-3\). Since the first state has minimum \(-3\), the second state must also have minimum \(-3\), forcing

\[
v_p(a_{n+1})=-3.
\]

This proves (9.3). \(\square\)

### Corollary 9.2: exact denominator pattern

Because each fraction is reduced,

\[
v_p(C_m)=\max(0,-v_p(a_m)).
\]

Theorem 9.1 gives

\[
\boxed{
(v_p(C_{n-1}),v_p(C_n),v_p(C_{n+1}))
=\bigl(3,\max(3-t,0),3\bigr).}
\tag{9.5}
\]

In particular:

- \(t=1\): denominator pattern \((3,2,3)\), and \(p\nmid A_n\);
- \(t=2\): denominator pattern \((3,1,3)\), and \(p\nmid A_n\);
- \(t=3\): denominator pattern \((3,0,3)\), and \(p\mid A_n\) may or may not occur;
- \(t\ge4\): denominator pattern \((3,0,3)\), but \(p\nmid A_n\).

Therefore

\[
\boxed{p\mid H_n\Longrightarrow v_p(b_n)=3,}
\]

but the converse need not hold. Also

\[
v_p(H_n)\le3
\tag{9.6}
\]

for every top-half prime \(p\mid H_n\), because \(v_p(b_n)=3\).

This table shows exactly how small the intersection \(p\mid H_n\) is: it is contained in one subcase of the cubic-multiplicity locus.

## 10. A block congruence derived only from the Casoratian and recurrence

There is a clean way to package the singular-row calculation.

For \(0\le j\le p-1\), define

\[
X_j=p^3a_{p+j}.
\]

Sections 6 and 7 show that each \(X_j\) is \(p\)-integral. We have

\[
X_0\equiv6=6b_0\pmod p,
\qquad
X_1\equiv30=6b_1\pmod p.
\]

For \(1\le j\le p-2\), the recurrence at \(m=p+j\) gives

\[
(p+j+1)^3X_{j+1}
=P(p+j)X_j-(p+j)^3X_{j-1}.
\]

Reducing modulo \(p\),

\[
(j+1)^3X_{j+1}
\equiv P(j)X_j-j^3X_{j-1}\pmod p.
\]

This is exactly the Apéry recurrence for \(6b_j\), with the same first two values. Hence

\[
\boxed{p^3a_{p+j}\equiv6b_j\pmod p
\qquad(0\le j<p).}
\tag{10.1}
\]

This is the \(q=1\) block congruence, obtained here without invoking the explicit companion sum.

Combining (10.1) with Lucas,

\[
\boxed{
 p\mid b_{p+j}
 \Longleftrightarrow
 v_p(a_{p+j})>-3
 \Longleftrightarrow
 v_p(C_{p+j})<3.}
\tag{10.2}
\]

Thus a top-half target is exactly a drop from the generic denominator exponent \(3\).

## 11. New exact carrier for the entire top-half radical

Define

\[
\Delta_n=
\frac{\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1})}
     {\gcd(C_{n-1},C_n,C_{n+1})}.
\tag{11.1}
\]

For any prime \(p\), if

\[
c_-=v_p(C_{n-1}),\qquad c_0=v_p(C_n),\qquad c_+=v_p(C_{n+1}),
\]

then

\[
v_p(\Delta_n)=
\max\{0,\min(c_-,c_+)-c_0\}.
\tag{11.2}
\]

### Theorem 11.1

For every \(p\ge7\) with \(n/2<p\le n\),

\[
\boxed{
 v_p(\Delta_n)=
 \begin{cases}
  \min(v_p(b_n),3),&p\mid b_n,\\
  0,&p\nmid b_n.
 \end{cases}}
\tag{11.3}
\]

#### Proof

If \(p\mid b_n\), put \(t=v_p(b_n)\). Corollary 9.2 gives

\[
(c_-,c_0,c_+)=\bigl(3,\max(3-t,0),3\bigr).
\]

Therefore

\[
v_p(\Delta_n)
=3-\max(3-t,0)
=\min(t,3).
\]

If \(p\nmid b_n\), equation (10.2) gives \(c_0=3\) in the interior block. Every normalized value \(p^3a_{p+j}\) is integral, so the adjacent denominator exponents are at most \(3\). Hence

\[
\min(c_-,c_+)\le3=c_0
\]

and \(v_p(\Delta_n)=0\).

At the boundary \(n=p\), the left neighbor \(a_{p-1}\) is \(p\)-integral, so again the curvature is zero. At the opposite boundary \(n=2p-1\), \(b_n\not\equiv0\pmod p\) and the left adjacent exponent is at most \(3\), so the curvature is again zero. \(\square\)

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rad}_{(n/2,n]}\Delta_n
=\prod_{\substack{n/2<p\le n\\p\mid b_n}}p}
\tag{11.4}
\]

for primes \(p\ge7\), and the omitted finite primes are harmless for asymptotics.

This is genuinely stronger than claim (1). Claim (1) only says that the numerator intersection \(H_n\) appears in adjacent denominators. Theorem 11.1 says that the positive curvature of those denominators detects **every** top-half divisor of \(b_n\), including the simple targets which do not divide \(A_n\).

## 12. Why this still does not prove an \(o(n)\) radical bound

Let

\[
R_n^{\mathrm{top}}
=\prod_{\substack{n/2<p\le n\\p\mid b_n}}p
\]

and

\[
R_n^{H}
=\prod_{\substack{n/2<p\le n\\p\mid H_n}}p.
\]

### 12.1 The claims as originally stated only control \(R_n^H\)

Claim (2) gives

\[
p\mid H_n\Longrightarrow v_p(b_n)=3.
\]

Hence

\[
(R_n^H)^3\mid b_n.
\]

Using

\[
\log b_n=n\log(17+12\sqrt2)+O(\log n),
\]

one obtains only

\[
\log R_n^H
\le
\frac13\log b_n
=rac{\log(17+12\sqrt2)}3n+O(\log n).
\tag{12.1}
\]

This is linear, not \(o(n)\).

Claim (1) also gives

\[
R_n^H\mid\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1}),
\]

but the classical denominator estimate

\[
C_m\mid\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,m)^3
\]

again gives only

\[
\log R_n^H=O(n).
\tag{12.2}
\]

Neither route supplies a little-oh saving.

More importantly, \(R_n^H\) omits almost all structurally possible targets. The valuation dictionary shows:

- every simple target \(v_p(b_n)=1\) is omitted;
- every double target is omitted;
- every target of multiplicity at least four is omitted;
- even a cubic target is omitted when \(v_p(a_n)=0\).

Thus claim (2), by itself, concerns a much smaller intersection and says nothing about the number of simple top-half prime divisors. Local Poisson behavior predicts that simple targets are the dominant population.

### 12.2 The new carrier captures all targets but is equivalent to the open problem

Theorem 11.1 gives

\[
R_n^{\mathrm{top}}
=\operatorname{rad}_{(n/2,n]}\Delta_n.
\]

This is an exact characteristic-zero reformulation. It does not yet compress height. Since

\[
\Delta_n\mid\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1}),
\]

the known denominator estimates imply only

\[
\log\Delta_n=O(n).
\]

The desired theorem is precisely

\[
\boxed{
\log\operatorname{rad}_{(n/2,n]}\Delta_n=o(n).}
\tag{12.3}
\]

No general theorem on denominators of \(G\)-functions, no local recurrence argument, and no prefix-lcm estimate gives (12.3). The current repository already records the relevant obstruction in its denominator-hole audits: an order-two rational recurrence can support arbitrarily prescribed isolated denominator holes unless one uses the distinguished global Apéry initial state in a genuinely nonlocal way.

### 12.3 Multiplicity conjectures would not solve the count problem

A conjecture that every top-half target is simple, or that cubic targets are rare, would make \(R_n^H\) empty or small. It would **not** bound \(R_n^{\mathrm{top}}\): one can still have \(\asymp n/\log n\) distinct simple prime divisors, each of valuation one. Therefore multiplicity control and target-count control are logically different problems.

## 13. Final classification

### Proved in this audit

1. The Casoratian is
   \[
   a_nb_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_n=6/n^3
   \]
   with positive sign.
2. \(H_n\mid\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1})\) and \(\gcd(H_n,C_n)=1\), after explicitly removing the apparent \(2,3\) obstruction using the Apéry Lucas property.
3. For \(p\ge7\),
   \[
   p^3a_p\equiv6,\qquad p^3a_{p+1}\equiv30\pmod p.
   \]
4. The proposed claim
   \[
   p\mid A_n,b_n\Longrightarrow v_p(b_n)=3
   \]
   is correct as an equality.
5. The full valuation dictionary (9.1)--(9.5) holds for every top-half target.
6. The denominator-curvature identity (11.3) detects the complete top-half target radical.

### Not proved, and still equivalent to the open pointwise problem

No argument here proves

\[
\log R_n^{\mathrm{top}}=o(n).
\]

The original two claims only characterize the small intersection \(p\mid H_n\). The new curvature lemma upgrades the characterization to all targets, but it supplies no sublinear height theorem.

### Precise reopen condition

The denominator route becomes a solution only after proving an Apéry-specific theorem such as

\[
\log
\frac{\gcd(C_{n-1},C_{n+1})}
     {\gcd(C_{n-1},C_n,C_{n+1})}
=o(n),
\]

or merely the same estimate after restricting its radical to \((n/2,n]\). Such a theorem must use the one globally distinguished Apéry solution, not only the local recurrence, Casoratian, or generic denominator growth.
