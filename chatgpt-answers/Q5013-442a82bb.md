# AESZ #209, Conservative Matrix Fields, and the exact certificate still needed for Problem 2.7

## Executive answer

There is a precise and useful CMF route from the AESZ #209 binomial sum to an order-three coefficient recurrence, but the CMF paper does **not** contain a ready-made AESZ #209 / Problem 2.7 proof that can simply be quoted.

The most important distinctions are:

1. **CMF dimension and matrix rank are independent.** A four-term scalar recurrence has an order-three companion matrix, hence rank \(3\). A higher-dimensional CMF means that this rank-three module has several commuting parameter-shift directions; it does not mean enlarging the companion merely because there are several variables.
2. **Every recurrence companion is already a one-dimensional CMF.** This observation is tautological and proves no limit. The nontrivial content is finding additional shift matrices satisfying the flatness identities, and an exact coboundary gauge from their trajectory to the printed Problem 2.7 companion.
3. **“Conservative” means noncommutative path independence**, not that a scalar error sum automatically telescopes to zero.
4. **A CMF factorization alone does not prove irrationality or even convergence.** The 2025 paper explicitly formulates much of the higher-dimensional asymptotic and arithmetic behavior as conjectures. For Problem 2.7 one still needs an exact period/normal-function certificate proving that

   \[
   p_n-(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3))q_n
   \]

   has no dominant component.
5. **The rank-four Calabi–Yau differential system and the rank-three coefficient companion are different modules.** A fourth-order differential equation may yield a four-term, order-three coefficient recurrence without any rank-four-to-rank-three quotient. The two “orders” measure different things.
6. The AESZ coefficient formula does provide a concrete starting point. Its summand is a terminating \({}_5F_4(1)\), its two elementary shift quotients can be written explicitly, and creative telescoping produces a rank-three quotient modulo \(k\)-differences. This is the closest exact bridge between WZ and CMF.

There is also a status issue in the repository that this route should resolve. The current [`STATUS.md`](https://github.com/xiangyazi24/Ramanujan_Challenge/blob/main/STATUS.md) labels Problem 2.7 solved, but the current [`problems/2.7/proof.tex`](https://github.com/xiangyazi24/Ramanujan_Challenge/blob/main/problems/2.7/proof.tex) still states its main theorem under the assumption \(c_0(e)=0\), and the abstract describes only a \(5100\)-digit numerical certificate. An exact AESZ/CMF/WZ construction would be valuable precisely because it could replace that remaining assumption.

The concrete recipe is given in Sections 6–10 below.

# 1. What a rank-three CMF actually is

The relevant source is Weinbaum–Leibtag–Kalisch–Shalyt–Kaminer, [*On Conservative Matrix Fields: Continuous Asymptotics and Arithmetic*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08138), especially Definitions 11–17 and Theorem 3.1.

## 1.1 Definition

Let \(K\) be a characteristic-zero field and let

\[
x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d).
\]

A CMF of **dimension** \(d\) and **rank** \(r\) is a map

\[
M:\mathbb Z^d\longrightarrow \operatorname{GL}_r(K(x)),
\qquad v\longmapsto M_v(x),
\]

satisfying the cocycle identity

\[
\boxed{
M_{v+w}(x)=M_v(x)M_w(x+v).
}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Writing \(M_i=M_{e_i}\) for the coordinate generators, this is equivalent to the elementary-square or zero-curvature identities

\[
\boxed{
M_i(x)M_j(x+e_i)=M_j(x)M_i(x+e_j)
\qquad(1\le i,j\le d).
}
\tag{1.2}
\]

Equivalently, the ordered product around every elementary lattice square is the identity:

\[
M_i(x)M_j(x+e_i)M_i(x+e_j)^{-1}M_j(x)^{-1}=I_r.
\]

This is the exact meaning of **conservative**. A matrix product from \(x\) to \(x+v\) depends only on the endpoints, not on the chosen lattice path.

The paper defines a CMF over a field \(K\), not intrinsically “over the integers.” An integral CMF suitable for arithmetic needs an additional invariant lattice or denominator-clearing theorem.

## 1.2 The rank-three companion

Write a scalar four-term recurrence as

\[
c_0(n)u_n+c_1(n)u_{n+1}+c_2(n)u_{n+2}+c_3(n)u_{n+3}=0,
\qquad c_3(n)\ne0.
\tag{1.3}
\]

For the state

\[
X_n=(u_n,u_{n+1},u_{n+2})^T,
\]

one has

\[
X_{n+1}=C(n)X_n,
\]

with

\[
\boxed{
C(n)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
-c_0(n)/c_3(n)&-c_1(n)/c_3(n)&-c_2(n)/c_3(n)
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\tag{1.4}
\]

This is the exact \(3\times3\) generalization of the \(2\times2\) companion used for a three-term recurrence. Nothing essentially new happens in passing from rank \(2\) to rank \(3\): the state remembers three consecutive values rather than two.

For a single shift direction, define

\[
M_m(n)=C(n+m-1)\cdots C(n+1)C(n),
\qquad m\ge1.
\]

Then

\[
M_{m+\ell}(n)=M_m(n+\ell)M_\ell(n)
\]

up to the row/column orientation convention. Thus every rational companion recurrence gives a dimension-one CMF. The paper states this explicitly in Example 2.6.

This shows why the sentence “the recurrence has a CMF” is not yet a theorem about its limit: every recurrence does.

## 1.3 Higher-dimensional factorization

A genuine higher-dimensional construction supplies matrices

\[
M_1(x),\ldots,M_d(x)\in\operatorname{GL}_3(K(x))
\]

satisfying (1.2). A trajectory is a base point \(x_0\) and a direction \(v\in\mathbb Z^d\). Its one-step matrix is

\[
T_{x_0,v}(n)=M_v(x_0+nv),
\]

and the \(n\)-step product is

\[
M_{nv}(x_0)=T_{x_0,v}(n-1)\cdots T_{x_0,v}(0).
\tag{1.5}
\]

The paper does not assert a universal factorization of every companion into some fixed list of “simple” elementary matrices. Its constructive statement is different: shift operators acting on a finite-dimensional Ore module produce the generator matrices \(M_i\). Their products are path independent because the shift operators commute.

# 2. Coboundaries: how a CMF trajectory becomes a companion recurrence

Two CMFs \(M\) and \(\widetilde M\) are coboundary equivalent if a rational matrix \(G(x)\) satisfies

\[
\boxed{
G(x)M_v(x)=\widetilde M_v(x)G(x+v).
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\widetilde M_v(x)=G(x)M_v(x)G(x+v)^{-1}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Along a trajectory this becomes

\[
G(x_0+nv)T(n)=\widetilde T(n)G(x_0+(n+1)v).
\tag{2.3}
\]

If \(\widetilde T(n)\) is the companion (1.4), then (2.3) is the exact finite certificate connecting the CMF construction to the printed scalar recurrence.

The Apéry example in Section 2.6 of the CMF paper does precisely this: a rank-two trajectory is changed by a rational coboundary into the familiar \(2\times2\) Apéry companion.

For Problem 2.7 the corresponding load-bearing certificate would be

\[
\boxed{
G(n)T_{\mathrm{AESZ/CMF}}(n)=C_{27}(n)G(n+1),
\qquad G(n)\in\operatorname{GL}_3(\mathbb Q(n)).
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

It is checkable by clearing denominators and verifying nine polynomial identities. This matrix identity, plus matching initial data, would establish that the two constructions give the same rational sequences.

At present, the project proof does not print such a \(G(n)\).

# 3. What the CMF construction proves—and what it does not

## 3.1 The exact theorem in the paper

Let \(f(x,z)\) be D-finite for a multivariate Ore algebra containing parameter shifts \(S_{x_i}\) and possibly Euler derivatives \(\theta_z\). Suppose the Ore image

\[
\mathcal A.f
\]

has finite dimension \(r\) over \(K(x,z)\). Choose a basis

\[
B=(b_1.f,\ldots,b_r.f).
\]

For every lattice shift \(v\), define \(M_v^f\) by

\[
\boxed{
B\,M_v^f=S_v.B=\sigma_v(B).
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Theorem 3.1 of the paper proves that the matrices \(M_v^f\) form a rank-\(r\) CMF. In computational terms:

1. compute an annihilating left ideal for \(f\);
2. choose standard monomials modulo a Gröbner basis as \(B\);
3. reduce \(S_{x_i}b_j\) modulo the annihilator;
4. place the reduction coefficients in the \(i\)-th shift matrix.

Different choices of basis give coboundary-equivalent CMFs.

This is the concrete construction to apply to AESZ #209.

## 3.2 Why the factorization does not by itself prove the limit

A CMF supplies exact algebraic transport. It does not automatically say which asymptotic mode a given initial vector selects.

There are several decisive reasons:

- Every one-dimensional companion recurrence is a CMF.
- The paper exhibits CMF ratios that do not converge.
- The paper's high-dimensional continuity and arithmetic claims are stated as conjectures, not general theorems.
- Even in ordinary Poincaré–Perron theory, the roots determine possible modes, but the vanishing of a particular connection coefficient is a global datum.

The CMF ratio is

\[
L_{x,v}^{p,p',q,q'}(n)
=
\frac{p^TM_{nv}(x)p'}{q^TM_{nv}(x)q'}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The framework proves that such objects are structured D-finite ratios and that rational gauges preserve already-established limits under suitable hypotheses. It does not prove that an arbitrary ratio converges to the desired period.

For Problem 2.7 one still needs to prove all of the following:

1. the denominator sequence has a nonzero dominant coefficient;
2. the error
   \[
   e_n=p_n-(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3))q_n
   \]
   lies in the recessive plane;
3. the numerator and denominator have the required arithmetic control if an irrationality result is claimed.

A CMF built from an integral or Barnes representation can make item 2 transparent, because the error may be represented by a contour or real integral with a manifestly smaller phase. But this is an additional property of the chosen realization, not a consequence of flatness alone.

# 4. Why a rank-four Calabi–Yau system can yield an order-three recurrence

Let the AESZ Picard–Fuchs operator be written in Euler form as

\[
\mathcal L_z=\sum_{j=0}^{m}z^jP_j(\theta),
\qquad \theta=z\frac{d}{dz},
\qquad \deg P_j\le4.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Its **differential order** is

\[
\max_j\deg P_j=4.
\]

For a power series

\[
F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}A_nz^n,
\]

coefficient extraction gives

\[
\sum_{j=0}^{m}P_j(n-j)A_{n-j}=0.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The number of shifts in (4.2) is \(m+1\), so its **recurrence order** is \(m\), not the differential order \(4\).

Consequently, if the fourth-order AESZ operator has degree \(3\) in \(z\), its coefficients naturally satisfy a four-term, order-three recurrence. No projection from a four-dimensional vector space to a three-dimensional one is required.

This resolves the apparent \(4\times4\) versus \(3\times3\) paradox:

- the \(4\times4\) Gauss–Manin companion acts on
  \[
  (F,\theta F,\theta^2F,\theta^3F);
  \]
- the \(3\times3\) recurrence companion acts on
  \[
  (A_n,A_{n+1},A_{n+2}).
  \]

They live in different categories and use different independent variables.

A genuine rank reduction would require an explicit matrix projection or intertwiner, for example

\[
P(n)M_4(n)=M_3(n)P(n+1),
\qquad \operatorname{rank}P=3,
\tag{4.3}
\]

with an invariant one-dimensional kernel. The term “sub-CMF” in the 2025 paper refers primarily to restricting the lattice of parameter directions; it does not automatically reduce matrix rank.

The current Problem 2.7 proof also records that its order-three difference operator is not a symmetric square. Thus a hypothetical relation to the rank-four AESZ system should not be described as a simple symmetric-square descent without a new exact certificate.

# 5. The AESZ #209 coefficient as a terminating hypergeometric sum

Put

\[
T(n,k)=inom{2n}{n}inom nk^2inom{n+k}{n}inom{n+2k}{n},
\qquad
A_n=\sum_{k=0}^nT(n,k).
\tag{5.1}
\]

The inner sum is exactly

\[
\boxed{
A_n=inom{2n}{n}\,
{}_5F_4\!\left(
\begin{matrix}
-n,-n,n+1,\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n+2}{2}\\
1,1,1,\frac12
\end{matrix};1
\right).
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

This follows from

\[
\binom{n+2k}{n}
=
\frac{((n+1)/2)_k((n+2)/2)_k}{(1/2)_k\,k!}.
\]

The first elementary shift quotient is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{T(n,k+1)}{T(n,k)}
=
\frac{(n-k)^2(n+k+1)(n+2k+1)(n+2k+2)}
{(k+1)^3(2k+1)(2k+2)}.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

The \(n\)-shift quotient is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{T(n+1,k)}{T(n,k)}
=
\frac{2(2n+1)(n+k+1)(n+2k+1)}
{(n+1)(n-k+1)^2}.
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

These satisfy the scalar flatness identity

\[
R_n(n,k)R_k(n+1,k)=R_k(n,k)R_n(n,k+1).
\tag{5.5}
\]

Thus the summand itself defines a rank-one, dimension-two CMF in the variables \((n,k)\).

This rank-one CMF is **not** the rank-three Problem 2.7 companion. The missing operation is definite summation in \(k\), equivalently quotienting by total \(k\)-differences.

# 6. The exact bridge from WZ to a rank-three companion

A creative-telescoping certificate has the form

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{j=0}^{3}\ell_j(n)T(n+j,k)
=\Delta_k\bigl(R(n,k)T(n,k)\bigr),
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

where

\[
\Delta_kF(n,k)=F(n,k+1)-F(n,k).
\]

If the boundary term vanishes at \(k=0\) and \(k=n+1\), summing (6.1) proves

\[
\sum_{j=0}^{3}\ell_j(n)A_{n+j}=0.
\tag{6.2}
\]

This is the exact WZ certificate one should compute for the AESZ #209 sequence. Guessing (6.2) from many terms is not enough; the rational function \(R(n,k)\) is the finite proof.

There is a useful matrix interpretation. In an Ore localization, form the quotient of the summand module by \(k\)-differences,

\[
\mathcal H=
\frac{\langle S_n^jT:j\ge0\rangle_{\mathbb Q(n,k)}}
{\Delta_k(\text{summand module})}.
\tag{6.3}
\]

If the minimal telescoper has order \(3\), then the cyclic subspace generated by \([T]\) has basis

\[
[T],\quad[S_nT],\quad[S_n^2T].
\]

The action of \(S_n\) on this basis is exactly a \(3\times3\) companion matrix. The summation functional

\[
[T]\longmapsto\sum_kT(n,k)
\]

kills total differences and converts that matrix action into the scalar recurrence for \(A_n\).

This is the most concrete answer to “are the WZ relations the CMF?”:

- the two raw shift quotients form a rank-one scalar CMF;
- creative telescoping passes to a finite-dimensional quotient modulo \(k\)-differences;
- the \(n\)-shift on that quotient yields the rank-three companion.

The 2025 CMF paper does not spell out this definite-summation quotient as an AESZ #209 example, but it is fully compatible with its Ore-module construction.

# 7. A practical subtlety: half-parameter shifts and \(z=1\)

The parameter vector in (5.2) is

\[
a(n)=\left(-n,-n,n+1,\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n+2}{2}ight).
\]

The paper defines CMFs on an integer shift lattice. A one-step change \(n\mapsto n+1\) moves two parameters by \(1/2\), so it is not directly an integer lattice direction in these coordinates.

There are three remedies.

## 7.1 Split parity

Write

\[
n=2m+\varepsilon,
\qquad \varepsilon\in\{0,1\}.
\]

Then \(m\mapsto m+1\), equivalently \(n\mapsto n+2\), shifts the upper parameters by

\[
\boxed{v=(-2,-2,+2,+1,+1),}
\tag{7.1}
\]

an integral direction. Construct one CMF trajectory for each parity and then recombine them.

## 7.2 Reparameterize

Introduce independent integer parameters for the doubled quantities before restricting to the diagonal specialization. This can preserve a one-step trajectory but enlarges the lattice dimension.

## 7.3 Use the finite-sum Ore module directly

The WZ route of Section 6 avoids parameter-lattice half-shifts altogether and is likely the simplest computational approach.

There is a second subtlety. The paper's generic \({}_pF_q\) shift formulas use a differential basis and contain factors involving \(1-z\). Our \({}_5F_4\) is evaluated at \(z=1\), where the generic \(p=q+1\) differential system is singular, although the series terminates because of the parameters \(-n\).

Therefore one should not insert \(z=1\) blindly into the generic formula. Either:

- construct at symbolic \(z\), find a basis regular on the terminating locus, and take the specialization carefully; or
- use the finite-sum/WZ realization, where termination and boundary conditions are explicit.

# 8. What must be shown to identify AESZ #209 with the printed Problem 2.7 recurrence

The statement “Problem 2.7 is AESZ #209” can mean several inequivalent things. The repository needs one of the following exact certificates.

## 8.1 Literal equality after a scalar gauge

Find a hypergeometric factor \(h_n\ne0\) such that, after

\[
q_n=h_nA_{n+s},
\]

the recurrence for \(A_n\) becomes the printed \(L_{27}\). This requires an exact rational ratio

\[
\frac{h_{n+1}}{h_n}\in\mathbb Q(n)
\]

and equality of all four transformed coefficients.

## 8.2 A contiguous combination

The challenge denominator may be a rational combination of nearby AESZ coefficients:

\[
q_n=r_0(n)A_n+r_1(n)A_{n+1}+r_2(n)A_{n+2}.
\tag{8.1}
\]

This is an Ore intertwiner \(R=r_0+r_1S+r_2S^2\). The certificate is

\[
\boxed{
L_{27}R=Q L_{209}
}
\tag{8.2}
\]

for an exact quotient operator \(Q\).

## 8.3 A rational matrix gauge

Construct the trajectory matrix \(T_{209}(n)\) from the CMF and solve

\[
\boxed{
G(n)T_{209}(n)=C_{27}(n)G(n+1).
}
\tag{8.3}
\]

This is the most flexible formulation and allows a change of cyclic vector that is invisible at the level of Poincaré roots.

In every case, three independent initial values must be matched. Poincaré polynomials, first coefficients, and hundreds of numerical terms are not substitutes for (8.2) or (8.3).

The present project file does not yet contain such a certificate.

# 9. The holomorphic period is not yet the \(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\) certificate

Even after the AESZ coefficient recurrence is identified exactly, the holomorphic period \(A_n\) supplies only the **pure period carrier**. The desired limit is a mixed extension involving weight \(2\) and weight \(3\).

A useful exact deformation is

\[
J(s)=\int_0^1\!\int_0^1
\frac{(xy)^s}{1-xy}\,dx\,dy
=\zeta(2,s+1),
\]

for which

\[
\boxed{
\left(1-\frac12\partial_s\right)J(s)\bigg|_{s=0}
=\zeta(2)+\zeta(3).
}
\tag{9.1}
\]

The analogous AESZ construction would require an independently defined family

\[
\mathcal I_n(s)
=\int_\Gamma H_n(x;s)\,\omega(x;s)
\]

or a Barnes sum such that:

1. a single exact telescoper annihilates \(\mathcal I_n(s)\) for symbolic \(s\);
2. applying \(1-\frac12\partial_s\) at \(s=0\) yields
   \[
   q_n(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3))-p_n;
   \]
3. its contour or real-domain phase gives a rigorous recessive bound;
4. the first three values match the challenge sequences.

Alternatively, one may construct separate \(\zeta(2)\)- and \(\zeta(3)\)-Barnes forms with the same denominator, as in Dauguet–Zudilin, and prove equality of their denominator sequences by a hypergeometric transformation.

This extension layer is exactly where the current numerical statement

\[
c_0\bigl(p-(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3))q\bigr)=0
\]

must be replaced by an exact identity.

A CMF for the holomorphic period alone cannot prove it.

# 10. Concrete reconstruction protocol

The following is the shortest honest workflow I would implement.

## Phase A: certify AESZ #209 at the scalar level

1. **Generate coefficients exactly** from (5.1).
2. **Run exact creative telescoping** on \(T(n,k)\), obtaining both:
   - the minimal telescoper \(L_{209}\);
   - the rational WZ certificate \(R(n,k)\) in (6.1).
3. **Verify the certificate symbolically** after clearing denominators.
4. **Derive the fourth-order Picard–Fuchs operator** from the recurrence, or extract the recurrence from the published AESZ operator, and verify equality in both directions.
5. Record the exact index convention and the initial coefficients
   \[
   1,14,978,103820,13387570,\ldots.
   \]

## Phase B: connect AESZ #209 to Problem 2.7

6. Form the exact \(3\times3\) companion \(C_{209}(n)\).
7. Compare \(C_{209}(n)\) with the printed \(C_{27}(n)\):
   - first test scalar Pochhammer gauges;
   - next test an Ore intertwiner of order \(1\) or \(2\);
   - finally solve the matrix gauge equation (8.3).
8. Verify the resulting identity by `field_simp`/polynomial reduction, not by sample values.
9. Match a full initial basis, not only one sequence.

## Phase C: build the higher-dimensional CMF

10. Introduce independent Pochhammer parameters for the terminating \({}_5F_4\), or work with the bivariate summand \(T(n,k)\).
11. Compute an annihilator Gröbner basis in the relevant Ore algebra.
12. Choose a basis \(B\) of the finite Ore image.
13. Reduce every coordinate shift of \(B\) to obtain \(M_i\).
14. Check every flatness identity
    \[
    M_i\sigma_i(M_j)=M_j\sigma_j(M_i)
    \]
    exactly.
15. Select the diagonal or parity-split trajectory reproducing the AESZ coefficients.
16. Find the coboundary to \(C_{27}(n)\).

## Phase D: construct the mixed period

17. Add a logarithmic deformation parameter or a Barnes normal-function companion.
18. Produce a second exact telescoping identity proving that the error sequence satisfies the same recurrence.
19. Evaluate the initial boundary values as rational combinations of
    \[
    1,\quad\zeta(2),\quad\zeta(3).
    \]
20. Prove the error is recessive by a real-integral maximum, a saddle estimate, or contour deformation.
21. Prove the denominator has a nonzero dominant coefficient.
22. Conclude the exact limit and then address denominator/height growth if irrationality is sought.

Phases A and B identify the recurrence. Phase D identifies the constant. The CMF framework organizes Phase C and can make Phase D natural, but it does not eliminate it.

# 11. Exact verification templates

## 11.1 Summand flatness

```sage
# In K = Frac(QQ[n,k])
Rn = 2*(2*n+1)*(n+k+1)*(n+2*k+1) / ((n+1)*(n-k+1)^2)
Rk = ((n-k)^2*(n+k+1)*(n+2*k+1)*(n+2*k+2)
      / ((k+1)^3*(2*k+1)*(2*k+2)))

assert (Rn * Rk(n=n+1) - Rk * Rn(k=k+1)).factor() == 0
```

The substitution syntax should be adapted to the CAS, but the verified identity must be exact.

## 11.2 WZ certificate

```sage
lhs = sum(ell[j](n) * T(n+j,k) for j in range(4))
rhs = cert(n,k+1)*T(n,k+1) - cert(n,k)*T(n,k)
assert numerator((lhs-rhs).factor()) == 0
```

One must separately prove the endpoint terms vanish.

## 11.3 CMF flatness

```sage
for i in range(d):
    for j in range(i+1, d):
        Z = M[i] * shift(M[j], i) - M[j] * shift(M[i], j)
        assert all(entry.numerator() == 0 for entry in Z.list())
```

## 11.4 Gauge to the Problem 2.7 companion

```sage
Z = G(n) * T209(n) - C27(n) * G(n+1)
assert all(entry.numerator() == 0 for entry in Z.list())
assert det(G(n)) != 0
```

These four finite checks are the core machine-verifiable part of the proposed proof.

# 12. Final answers to the six questions

## 1. How does CMF generalize the \(2\times2\) construction?

A recurrence of order \(r\) uses an \(r\times r\) companion. A four-term recurrence therefore uses rank \(3\). A dimension-\(d\) CMF consists of \(d\) commuting-shift \(3\times3\) generators satisfying (1.2). There is no special obstruction at rank \(3\).

## 2. Is recessiveness built into CMF factorization?

No. Flatness gives path-independent algebraic transport. Recessiveness follows only when the CMF is accompanied by an integral, contour, or exact normal-function representation with a smaller phase, or by some other connection-coefficient certificate. The general higher-dimensional convergence principles in the 2025 paper are partly conjectural.

## 3. Must a \(4\times4\) AESZ system reduce to \(3\times3\)?

No. Differential order \(4\) and coefficient-recurrence order \(3\) are independent. Equation (4.2) explains how a fourth-order Picard–Fuchs equation can have a four-term coefficient recurrence. An actual rank reduction would require an explicit projection or intertwiner.

## 4. Which CMF definitions and theorems apply?

Use:

- Definition 11: CMF cocycle;
- the generator flatness criterion following it;
- Definitions 12–13: evaluation and trajectory products;
- Definition 14: coboundary equivalence;
- Definition 15 and Theorem 3.1: shift matrices from a finite Ore image;
- Proposition 3.2: basis changes give coboundaries;
- Lemma 3.3: compatibility with differential systems;
- Definition 17: CMF ratios;
- Section 4 results only after checking their convergence hypotheses.

Do not use the conjectural Section 5 phenomena as if they were general theorems.

## 5. Can the CMF be reverse-engineered from the binomial sum?

Yes, in two stages. The explicit quotients (5.3)–(5.4) give a scalar two-dimensional CMF for the summand. Creative telescoping modulo \(k\)-differences gives the rank-three \(n\)-shift companion for the definite sum. The exact WZ certificate is the first object to compute.
