# R3-e: growing factorial moments for pointwise Apéry bounds

## 0. Executive verdict

Let

\[
H(n)=\#\{p\text{ prime}:n/2<p\le n,\ p\mid b_n\}.
\]

The growing-factorial-moment route is mathematically sound, but it asks for a genuinely new **cross-characteristic quasi-independence theorem**. The closest precedents each supply only one part of the desired mechanism:

1. **Montgomery--Soundararajan** supply the closest *logical template*: expand a high moment, reduce it to uniform joint correlations of up to `K` distinct local conditions, and classify repeated-index/diagonal terms. Their prime theorem is conditional on an extremely strong quantitative Hardy--Littlewood conjecture and is stated for each fixed `K`, not for `K=K(N)`. Even if one pretends all constants are uniform, their displayed error terms do not reach `K\asymp\log N`.
2. **Granville--Soundararajan's sieve moment proposition** is the closest *abstract engine*. Its error term is exactly a sum of remainders over products of at most `k` local primes. But its uniform range is
   \[
   k\le \sigma_P^{2/3};
   \]
   for ordinary prime-divisor counting, where `\sigma_P^2\asymp\log\log X`, this is only
   \[
   k\le (\log\log X)^{1/3}.
   \]
3. **Fouvry--Kowalski--Michel** supply the closest *geometric multi-correlation theorem*: a product of a fixed number of bounded-conductor trace functions over one finite field has square-root cancellation away from classified diagonal tuples. Their constants depend on the number of factors and the conductors, and the theorem is not a growing-`k`, varying-characteristic result. More seriously, the indicator `1_{b_r=0}` is not presently represented by a bounded-conductor sheaf in the index variable, and the factors in our correlation live over different fields `\mathbf F_{p_i}`.
4. **Sathe--Selberg/Selberg--Delange and Halberstam-type Erdős--Kac theorems** exploit multiplicative product structure. They control the *value* `\omega(m)=j` up to `j\asymp\log\log X`, or centered moments of order at most a small power of `\log\log X`; this is very different from moment order `k\asymp\log X`.
5. **Polymath8 dense divisibility** is a modulus-factorization technology for congruence classes to smooth squarefree moduli. Our intersections are exact equalities in an interval of length `X`, while the product `p_1\cdots p_k` is about `X^k`; the CRT period is therefore radically undersampled. Dense divisibility does not by itself estimate the required additive codegrees.

The clean condition to attack is an **aggregate high-codegree bound**, not a per-tuple asymptotic. On a dyadic interval `I_X=(X,2X]`, put

\[
A_{p,X}:=(p+Z_p)\cap I_X,
\qquad
Z_p=\{0\le r<p:p\mid b_r\}.
\]

Then, exactly,

\[
H(n)=\sum_p 1_{A_{p,X}}(n)\qquad(n\in I_X)
\]

and

\[
M_k(X):=\sum_{n\in I_X}(H(n))_k
 =\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\\\text{pairwise distinct}}}
 \left|A_{p_1,X}\cap\cdots\cap A_{p_k,X}\right|.            \tag{0.1}
\]

A sufficient Poisson-type hypothesis is

\[
M_k(X)
 \le C^k(k!)^A |I_X|^{1-k}
       \left(\sum_p|A_{p,X}|\right)^k                  \tag{QI}
\]

uniformly for `1\le k\le c\log X`. If additionally

\[
\sum_p|A_{p,X}|\ll |I_X|/\log X,                       \tag{I}
\]

then

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)\ll
\begin{cases}
\log X/\log\log X,&A<2,\\
\log X,&A=2,\\
(\log X)^{A-1},&A>2,
\end{cases}                                             \tag{0.2}
\]

with the `A=2` line stated in the safe non-optimized form. In particular the desired pointwise result follows.

There is also a simpler hypothesis matching the question exactly:

\[
\frac1{|I_X|}\sum_{n\in I_X}(H(n))_k\le C^k(k!)^A
\quad(1\le k\le c\log X).                              \tag{FM}
\]

It implies

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)\ll (\log X)^{\max(1,A)}.             \tag{0.3}
\]

The important strategic refinement is that `k\asymp\log X` is needed for a **polylogarithmic** maximum, but not for the conjecture `H(n)=o(n/\log n)`. A single moment order `K=K(X)\to\infty`, even very slowly, already suffices if

\[
M_K(X)\le |I_X|\,C^K(K!)^A.                             \tag{0.4}
\]

Indeed it gives `\max H=X^{o(1)}`. Thus the realistic first milestone is not `K=c\log X`; it is a genuinely uniform theorem for some effective `K(X)\to\infty` with controlled constants.

## 1. The exact factorial-moment object

### 1.1 Dyadic row model

Fix

\[
I_X=(X,2X]\cap\mathbf Z,
\qquad
\mathcal P_X=\{p\text{ prime}:X/2<p\le 2X\}.
\]

For `p\in\mathcal P_X`, define

\[
A_{p,X}=(p+Z_p)\cap I_X.
\]

For `n\in I_X`, the following are equivalent:

\[
\begin{aligned}
n\in A_{p,X}
&\Longleftrightarrow n=p+r\text{ for some }0\le r<p\text{ with }p\mid b_r\\
&\Longleftrightarrow n/2<p\le n\text{ and }p\mid b_{n-p}\\
&\Longleftrightarrow n/2<p\le n\text{ and }p\mid b_n.
\end{aligned}
\]

The last equivalence is Gessel's two-digit congruence

\[
b_{p+r}\equiv b_1b_r=5b_r\pmod p
\]

for `p\ge7`. Consequently

\[
H(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}1_{A_{p,X}}(n).              \tag{1.1}
\]

This formulation removes an ambiguity that appears if one speaks only of “congruence classes.” Each `A_{p,X}` is an actual subset of one common integer interval.

### 1.2 Exact high-codegree identity

For an integer `k\ge1`, let

\[
(x)_k=x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1).
\]

Expanding `(1.1)` gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
M_k(X)
&:=\sum_{n\in I_X}(H(n))_k\\
&=\sum_{\substack{(p_1,\ldots,p_k)\in\mathcal P_X^k\\p_i\text{ pairwise distinct}}}
  \sum_{n\in I_X}\prod_{i=1}^k1_{A_{p_i,X}}(n)\\
&=\sum_{\substack{(p_1,\ldots,p_k)\in\mathcal P_X^k\\p_i\text{ pairwise distinct}}}
  C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k),                           \tag{1.2}
\end{aligned}
\]

where

\[
C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k)
 :=|A_{p_1,X}\cap\cdots\cap A_{p_k,X}|.                  \tag{1.3}
\]

Equivalently, taking `p_1` as an anchor and declaring an out-of-range zero indicator to be zero,

\[
C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k)
 =\sum_{z\in Z_{p_1}}1_{I_X}(p_1+z)
   \prod_{i=2}^k
   1_{Z_{p_i}}\bigl(z+p_1-p_i\bigr).                     \tag{1.4}
\]

Thus the required object is a **cross-characteristic shifted correlation** of zero sets. Choosing `z\in Z_{p_1}` fixes the common integer `n=p_1+z`; all other zero coordinates are forced by

\[
z_i=z+p_1-p_i.                                          \tag{1.5}
\]

An intersection can contain more than one integer, but its cardinality is at most `\min_i|Z_{p_i}|`.

For unordered prime tuples,

\[
M_k(X)=k!\sum_{p_1<\cdots<p_k}C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k).       \tag{1.6}
\]

This is the precise high-order additive energy that must be bounded.

### 1.3 Calibration: what a genuine second moment would already prove

There is a useful logical warning. From one summand at a maximizing point,

\[
(H(n))_k\le M_k(X).                                      \tag{1.7}
\]

In particular, a **global** estimate

\[
M_2(X)\ll X^{2-\delta}                                  \tag{1.8}
\]

would already imply

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)\ll X^{1-\delta/2}=o(X/\log X).       \tag{1.9}
\]

At Poisson scale, `M_2(X)\ll X` would give `H(n)\ll X^{1/2}` pointwise.

Therefore the proved `X^{2/3+o(1)}` **exceptional-set theorem must not be conflated with a global bound of the form `(1.8)`**. A density theorem can leave one arbitrarily large spike inside its exceptional set. The role of growing moments is to control the mass of those spikes, not merely to improve the density statement.

### 1.4 Independent-row benchmark

Put

\[
N_X=|I_X|\asymp X,
\qquad
S_X=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}|A_{p,X}|,
\qquad
\lambda_X=S_X/N_X.                                      \tag{1.10}
\]

If subsets of an `N_X`-point universe with the same row sizes were placed independently and uniformly, then

\[
\mathbf E\,C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k)
 =N_X^{1-k}\prod_{i=1}^k|A_{p_i,X}|.                    \tag{1.11}
\]

Summing over ordered distinct tuples gives the benchmark

\[
M_k(X)\lesssim N_X^{1-k}S_X^k=N_X\lambda_X^k.           \tag{1.12}
\]

Numerically, `|Z_p|` has bounded mean, so one expects

\[
S_X\asymp X/\log X,
\qquad
\lambda_X\asymp1/\log X.                               \tag{1.13}
\]

The strict Poisson factorial moment would be `\lambda_X^k`; a bound with an extra `C^k(k!)^A` is a generous factorial-overhead version of quasi-independence.

## 2. Precedents for growing moments

## 2.1 Montgomery--Soundararajan: the precise range and hypothesis

The relevant paper is Hugh Montgomery and K. Soundararajan, *Primes in short intervals*, arXiv:math/0409258, especially Theorems 1--3 and Corollary 1.

They define

\[
M_K(N;H)=\sum_{n\le N}(\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)-H)^K.           \tag{2.1}
\]

For a set `\mathcal D=\{d_1,\ldots,d_k\}` of distinct shifts, write

\[
\sum_{n\le x}\prod_{i=1}^k\Lambda(n+d_i)
 =\mathfrak S(\mathcal D)x+E_k(x;\mathcal D).            \tag{2.2}
\]

Their Theorem 3 assumes

\[
E_k(x;\mathcal D)\ll N^{1/2+\varepsilon}                \tag{2.3}
\]

**uniformly** for every

\[
1\le k\le K,
\quad 0\le x\le N,
\quad d_i\text{ distinct},
\quad1\le d_i\le H.                                    \tag{2.4}
\]

Under this hypothesis they obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
M_K(N;H)
={}&\mu_KH^{K/2}\int_1^N(\log(x/H)+B)^{K/2}\,dx\\
&+O\!\left(
 N(\log N)^{K/2}H^{K/2}
 \left(\frac H{\log N}\right)^{-1/(8K)}
 +H^KN^{1/2+\varepsilon}
 \right),                                               \tag{2.5}
\end{aligned}
\]

uniformly for

\[
\log N\le H\le N^{1/K}.                                \tag{2.6}
\]

Here `\mu_K` is the `K`-th centered Gaussian moment.

### What is actually uniform in `K`?

The published theorem is a **fixed-`K` theorem**. Its `O`-constants and the input hypothesis are not supplied as a theorem uniform for `K=K(N)`. Corollary 1 assumes `(2.3)` for “arbitrarily large `K`,” meaning fixed-by-fixed: for every desired fixed moment one assumes the corresponding finite collection of Hardy--Littlewood estimates and then invokes the method of moments. It does not assert one simultaneous bound for `K\to\infty`.

There are three separate obstructions to reading it as a growing-order theorem.

1. The range `(2.6)` would require, formally,
   \[
   K\le \frac{\log N}{\log H}.                          \tag{2.7}
   \]
2. Even if all hidden constants were uniform, the first relative error in `(2.5)` tends to zero only if
   \[
   \frac{\log(H/\log N)}{K}\longrightarrow\infty.       \tag{2.8}
   \]
3. The Hardy--Littlewood remainder `(2.3)` must hold simultaneously for every set of up to `K` shifts. For `k=1`, the stated square-root error is already equivalent to RH; for larger `k` it is a very strong uniform prime-tuples hypothesis.

Combining `(2.7)` and `(2.8)`, a hypothetical uniformized version would require roughly

\[
K=o\!\left(
\min\left\{\log(H/\log N),\frac{\log N}{\log H}\right\}
\right).                                               \tag{2.9}
\]

Examples:

- If `H=(\log N)^A` with fixed `A>1`, then `(2.8)` forces
  \[
  K=o(\log\log N).                                      \tag{2.10}
  \]
- If `H=\exp((\log N)^\alpha)`, then the two displayed constraints give
  \[
  K=o\bigl((\log N)^{\min(\alpha,1-\alpha)}\bigr).      \tag{2.11}
  \]
  Optimizing at `\alpha=1/2` gives at most `K=o(\sqrt{\log N})`, before tracking hidden constants and the second error term.

Thus **Montgomery--Soundararajan do not prove, or even formally furnish through their displayed errors, a `K\asymp\log N` moment theorem**.

### What is unconditional in that paper?

Their Theorems 1 and 2 give unconditional combinatorial estimates for centered moments of reduced residues and averages of the modified singular series. They hold for every fixed integer `k`, with constants `O_k` and error exponents such as `1/(7k)`. These are essential ingredients in the prime moment calculation, but the passage to actual primes uses `(2.3)`. The prime short-interval Gaussian moments are therefore conditional. Chan's related higher-moment equivalences under RH are also fixed-order results; see T. H. Chan, *Higher moments of primes in short intervals I*, arXiv:math/0409530.

### Relevance to the Apéry problem

The analogy is exact at the structural level:

- their distinct shift set `\mathcal D` corresponds to a distinct prime tuple `(p_1,\ldots,p_k)`;
- their Hardy--Littlewood remainder corresponds to
  \[
  C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k)
  -N_X^{1-k}\prod_i|A_{p_i,X}|;                         \tag{2.12}
  \]
- their averaging of singular series corresponds to classifying forced Apéry diagonals such as reflection or other exact zero-set symmetries.

What is missing is precisely the analogue of `(2.3)` with constants controlled as `k` grows.

## 2.2 Granville--Soundararajan: the closest general sieve theorem

The most relevant abstract result is Andrew Granville and K. Soundararajan, *Sieving and the Erdős--Kac theorem*, arXiv:math/0606039.

Let `\mathcal A` be a multiset of size `x`, and suppose

\[
\mathcal A_d:=\#\{a\in\mathcal A:d\mid a\}
 =x\frac{h(d)}d+r_d.                                    \tag{2.13}
\]

For a set `P` of primes, define

\[
\mu_P=\sum_{p\in P}\frac{h(p)}p,
\qquad
\sigma_P^2=\sum_{p\in P}\frac{h(p)}p
\left(1-\frac{h(p)}p\right).                            \tag{2.14}
\]

Their Proposition 3 says, for even `k\le\sigma_P^{2/3}`,

\[
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}(\omega_P(a)-\mu_P)^k
=C_kx\sigma_P^k
 \left(1+O\left(\frac{k^3}{\sigma_P^2}\right)\right)
+O\left(
 \mu_P^k\sum_{d\in\mathcal D_k(P)}|r_d|
 \right),                                               \tag{2.15}
\]

with a corresponding upper bound for odd `k`. Here `\mathcal D_k(P)` is the set of squarefree products of at most `k` primes from `P`, and `C_k` is the Gaussian moment. Proposition 4 gives the analogous weighted statement for strongly additive functions.

This theorem exposes the exact high-order cost: one must control the total remainder over **all products of at most `k` local conditions**. It is the multiplicative analogue of our desired bound on

\[
\sum_{p_1,\ldots,p_k}
\left(
C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k)-\text{independent benchmark}
\right).                                               \tag{2.16}
\]

For ordinary integers, `\sigma_P^2\asymp\log\log x`, so

\[
k\le\sigma_P^{2/3}\asymp(\log\log x)^{1/3}.            \tag{2.17}
\]

Their Theorem 1 is unconditional in that range for `\omega(n)`. The same sieve proposition, combined with Bombieri--Vinogradov, recovers Halberstam's Erdős--Kac theorem for shifted primes `p-1`. But neither the range nor the mechanism approaches `k\asymp\log X`.

For Apéry rows there is no multiplicative object `d=\prod p_i` for which a relation like `(2.13)` factorizes. The local condition attached to `p_i` is

\[
n-p_i\in Z_{p_i},                                      \tag{2.18}
\]

and a collection of such conditions is an exact additive alignment, not divisibility by a product modulus. Thus Proposition 3 is a blueprint for the bookkeeping, not an applicable theorem.

## 2.3 Sathe--Selberg, Selberg--Delange, and prime-divisor Poisson laws

There are two different parameters that are often conflated.

1. In Sathe--Selberg, `j` is the **value** of `\omega(m)`. The classical local asymptotic is uniform in a range roughly
   \[
   j\le(2-\delta)\log\log X.                            \tag{2.19}
   \]
2. In a moment theorem, `k` is the **order of the moment**. Granville--Soundararajan's explicit uniform centered-moment range is only `(\log\log X)^{1/3}`.

The Selberg--Delange transform has the schematic form

\[
\frac1X\sum_{m\le X}z^{\omega(m)}
=(\log X)^{z-1}\Phi(z)\{1+o(1)\},                      \tag{2.20}
\]

uniformly for `z` in a fixed compact set. This gives mod-Poisson convergence and precise large deviations on the natural `\log\log X` scale. A modern discussion of the Sathe--Selberg/mod-Poisson interface is Y. Barhoumi-Andréani, *A penalised model reproducing the mod-Poisson fluctuations in the Sathe--Selberg theorem*, arXiv:1701.03432.

However, extracting derivatives of order `k\asymp\log X` from `(2.20)` would require control of the transform on contours whose radius grows with `k`; fixed-compact Selberg--Delange uniformity does not provide this. More fundamentally, the Euler product makes the divisibility events nearly independent. Our events link the modulus `p` to the shifted index `n-p`, so there is no Euler product or independent-prime decomposition.

These theorems are therefore excellent evidence for what a successful factorial-moment proof should look like, but they do not address the cross-characteristic twist.

## 2.4 Sieve weights and fixed higher correlations

Goldston--Yıldırım computed fixed higher correlations of truncated divisor-sum approximations to `\Lambda`; see

- *Higher correlations of divisor sums related to primes I: triple correlations*, arXiv:math/0111212;
- *Higher correlations of divisor sums related to primes III: k-correlations*, arXiv:math/0209102.

These are important precedents for expanding a `k`-fold product, organizing diagonal partitions, and evaluating singular-series main terms. But `k` is an external fixed parameter; the papers do not provide a theorem uniform for `k\asymp\log X`. GPY/Maynard/Polymath sieve dimensions can be chosen large for a fixed target number of primes, but in the asymptotic theorem the dimension is fixed before `X\to\infty`, and the constants grow rapidly.

## 2.5 Which precedent is actually closest to `k\asymp\log X`?

The answer is: **none unconditionally**.

| Framework | Uniform order actually available | What supplies independence? | Main mismatch here |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Montgomery--Soundararajan | fixed `K`; formal displayed errors much smaller than `\log N` | quantitative Hardy--Littlewood for all shift sets | hypothesis is itself the desired high-order joint law |
| Granville--Soundararajan sieve | `k\le\sigma^{2/3}`, hence `(\log\log X)^{1/3}` for `\omega` | multiplicative sieve remainders `r_d` | no product-modulus factorization |
| Sathe--Selberg/Selberg--Delange | values `\omega(n)=j` up to `j\asymp\log\log X`; transform on fixed `z`-compacta | Euler product | wrong parameter and wrong structure |
| Goldston--Yıldırım/GPY | fixed correlation order | divisor-sum expansion | constants and diagonals not uniform to `\log X` |
| FKM trace products | fixed number of factors over one `\mathbf F_p` | geometric monodromy and Deligne | different fields, zero detector, conductor growth |
| Polymath8 dense divisibility | many factors inside one smooth modulus, with fixed analytic decomposition | CRT plus Type I/II/III distribution | interval undersamples product modulus; sets are not residue classes of a common global object |

The closest conceptual statement is a **uniform Hardy--Littlewood conjecture for the family of rows `p+Z_p`**, expressed as the aggregate quasi-independence hypothesis `(QI)` below.

## 3. High-order additive-energy technology

## 3.1 Same-group additive energy and Gowers norms

For subsets of one finite abelian group, high additive energies and Gowers norms are standard tools. Large energy can be converted, through Balog--Szemerédi--Gowers, dependent random choice, or inverse Gowers theorems, into approximate algebraic structure. The Green--Tao--Ziegler inverse theorem, arXiv:1009.3998, is the flagship high-order structural result.

This technology is primarily **inverse**:

\[
\text{large energy}\Longrightarrow\text{structured obstruction}.         \tag{3.1}
\]

It does not give an upper bound until one separately proves that the structured obstruction cannot occur. In our setting the rows live naturally in different groups `\mathbf F_p`, and after embedding them into `I_X` the most dangerous structure is precisely a common-core or “sunflower” configuration:

\[
n_0\in A_p\quad\text{for many }p.                         \tag{3.2}
\]

That is not excluded by row cardinalities, reflection, non-consecutive zeros, or generic behavior away from `n_0`.

A plausible use of additive combinatorics would therefore be **contrapositive**:

1. show that excessively large `M_k(X)` forces a low-complexity common-core or additive pattern among many rows;
2. prove an Apéry-specific characteristic-zero theorem excluding that pattern.

Step 2 is currently exactly the missing cross-prime arithmetic input. Moreover, quantitative inverse-theorem constants deteriorate rapidly with the Gowers order, and no available result is designed for order `\asymp\log X` across varying prime fields.

## 3.2 Fouvry--Kowalski--Michel multi-correlations

The central primary source is É. Fouvry, E. Kowalski, and Ph. Michel, *A study in sums of products*, arXiv:1405.2293.

Their Proposition 1.1 considers a fixed `k`-tuple of middle-extension sheaves `\mathcal F_i` on `\mathbf A^1_{\mathbf F_p}` and a sheaf `\mathcal G`. If

\[
H_c^2\!\left(
\mathbf A^1_{\overline{\mathbf F}_p},
\bigotimes_{i=1}^k\mathcal F_i\otimes D(\mathcal G)
\right)=0,                                              \tag{3.3}
\]

then the corresponding complete sum of a product of trace functions has square-root cancellation. The implied constant depends only on `k` and the conductors. The paper develops Goursat--Kolchin--Ribet criteria and “normal tuple” conditions that classify the diagonal cases where `(3.3)` fails.

This is exactly the geometric pattern one would want:

\[
\text{generic tuple}\Rightarrow\sqrt p\text{ cancellation},
\qquad
\text{exceptional tuple}\Rightarrow\text{explicit diagonal}.             \tag{3.4}
\]

There are, however, four decisive mismatches.

### (a) The zero indicator is not a bounded-conductor trace function

The formal additive-character identity is

\[
1_{b_r=0\bmod p}
 =\frac1p\sum_{t\in\mathbf F_p}e_p(tb_r).                \tag{3.5}
\]

Alternatively, over `\mathbf F_p`, it is the polynomial `1-b_r^{p-1}`. Both representations have complexity growing with `p` unless `r\mapsto b_r` is itself realized by a very special bounded-conductor sheaf and the `t`-average can be controlled uniformly. Presently `b_r` as a function of the **coefficient index** is not known to be a bounded-conductor trace function. The K3/hypergeometric motive controls fibers or Hasse data, but coefficient selection is a different operation.

### (b) The factors live over different fields

Our correlation `(1.4)` contains

\[
1_{Z_{p_i}}(z+p_1-p_i),
\qquad i=1,\ldots,k,                                    \tag{3.6}
\]

with each factor defined modulo a different prime `p_i`. FKM sums products over one `\mathbf F_p`. Passing to the CRT ring modulo `Q=p_1\cdots p_k` does not create a finite field, and the interval length is only `X`, while `Q\asymp X^k`.

### (c) Completion loses the problem

If one periodically extends each detector,

\[
1_{Z_p}(n\bmod p)
 =\frac1p\sum_{a\bmod p}\widehat{1_{Z_p}}(a)e(an/p),     \tag{3.7}
\]

then a `k`-fold product has common period `Q=p_1\cdots p_k`. Complete orthogonality occurs only over length `Q`, whereas we sum over an interval of length `X\ll Q`. At order two this is the familiar `Q^2` localization/large-sieve wall recorded in the workspace; at growing order the mismatch becomes worse.

### (d) The dependence on `k` is not in a usable form

Proposition 1.1 states that the constant depends on `k` and the conductors; it is not a theorem with explicit factorial-type control uniform for `k=c\log p`. Tensor ranks, Betti numbers, and the number of exceptional partitions all grow with `k`. The combinatorial count of partitions is of size `\exp(O(k\log k))`, which would be acceptable, but the geometric constants and conductor of the zero detector are not currently controlled on that scale.

The recent Fouvry--Kowalski--Michel--Sawin preprint *Bilinear forms with trace functions*, arXiv:2511.09459, develops a more robust stratification and Goursat--Kolchin--Ribet mechanism. It remains a fixed-auxiliary-moment, one-characteristic theory; it does not remove the four obstructions above.

The FKM inverse theorem for Gowers norms of trace functions, arXiv:1211.3282, is relevant only after a bounded-conductor trace-function model is found.

## 3.3 What an FKM-style proof would have to establish

A viable geometric program would need all of the following.

1. A compatible family of functions `K_p(r)` of conductor bounded independently of `p` and a bounded-complexity majorant
   \[
   1_{Z_p}(r)\le W_p(r)                                  \tag{3.8}
   \]
   with the correct mass `\sum_rW_p(r)=O(1)` or `p^{o(1)}`.
2. A cross-characteristic correlation theorem for the integer restrictions
   \[
   W_{p_i}(n-p_i),                                       \tag{3.9}
   \]
   not merely complete sums in one field.
3. A classification of all exceptional `k`-tuples whose total contribution is at most
   \[
   C^k(k!)^A                                             \tag{3.10}
   \]
   times the independent main term.
4. Explicit constants with
   \[
   \log C_k=O(k\log k),                                  \tag{3.11}
   \]
   if the goal is a polylogarithmic maximum at `k\asymp\log X`.

At present even item 1 is missing. Thus citing Deligne/FKM alone is not a proof roadmap; the difficult theorem must be stated as one of these interfaces.

## 3.4 Polymath8 dense divisibility

The relevant Polymath8 papers are

- *New equidistribution estimates of Zhang type*, arXiv:1402.0811;
- *Variants of the Selberg sieve, and bounded intervals containing many primes*, arXiv:1407.4897.

Dense divisibility permits a large smooth squarefree modulus to be factored at many useful scales, enabling Type I/II/III estimates for primes in CRT residue classes. The method succeeds because the arithmetic condition remains a congruence modulo one composite modulus and the summation ranges can exploit its factorization.

For a fixed Apéry prime tuple, the equations are

\[
n=p_i+z_i,
\qquad z_i\in Z_{p_i}.                                  \tag{3.12}
\]

They imply a residue class modulo `Q=\prod p_i`, but the interval has length `X`, so for `k\ge2` it samples at most one representative of most CRT classes. Factoring `Q` does not create averaging over the missing representatives. In addition, the permitted residue set modulo `p_i` is itself an arithmetically moving set `p_i+Z_{p_i}`, not a fixed smooth congruence class.

Polymath8 technology would become relevant only after a new representation converts the Apéry zero condition into a bilinear/multilinear sequence with distributable coefficients. In the current formulation it does not yield a high-order energy bound.

## 3.5 Honest positive conclusion from the technology survey

The literature suggests the following **shape**, but not the theorem itself:

1. expand factorial moments exactly;
2. isolate diagonal/forced configurations;
3. prove square-root or power-saving cancellation for generic configurations;
4. track constants as `k` grows;
5. sum all generic and exceptional tuples.

Montgomery--Soundararajan provide steps 1--2 conditional on prime-tuples input; FKM provides steps 2--3 for fixed products in one field; Granville--Soundararajan explains the required aggregate remainder ledger. The Apéry problem needs a new theorem combining all three in a cross-characteristic setting.

## 4. Honest obstruction analysis

## 4.1 Row marginals cannot prevent a spike

Even the ideal row bound `|Z_p|=1` does not imply a pointwise result. Fix an integer `n_0`. An adversarial family may place

\[
n_0-p\in Z_p
\]

for every prime `p\in(n_0/2,n_0]`. Then

\[
H(n_0)\sim\frac{n_0}{2\log n_0},                        \tag{4.1}
\]

although every row has one point. Reflection can be enforced by adding the reflected point, still with bounded row size. Thus the no-go theorem for row information is exactly compatible with the moment formulation.

## 4.2 Pairwise information can miss a sparse common core

A family can be random away from one common point and still have a huge high-order codegree. Pairwise statistics averaged over a large universe can look acceptable while a single “sunflower center” survives. A density theorem permits such a center if it controls only the number of exceptional `n` and not the full contribution

\[
\sum_n(H(n))_2.                                         \tag{4.2}
\]

The factorial moment of order `k` amplifies a common core of degree `T` by `(T)_k`; this is why a uniform high-order bound is a natural anti-sunflower hypothesis.

## 4.3 Per-tuple relative asymptotics are impossible

Under the independent model, a fixed `k`-tuple with bounded row sizes has expected intersection

\[
\asymp X^{1-k}.                                         \tag{4.3}
\]

For `k\ge2` this is below one. Since an actual intersection is an integer, no uniform estimate

\[
C_X(p_1,\ldots,p_k)\ll X^{1-k}                          \tag{4.4}
\]

can hold for every tuple that happens to intersect. The correct target must be **aggregate** after summing prime tuples, with a separate classification of exceptional tuples. This is the same reason Hardy--Littlewood moment arguments average singular series rather than demand an error smaller than the main term for each rare pattern.

## 4.4 The constants are part of the theorem

Suppose

\[
M_k(X)\le X\,B_k.                                       \tag{4.5}
\]

At `k=c\log X`, one gets

\[
\max H\lesssim \log X+B_k^{1/k}.                       \tag{4.6}
\]

Therefore:

- `B_k\le C^k(k!)^A=\exp(O(k\log k))` gives a polylogarithmic maximum;
- `B_k=\exp(O(k^2))` gives at best `X^{O(c)}` if `k=c\log X`, though choosing a smaller growing order may still prove `o(X/\log X)`;
- an unspecified `O_k(1)` is useless for a growing-order theorem.

Every proposed FKM, sieve, or combinatorial proof must include an explicit `k`-dependence ledger.

## 4.5 Fixed-order CRT amplification is not this theorem

The workspace note `Q32_GROWING_CRT_ENTROPY_2026-07-28.md` proves that the pure signed-CRT construction remains entropy-critical even when its order grows up to `\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor-2`. It **generates** many simultaneous-hit indices but does not give an upper bound on how many generated data can collide.

The present factorial-moment route asks for the complementary statement:

\[
\text{an upper bound on the number of simultaneous-hit configurations}.   \tag{4.7}
\]

Thus it is not refuted by the growing-CRT no-go; it is precisely the additional cross-prime compression theorem that the no-go says is absent.

Likewise, the rational “evaluated moments” in `Q32_GROWING_EVALUATED_MOMENT_FAMILY_2026-07-30.md` are different objects. Their obstruction is denominator height and loss of target divisibility. The statistical factorial moments `(1.2)` have no such denominator, but require a new correlation bound.

## 5. Clean conditional theorems

## 5.1 Deterministic moment-to-maximum lemma

Let `I` be a finite set, `H:I\to\mathbf Z_{\ge0}`, and

\[
M_k=\sum_{n\in I}(H(n))_k.
\]

Let `h=\max_IH`. For every `1\le k\le h`,

\[
(h)_k\le M_k.                                           \tag{5.1}
\]

Since

\[
(h)_k=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(h-j)\ge(h-k+1)^k,               \tag{5.2}
\]

we have the exact inequality

\[
\boxed{
 h\le k-1+M_k^{1/k}.
}                                                        \tag{5.3}
\]

If `h<k`, the same upper bound is automatic. This one-line lemma is the complete deterministic endpoint of the method.

## 5.2 Direct factorial-moment criterion

### Theorem A

Assume that for constants `c,C>0` and `A\ge0`, for every sufficiently large `X` and every integer

\[
1\le k\le c\log X,                                     \tag{5.4}
\]

one has

\[
\frac1{N_X}M_k(X)\le C^k(k!)^A.                        \tag{5.5}
\]

Then

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)\ll_{c,C,A}
(\log X)^{\max(1,A)}.                                  \tag{5.6}
\]

#### Proof

Take `k=\lfloor c\log X\rfloor`. By `(5.3)`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\max H
&\le k-1+\bigl(N_XC^k(k!)^A\bigr)^{1/k}\\
&\le k+N_X^{1/k}C(k!)^{A/k}.                            \tag{5.7}
\end{aligned}
\]

Here `N_X^{1/k}=O_c(1)` and `(k!)^{1/k}\le k`, so

\[
\max H\ll \log X+(\log X)^A.                           \tag{5.8}
\]

This proves `(5.6)`. `\square`

For `A=1`, the hypothesis in the question gives `H(n)\ll\log n`; for general fixed `A`, it gives a polylogarithmic maximum.

## 5.3 Row-normalized quasi-independence

The direct criterion ignores that the mean intensity should be about `1/\log X`. The natural hypothesis is the following.

### Hypothesis AQI(`c,C,A`)

For every `1\le k\le c\log X`,

\[
\boxed{
M_k(X)
 \le C^k(k!)^A N_X^{1-k}S_X^k.
}                                                        \tag{5.9}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\frac{M_k(X)}{N_X}\le C^k(k!)^A\lambda_X^k.             \tag{5.10}
\]

Written directly in terms of zero sets, this is

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\in\mathcal P_X\\p_i\text{ distinct}}}
 \sum_{z\in Z_{p_1}}1_{I_X}(p_1+z)
 \prod_{i=2}^k1_{Z_{p_i}}(z+p_1-p_i)\\
&\hspace{20mm}\le
 C^k(k!)^A N_X^{1-k}
 \left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_X}|(p+Z_p)\cap I_X|\right)^k.   \tag{5.11}
\end{aligned}
\]

This is the cleanest conditional theorem to isolate as a separate research target.

### Theorem B

Assume AQI(`c,C,A`) and the first-intensity estimate

\[
S_X\le L\frac{N_X}{\log X}.                             \tag{5.12}
\]

Then

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)\ll_{c,C,A,L}
\log X+(\log X)^{A-1}.                                  \tag{5.13}
\]

In particular it is polylogarithmic. If `A<2`, then the sharper bound

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)\ll_{C,A,L}
\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}                               \tag{5.14}
\]

holds.

#### Proof of `(5.13)`

Use `k=\lfloor c\log X\rfloor` in `(5.3)` and `(5.9)`:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\max H
&\le k+N_X^{1/k}C(k!)^{A/k}\frac{S_X}{N_X}\\
&\ll \log X+\frac{k^A}{\log X},                         \tag{5.15}
\end{aligned}
\]

which is `(5.13)`.

#### Proof of `(5.14)`

If `H(n)\ge k`, then `(H(n))_k\ge k!`, hence `M_k(X)\ge k!`. But `(5.9)` and `(5.12)` give

\[
\frac{M_k(X)}{k!}
\le N_X\left(\frac{CL}{\log X}\right)^k(k!)^{A-1}.      \tag{5.16}
\]

Choose

\[
k=\left\lceil D\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}\right\rceil,
\qquad D>\frac1{2-A}.                                   \tag{5.17}
\]

Stirling's formula gives

\[
\log\frac{M_k(X)}{k!}
\le \{1-D(2-A)+o(1)\}\log X<0.                          \tag{5.18}
\]

Thus `M_k(X)<k!`, so no `n` has `H(n)\ge k`. `\square`

For a strict Poisson factorial moment (`A=0`), the threshold constant is asymptotically `1/2`; for the question's `k!` overhead (`A=1`), any `D>1` works.

## 5.4 The genuinely minimal one-order hypothesis

Uniformity through `c\log X` is convenient but not logically minimal. From `(5.3)` we have:

### Theorem C

Let `K=K(X)` be any positive integer. If

\[
K=o(X/\log X)                                           \tag{5.19}
\]

and

\[
M_K(X)^{1/K}=o(X/\log X),                               \tag{5.20}
\]

then

\[
\max_{n\in I_X}H(n)=o(X/\log X).                        \tag{5.21}
\]

This is essentially the weakest pure moment statement: it is just `(5.3)`.

A simple sufficient form is

\[
K(X)\to\infty,
\qquad
M_K(X)\le N_XC^K(K!)^A.                                \tag{5.22}
\]

Indeed

\[
\max H\le K+C\,N_X^{1/K}K^A.                           \tag{5.23}
\]

If `K\to\infty` and `K=X^{o(1)}`, then `N_X^{1/K}=X^{o(1)}`, so

\[
\max H=X^{o(1)},                                        \tag{5.24}
\]

far stronger than the pointwise conjecture. For example, `K=\lfloor\log\log X\rfloor` is already enough.

This gives a much more realistic staged goal:

1. first prove factorial-scale bounds for `K(X)\to\infty` arbitrarily slowly;
2. then quantify them to `K\asymp\log\log X`;
3. only then target `K\asymp\log X` for a polylogarithmic maximum.

## 5.5 Weighted AQI: the robust form for pruning and dyadic decompositions

For nonnegative weights `w_p`, define

\[
M_k(X;w)=
\sum_{n\in I_X}
\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\\\text{distinct}}}
\prod_{i=1}^kw_{p_i}1_{A_{p_i,X}}(n).                   \tag{5.25}
\]

A robust hypothesis is

\[
M_k(X;w)
\le C^k(k!)^A N_X^{1-k}
 \left(\sum_pw_p|A_{p,X}|\right)^k                      \tag{5.26}
\]

for all nonnegative weights and `k\le K(X)`. This is stronger than needed for the unweighted theorem, but it is stable under:

- deleting large or exceptional rows;
- restricting primes to short subintervals;
- separating reflection/central branches;
- applying a majorant in place of the exact zero indicator.

A trace-function or sieve proof would naturally aim for `(5.26)`.

## 6. A realistic proof program

## Phase 0: identify exactly what the current second-moment theorem controls

Before building higher moments, record whether the proved `X^{2/3+o(1)}` theorem bounds

\[
\#\{n:H(n)>T\},                                         \tag{6.1}
\]

a truncated moment, or the full global sum `M_2(X)`. If it bounded `M_2(X)\le X^{2-\delta}`, the pointwise conjecture would already follow from `(1.9)`. The unresolved term should be written explicitly as the contribution of the exceptional spikes.

## Phase 1: first intensity and row truncation

Establish, or isolate as a hypothesis,

\[
S_X=\sum_p|A_{p,X}|\ll X/\log X.                        \tag{6.2}
\]

If only weaker information on `|Z_p|` is available, split

\[
\mathcal P_X=\mathcal P_X^{\rm small}\sqcup
\mathcal P_X^{\rm large}                               \tag{6.3}
\]

according to row size. Prove that large rows contribute negligibly to every target moment, then formulate AQI for bounded rows. The pointwise row bound `|Z_p|\ll p^{2/3}` alone is not enough; an averaged tail estimate for `|Z_p|` is needed.

## Phase 2: prove a fixed-`k` correlation theorem with an explicit diagonal classification

For each fixed `k`, seek

\[
M_k(X)\ll_k N_X^{1-k}S_X^k+E_k(X).                       \tag{6.4}
\]

The theorem must identify all forced configurations:

- repeated primes, already removed by the falling factorial;
- reflection-related zero coordinates;
- central zeros;
- any Frobenius or modular symmetry forcing equality of two row points.

The generic remainder `E_k(X)` must be expressed in a form that can later be summed uniformly in `k`. A statement merely `E_k=o_k(X)` is insufficient.

## Phase 3: track the `k`-dependence

The target budget for a polylogarithmic maximum is

\[
M_k(X)\le X\exp(O(k\log k))                             \tag{6.5}
\]

for `k\le c\log X`, or the row-normalized version `(5.9)`. Every component must respect this budget:

1. number of set partitions/diagonals: `\exp(O(k\log k))` -- acceptable;
2. geometric conductor and Betti constants: must be at most factorial-type;
3. exceptional tuple count: factorial-type relative to the independent benchmark;
4. Fourier/character expansions: cannot introduce `p^k` uncontrolled terms.

An `\exp(O(k^2))` constant may still prove the pointwise conjecture at a smaller order, but not the desired polylog result at `k=c\log X`.

## Phase 4: target slowly growing order first

The first meaningful new theorem should be

\[
M_{K(X)}(X)\le X C^{K(X)}(K(X)!)^A                     \tag{6.6}
\]

for some explicit `K(X)\to\infty`, even `K(X)=\log\log\log X`. By Theorem C this already gives `H(n)=X^{o(1)}`. This target is much closer to the fixed-`k` FKM/sieve literature than `K=c\log X` and creates a quantifiable bridge rather than a qualitative “for each fixed `k`” statement.

## Phase 5: possible analytic mechanisms

### 6.5.1 Aggregate Fourier majorants

Construct nonnegative majorants `W_p\ge1_{Z_p}` with

\[
\sum_rW_p(r)=O(1)                                       \tag{6.7}
\]

and controlled Fourier norms. Expand the weighted moment and seek a multilinear large-sieve inequality that exploits arithmetic cancellation in the coefficients, not merely frequency spacing. Standard large sieve alone pays the product-modulus localization cost and will not suffice.

### 6.5.2 Compatible global sheaf or motive

A successful geometric approach needs one global object whose reduction at `p` simultaneously controls the **coefficient-index function** `r\mapsto b_r\bmod p`. A family that only controls a fiber parameter or the central coefficient will not address `(1.4)`. Once such an object exists, use FKM normal-tuple classification and track the tensor conductor with `k`.

### 6.5.3 Cumulants/cluster expansion

Instead of estimating every factorial moment directly, bound sums of connected joint cumulants. A Poisson law follows if connected clusters of size `j` have total mass `\ll C^j j!^A\lambda_X^j`. This can be more economical because disconnected configurations automatically factor. But it still requires a cross-prime connected-correlation theorem; there is no sparse dependency graph for free.

### 6.5.4 Combinatorial inverse theorem for a spike

Prove that failure of AQI forces a structured subfamily of rows, for example many primes sharing several common integers or lying in a low-rank additive pattern. Then attack that structured configuration using Apéry recurrences, resultants, modular forms, or a global height argument. This is a plausible way to turn additive-combinatorial inverse technology into a useful dichotomy, but the arithmetic exclusion theorem remains to be discovered.

## Phase 6: sharp falsification criteria

A candidate proof route should be considered closed unless it supplies all three:

1. **cross-prime content:** it cannot be a theorem applied separately to each `Z_p`;
2. **aggregate rare-pattern control:** it must handle the integer granularity barrier `(4.3)`;
3. **explicit order dependence:** no bare `O_k(1)` at the final stage.

## 7. Direct answers to the three questions

### Answer to Question 1

- Montgomery--Soundararajan's prime moment theorem is conditional on the square-root Hardy--Littlewood remainder `(2.3)` uniformly for every shift set of size at most fixed `K`. It is stated for fixed `K`. The theorem's formal range is `H\le N^{1/K}`, and its displayed error additionally requires `K=o(\log(H/\log N))` for an asymptotic. For `H=(\log N)^A`, even a hypothetical fully uniform version would permit only `K=o(\log\log N)`; optimizing a subpower `H` gives at best about `o(\sqrt{\log N})`, not `c\log N`. The unconditional portion concerns singular-series/reduced-residue combinatorics for fixed `k`, not actual prime moments. [arXiv:math/0409258]
- Granville--Soundararajan Proposition 3 is the closest general sieve theorem. It requires the total sieve remainder over squarefree products of at most `k` primes and is uniform for `k\le\sigma_P^{2/3}`. For `\omega(n)`, this is `(\log\log X)^{1/3}`. [arXiv:math/0606039]
- Sathe--Selberg controls the value of `\omega(n)` on the `\log\log X` scale; Selberg--Delange controls a transform in fixed compact `z`-ranges. Neither gives moment order `c\log X`. Halberstam's shifted-prime theorem retains the multiplicative sieve structure and the same log-log scale.
- Fixed higher correlations of divisor-sum weights are useful precedents for diagonal bookkeeping, but they do not provide `k\asymp\log X` uniformity. [arXiv:math/0111212, arXiv:math/0209102]
- No existing framework cited here comes close unconditionally to `k\asymp\log X`. The closest *conditional formulation* is a uniform Hardy--Littlewood/AQI hypothesis for the rows.

### Answer to Question 2

- The exact object is `(1.2)--(1.4)`, the high-order codegree of the set family `\{p+Z_p\}` in one integer interval.
- Same-group additive-energy and Gowers technology can classify structure caused by large energy, but it supplies no upper bound without an Apéry-specific theorem excluding that structure.
- FKM gives fixed-factor square-root cancellation for products of bounded-conductor trace functions in one finite field, with classified diagonal tuples. It does not apply directly because `1_{Z_p}` is not known to have bounded conductor, the zero detector introduces `p` auxiliary frequencies, and different factors live in different characteristics. [arXiv:1405.2293, arXiv:1211.3282]
- The newer bilinear trace-function machinery does not change the fixed-order/cross-characteristic issue. [arXiv:2511.09459]
- Polymath8 dense divisibility controls congruence classes to factorizable smooth moduli; it cannot overcome the fact that an interval of length `X` undersamples a CRT modulus of size `X^k`. [arXiv:1402.0811, arXiv:1407.4897]
- No existing theorem gives the needed uniformity in `k`. A successful import from FKM would require a new global compatible sheaf/majorant and factorial-type control of all geometric constants.

### Answer to Question 3

The cleanest hypothesis is AQI:

\[
\sum_{\substack{p_1,\ldots,p_k\\\mathrm{distinct}}}
|(p_1+Z_{p_1})\cap\cdots\cap(p_k+Z_{p_k})\cap I_X|
\le C^k(k!)^A N_X^{1-k}
\left(\sum_p|(p+Z_p)\cap I_X|\right)^k               \tag{7.1}
\]

for `1\le k\le c\log X`, together with

\[
\sum_p|(p+Z_p)\cap I_X|\ll X/\log X.                  \tag{7.2}
\]

Then `\max_{I_X}H` is polylogarithmic by Theorem B; for `A<2` it is `O(\log X/\log\log X)`.

If one wants the logically weakest pure-moment theorem, it is simply:

\[
K+M_K(X)^{1/K}=o(X/\log X)                              \tag{7.3}
\]

for one order `K=K(X)`. A convenient sufficient condition is

\[
K(X)\to\infty,
\qquad
M_K(X)\le X C^K(K!)^A.                                 \tag{7.4}
\]

This already yields `\max H=X^{o(1)}`. Thus the first attack should seek any effective growing order, not necessarily `c\log X`.
