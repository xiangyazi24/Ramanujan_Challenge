# The reflected Apéry zero pair at the next p-adic layer
## Verdict
I do **not** obtain an aggregate bound for
$$
\mathcal L(n)=\sum_{\substack{\sqrt n<p\le n\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}\log p.
$$

Let
$$
s=p-1-t,\qquad h=s-t=p-1-2t,
$$

$$
p\mid b_t,\qquad p\mid b_s.
$$
Write
$$
b_t=p\beta_t,\qquad b_s=p\beta_s,
$$
and let
$$
\dot b_t:=2\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom tk^2\binom{t+k}{k}^2
\bigl(H_{t+k}-H_{t-k}\bigr)

be Gessel's derivative sequence. Its denominator is a $`p`$-unit for $`0\le t<p`$.

\boxed{
C_h(t)b_{t+1}\equiv-\,Q_h(t)\dot b_t\pmod p,
}
\tag{V}
$$
where
$$
Q_h(t)=\prod_{j=t+2}^{s}j^3,
\qquad
N_h(t)=pC_h(t).
$$
Both $`Q_h(t)`$ and $`b_{t+1}`$ are units modulo $`p`$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{
C_h(t)\equiv0\pmod p
\iff
\dot b_t\equiv0\pmod p
\iff
b_t\equiv b_{p-1-t}\pmod{p^2}.
}
\tag{VI}
$$
Thus the quotient $`C_h(t)`$ is not a second zero certificate. It is, up to an explicit unit, **exactly the first p-adic reflection defect**. The example from Q342,
$$
(p,t,h)=(17,3,10),\qquad C_{10}(3)\equiv2\pmod{17},
$$
is explained by
$$
\dot b_3=4438\equiv1\pmod{17}.
$$

$$
D_h(t)=(t+1)^3N_{h-1}(t+1),
\qquad
E_{p,t}=\frac{D_h(t)+Q_h(t)}p,

and

\kappa_{p,t}

then the exact identity is

\boxed{
C_h(t)b_{t+1}

}
\tag{VII}
$$
If $`\dot b_t=p\delta_t`$, so that one may divide $`C_h(t)`$ by $`p`$, then
$$
\boxed{
\frac{C_h(t)}p\,b_{t+1}
=-Q_h(t)\delta_t+E_{p,t}\beta_t+Q_h(t)\kappa_{p,t}.
}
\tag{VIII}
$$
There is no closure: each further division exposes a new normalized layer. Ordinary Lucas congruences and the known supercongruences do not constrain those layers uniformly at a moving interior digit.

p\mid b_t,\quad p\mid b_s,\quad
b_t-b_s\equiv p\dot b_t\pmod{p^2},

the pair $`(\beta_t,\beta_s)`$ has the form

with a formally free common coordinate $`u`$. Any normalized endpoint certificate that eliminates $`u`$ is therefore a function only of the derivative defect $`\dot b_t`$; any certificate that does not eliminate $`u`$ depends on an unconstrained $`\beta`$-layer. Products over several reflected pairs merely replace this by a polynomial in the several derivative defects.
This is the requested sharp obstruction. It does not prove that the actual Apéry $`\beta`$-layers are statistically independent; it proves that **zero divisibility, reflection, the continuant transfer law, companion values, and the complete mod-**$`p^2`$** Lucas theorem supply no further relation among them**.

# 1. Exact mod-$`p^2`$ reflection law
The primary input is Gessel's refinement of the Lucas congruence. For a prime $`p`$, $`0\le d<p`$, and every integer $`m`$,
$$
\boxed{
 b_{d+pm}\equiv
 \bigl(b_d+pm\dot b_d\bigr)b_m\pmod{p^2}.
}
\tag{1.1}
$$
The extension to all $`m\in\mathbf Z`$, including the denominator statement for $`\dot b_d`$, is Theorem 8 of Rowland--Yassawi--Krattenthaler.
The entire Apéry interpolation satisfies
$$
A(-1-z)=A(z).
\tag{1.2}
$$
Take $`d=t`$ and $`m=-1`$ in (1.1). Since
$$
t-p=-1-(p-1-t)=-1-s,
$$
reflection gives $`A(t-p)=A(s)=b_s`$, while $`A(-1)=A(0)=1`$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
 b_s\equiv b_t-p\dot b_t\pmod{p^2}.
}
\tag{1.3}
$$
After writing $`b_t=p\beta_t`$ and $`b_s=p\beta_s`$, division by $`p`$ gives
$$
\boxed{
\beta_t-\beta_s\equiv\dot b_t\pmod p.
}
\tag{1.4}
$$
Applying the same argument with $`t`$ and $`s`$ interchanged gives
$$
\boxed{
\dot b_s\equiv-\dot b_t\pmod p.
}
\tag{1.5}
$$
Thus an oriented reflected pair has only one first-order defect.
Define the special digit set
$$
D(p)=\{0\le d<p:b_d\equiv b_{p-1-d}\pmod{p^2}\}.
$$
Equations (1.3)--(1.4) give the exact characterization
$$
\boxed{
d\in D(p)\iff\dot b_d\equiv0\pmod p.
}
\tag{1.6}
$$
Rowland--Yassawi--Krattenthaler prove that $`D(p)`$ is precisely the maximal set of base-$`p`$ digits on which the ordinary Lucas product lifts uniformly from modulo $`p`$ to modulo $`p^2`$. Hence the derivative in (V) is not an artifact of the continuant calculation: it is the complete arbitrary-digit mod-$`p^2`$ obstruction already isolated in the primary literature.

# 2. The normalized continuant theorem
Recall
$$
N_0(x)=0,\qquad N_1(x)=1,
$$
$$
N_{j+1}(x)=P(x+j)N_j(x)-(x+j)^6N_{j-1}(x),
$$
where
$$
P(x)=34x^3+51x^2+27x+5.
$$
For any solution $`y`$ of the Apéry recurrence, the exact transfer identity on the interval from $`t`$ to $`s=t+h`$ is
$$
N_h(t)y_{t+1}
=Q_h(t)y_s+D_h(t)y_t,
\tag{2.1}
$$
where
$$
Q_h(t)=\prod_{j=t+2}^{s}j^3,
\qquad
D_h(t)=(t+1)^3N_{h-1}(t+1).
\tag{2.2}
$$
For even $`h`$, Q342 proved the exact factorization
$$
N_h(x)=(2x+h+1)C_h(x).
\tag{2.3}
$$
Since $`2t+h+1=p`$,
$$
N_h(t)=pC_h(t).
\tag{2.4}
$$
The factor $`p`$ in (2.4) is forced for every reflected interval, whether or not its endpoints are zeros.
## 2.1 A coefficient identity from the companion solution
For $`0\le j<p`$, both $`a_j`$ and $`b_j`$ are $`p`$-integral. The audited palindromy of the two fundamental solutions gives
$$
a_s\equiv a_t\pmod p,
\qquad
b_s\equiv b_t\pmod p.
\tag{2.5}
$$
At a $`b`$-zero, the Wronskian
$$
a_{t+1}b_t-a_tb_{t+1}=\frac6{(t+1)^3}
\tag{2.6}
$$
reduces to
$$
-a_tb_{t+1}\equiv\frac6{(t+1)^3}\pmod p.
\tag{2.7}
$$
Therefore
$$
a_t\not\equiv0,\qquad b_{t+1}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{2.8}
$$
Apply (2.1) to $`y=a`$. By (2.4), the left side is zero modulo $`p`$, and by (2.5), $`a_s=a_t`$. Hence
$$
0\equiv\bigl(Q_h(t)+D_h(t)\bigr)a_t\pmod p.
$$
Using (2.8),
$$
\boxed{
D_h(t)\equiv-Q_h(t)\pmod p.
}
\tag{2.9}
$$
This step is where the companion solution is useful. It supplies a palindromic solution that is nonzero at a $`b`$-zero. It does not supply a new vanishing condition.
## 2.2 Proof of the quotient identity
Apply (2.1) to $`y=b`$ and substitute
$$
N_h(t)=pC_h(t),\quad b_t=p\beta_t,\quad b_s=p\beta_s.
$$
After division by $`p`$,
$$
C_h(t)b_{t+1}
=Q_h(t)\beta_s+D_h(t)\beta_t.
\tag{2.10}
$$
Reducing modulo $`p`$ and using (2.9),
$$
C_h(t)b_{t+1}
\equiv Q_h(t)(\beta_s-\beta_t)\pmod p.
$$
Equation (1.4) now gives
$$
\boxed{
C_h(t)b_{t+1}\equiv-\,Q_h(t)\dot b_t\pmod p.
}
\tag{2.11}
$$
Every factor in $`Q_h(t)`$ lies in $`\{1,\ldots,p-1\}`$, and $`b_{t+1}`$ is a unit by (2.8). This proves (VI).
Using (2.7), one may solve for the inverse of $`b_{t+1}`$ and obtain the companion form
$$
\boxed{
C_h(t)
\equiv
\frac{Q_h(t)(t+1)^3}{6}\,a_t\dot b_t
\pmod p.
}
\tag{2.12}
$$
The companion value $`a_t`$ is an explicit unit. Thus adjoining companion data only rescales the same derivative defect.

# 4. The next layer and why it does not close
Equation (2.9) implies that
$$
E_{p,t}:=\frac{D_h(t)+Q_h(t)}p
$$
is an integer. Equation (1.4) implies that
$$
\kappa_{p,t}
:=\frac{\beta_s-\beta_t+\dot b_t}{p}
$$
is $`p`$-integral. Thus
$$
D_h(t)=-Q_h(t)+pE_{p,t},
$$
$$
\beta_s=\beta_t-\dot b_t+p\kappa_{p,t}.
$$
Substitution in the exact equality (2.10) gives
$$
C_h(t)b_{t+1}
=-Q_h(t)\dot b_t
+p\bigl(E_{p,t}\beta_t+Q_h(t)\kappa_{p,t}\bigr),
$$
which is (VII).
Suppose the exceptional condition $`\dot b_t\equiv0\pmod p`$ holds. Write
$$
\dot b_t=p\delta_t.
$$
Then $`p\mid C_h(t)`$, and division of (VII) by $`p`$ yields
$$
\frac{C_h(t)}p b_{t+1}
=-Q_h(t)\delta_t+E_{p,t}\beta_t+Q_h(t)\kappa_{p,t}.
$$
Three pieces now appear:
- the normalized derivative $`\delta_t`$;
- the common endpoint layer $`\beta_t`$;
- the second reflection defect $`\kappa_{p,t}`$.
No one of these is forced to vanish by $`p\mid b_t`$.
For the $`p=17`$ example, one can already see the new defect explicitly. Modulo $`17^3=4913`$,
$$
b_{13}\equiv4896=17\cdot288,
$$
so
$$
\beta_{13}\equiv288\pmod{289}.
$$
Also
$$
\dot b_3=4438\equiv103\pmod{289},
\qquad
\beta_3=85.
$$
Therefore
$$
\kappa_{17,3}
\equiv
\frac{288-85+103}{17}
=18
\equiv1\pmod{17}.
$$
The next reflection defect is nonzero. This computation does not require a conjectural interpolation modulo $`17^3`$; it is the exact normalized remainder left by the proven mod-$`p^2`$ formula.
There is also a conceptual reason not to expect the complex interpolation to provide a uniform rational hierarchy. The first derivative at an integer is rational and is exactly Gessel's sequence, but higher Taylor coefficients involve multiple zeta values; for example the Apéry entire function satisfies
$$
A''(0)=2\zeta(2).
$$
Thus a mod-$`p^3`$ arbitrary-digit lift cannot simply be obtained by reducing the ordinary second derivative. It must introduce $`p`$-dependent finite harmonic sums, divided congruences, or unit-root data. Those are precisely additional local layers, not a cross-prime relation.

# 5. Formal obstruction for normalized endpoint certificates
The mod-$`p^2`$ data of one reflected zero pair is
$$
\beta_t-\beta_s=\dot b_t
\qquad\text{in }\mathbf F_p.
\tag{5.1}
$$
Set
$$
u=\beta_t,\qquad v=\beta_t-\beta_s=\dot b_t.
$$
Then
$$
(\beta_t,\beta_s)=(u,u-v).
\tag{5.2}
$$
The zero and reflection laws determine $`v`$ only through the derivative; they give no equation for $`u`$.
## Theorem 5.1: first-order completeness
Consider any expression constructed from the normalized endpoints $`\beta_t,\beta_s`$ whose value is required to be independent of the common shift
$$
(\beta_t,\beta_s)\longmapsto
(\beta_t+c,\beta_s+c).
$$
As a function on $`\mathbf F_p^2`$, it factors through
$$
\beta_t-\beta_s=\dot b_t.
$$
Equivalently, after passing to the variables $`(u,v)`$, every common-layer-independent function is a function of $`v`$ alone.
### Proof
The fibers of the difference map
$$
(\beta_t,\beta_s)\longmapsto\beta_t-\beta_s
$$
are exactly the common-translation orbits. A function constant on each orbit factors through the orbit space, which is the difference coordinate. For polynomial certificates, the same statement holds in the coordinate ring of functions on $`\mathbf F_p^2`$; over the algebraic additive-group action, the invariant ring is generated by the difference. The possible formal Artin--Schreier factors $`u^p-u`$ vanish identically on $`\mathbf F_p`$-valued layers and therefore cannot supply a nonzero certificate. $`\square`$
The continuant quotient is the canonical example: (2.11) says it is an explicit unit times $`v`$.
For $`m`$ reflected pairs, write
$$
(\beta_{t_i},\beta_{s_i})=(u_i,u_i-v_i),
\qquad
v_i=\dot b_{t_i}.
$$
A certificate independent of every common layer $`u_i`$ is a function only of
$$
(v_1,\ldots,v_m).
$$
In particular,
$$
\boxed{
\prod_{i=1}^{m}C_{h_i}(t_i)
\equiv
U_p(\mathbf t)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\dot b_{t_i}
\pmod p,
}
\tag{5.3}
$$
where $`U_p(\mathbf t)`$ is an explicit nonzero product of the $`Q_{h_i}(t_i)`$ and $`b_{t_i+1}^{-1}`$.
Therefore a product over several reflected pairs vanishes modulo $`p`$ only if at least one pair belongs to $`D(p)`$. Merely having many zero pairs supplies no such implication. Using both orientations also adds nothing, because $`\dot b_{s_i}=-\dot b_{t_i}`$.
This theorem has a precise scope. It does not rule out an unknown Apéry-specific relation among the actual values $`u_i`$ and $`v_i`$. It proves that no such relation follows from the currently available endpoint divisibility, reflection, transfer, Wronskian, companion-palindromy, or mod-$`p^2`$ Lucas identities. Discovering such a relation would itself be the needed new theorem.

# 6. Mod-$`p^2`$ Lucas at the moving global index
Let
$$
n=qp+r,\qquad0\le r<p,
$$
and suppose $`p\mid b_r`$. Write $`b_r=p\beta_r`$. Gessel's congruence (1.1) gives
$$
\boxed{
\frac{b_n}{p}
\equiv
b_q\bigl(\beta_r+q\dot b_r\bigr)
\pmod p.
}
\tag{6.1}
$$
Thus the mod-$`p^2`$ Lucas law transports the first normalized layer from the digit to the full index. It does not remove it.
- If $`b_q`$ is a unit, then $`v_p(b_n)=1`$ or $`\ge2`$ according as $`\beta_r+q\dot b_r`$ is nonzero or zero.
- If $`p\mid b_q`$, then (6.1) gives extra multiplicity, but again no restriction on the existence of the original digit zero.
The support condition $`p\mid b_n`$ is already decided modulo $`p`$. Formula (6.1) refines multiplicity, not the number of primes in the support.
## A sharp CRT obstruction
Normalized values of one global integer carry no cross-prime restriction by themselves.
Let $`p_1,\ldots,p_m`$ be distinct primes, and prescribe arbitrary residues
$$
\gamma_i\in\mathbf F_{p_i}.
$$
Put
$$
P=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_i.
$$
Choose $`M`$ by the Chinese remainder theorem so that
$$
M\equiv
\gamma_i\left(\frac{P}{p_i}\right)^{-1}\pmod{p_i}
$$
for every $`i`$, and set $`B=PM`$. Then
$$
\boxed{
\frac{B}{p_i}\equiv\gamma_i\pmod{p_i}
}
\tag{6.2}
$$
for all $`i`$.
One may choose $`0\le M<P`$, so
$$
0\le B<P^2,\qquad
\log B<2\sum_i\log p_i.
\tag{6.3}
$$
Hence arbitrary normalized divisor layers can be encoded at height only proportional to the total prime weight. This is exactly the scale available from the exponential height of $`b_n`$. The ordinary product formula or height of $`b_n`$ can therefore give a linear bound, but not the required $`o(n)`$ bound.
The lemma is not a counterexample using the Apéry sequence. Its role is logical: any proof that uses only
$$
p\mid b_n
\quad\text{and}\quad
b_n/p\pmod p
$$
cannot force cross-prime compression. It must use a genuinely Apéry-specific relation among those normalized residues.

# 7. Companion values do not add a new zero channel
At a digit zero, the exact Wronskian identity is
$$
p\,a_{t+1}\beta_t-a_tb_{t+1}
=\frac6{(t+1)^3}.
\tag{7.1}
$$
Modulo $`p`$, this says $`a_t`$ and $`b_{t+1}`$ are reciprocal units up to the explicit scalar $`-6/(t+1)^3`$. At the next level, (7.1) expresses $`\beta_t`$ as the normalized cancellation between two companion-unit terms. It does not force $`\beta_t`$ to vanish and does not couple different primes.
Combining the companion with the quotient gives (2.12),
$$
C_h(t)
\equiv
\frac{Q_h(t)(t+1)^3}{6}a_t\dot b_t
\pmod p.
$$
Therefore every first-order companion reformulation is again the derivative defect multiplied by a unit. A higher companion block congruence would necessarily introduce normalized companion layers analogous to $`\beta_t`$; without a cross-prime theorem for those layers, it cannot improve $`\mathcal L(n)`$.

# 8. What the known supercongruences do and do not say
The strongest uniform arbitrary-digit statement presently available is the mod-$`p^2`$ formula (1.1). It is complete: the derivative-zero set $`D(p)`$ is exactly the set of digits for which the ordinary Lucas product lifts uniformly modulo $`p^2`$.
There are much stronger $`p^3`$ supercongruences at special digits. For example, for $`p\ge5`$,
$$
b_{pm}\equiv b_m\pmod{p^3},
$$
and, using reflection,
$$
b_{pm+p-1}\equiv b_m\pmod{p^3}.
$$
These are boundary-digit statements $`d=0`$ and $`d=p-1`$. They do not control the moving interior digit
$$
r=n\bmod p,
$$
nor the reflected representative $`t=\min(r,p-1-r)`$.
I found no primary result giving a uniform formula modulo $`p^3`$ for arbitrary interior $`d`$ that eliminates the new normalized layers in (VII)--(VIII). The available higher congruences are consistent with the obstruction: special digits have extra structure, while the general digit already acquires the free derivative defect modulo $`p^2`$.

# 9. Consequence for the moving-target problem
After the already audited small-prime and companion-channel estimates, the target remains
$$
\mathcal L(n)
=
\sum_{\substack{\sqrt n<p\le n\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}\log p.
$$
The present calculation does not reduce this sum. It partitions its primes according to the first reflection defect:
$$
\mathcal P_0(n)
=
\{p:p\mid b_t,\ \dot b_t\equiv0\pmod p\},
$$
$$
\mathcal P_1(n)
=
\{p:p\mid b_t,\ \dot b_t\not\equiv0\pmod p\}.
$$
For $`p\in\mathcal P_0(n)`$, the quotient $`C_h(t)`$ acquires an additional factor $`p`$, but its next quotient depends on $`\delta_t,\beta_t,\kappa_{p,t}`$. For $`p\in\mathcal P_1(n)`$, $`C_h(t)`$ is a unit and the continuant route stops immediately.
The data behind the mod-$`p^2`$ digit theorem makes $`\mathcal P_1`$ the generic case: $`D(p)`$ often consists only of the two endpoints and the center, whereas a noncentral zero pair need not lie in $`D(p)`$. The exact prime $`17`$ example realizes this generic obstruction.
Thus neither repeated division nor multiplication over pairs gives an unconditional bound for $`\mathcal L(n)`$.

# 10. The weakest theorem that would reopen the route
There are two levels.
## 10.1 Weakest local lifting statement
The exact local condition is now known:
$$
p\mid C_h(t)
\iff
p\mid\dot b_t.
$$
Therefore the weakest statement that would make the quotient-divisibility route apply to all but negligible moving weight is
$$
\boxed{
\sum_{\substack{\sqrt n<p\le n\\p\mid b_t\\p\nmid\dot b_t}}
\log p=o(n).
}
\tag{10.1}
$$
Under (10.1), almost all relevant weight would lift from the forced factor $`N_h(t)=pC_h(t)`$ to a genuine second factor $`p\mid C_h(t)`$. This would **reopen** a nonzero resultant or norm attack on the lifted branch.
It would not by itself finish the proof, because the next quotient still contains (VIII). Moreover, (10.1) is contrary to the observed shape of $`D(p)`$ and is not the plausible route.
The stronger tempting statement
$$
p\mid b_t\Longrightarrow p\mid\dot b_t
$$
is already false at $`(p,t)=(5,1)`$ and $`(17,3)`$.
## 10.2 Weakest genuinely useful cross-prime certificate theorem
A theorem in the following form would close the moving-target sum.
> **Horizontal normalized-layer certificate.** There exist fixed $`\eta>0`$ and $`A\ge0`$ such that, for every $`n`$ and every dyadic block $`(X,2X]\subset(\sqrt n,n]`$, one can construct a nonzero integer $`R_{n,X}`$ from a $`p`$-independent algebraic relation among the actual layers
> $``$
> (t,dot b_t,beta_t,kappa_\{p,t\},ldots)
> $``$
> satisfying
> $``$
> prod_\{substack\{X\<ple2X\\pmid b_\{,nbmod p\}\}\}p
> mid R_\{n,X\},
> $``$
> and
> $``$
> log\|R_\{n,X\}\|
> ll X\^\{1-eta\}(log n)\^A.
> $``$
Indeed,
$$
\sum_{\substack{X<p\le2X\\p\mid b_{\,n\bmod p}}}\log p
\le\log|R_{n,X}|,
$$
and summing over dyadic $`X`$ gives
$$
\mathcal L(n)
\ll n^{1-\eta}(\log n)^{A+1}=o(n).
$$
Q342 showed that the raw continuant resultant cannot be such an $`R_{n,X}`$ because its local factor is forced. The present answer shows that the first quotient cannot be such an $`R_{n,X}`$ either: it is a unit times a generally nonzero derivative, and its next lift introduces free normalized layers.
Accordingly, the genuinely missing input is a **horizontal relation among the derivative/normalized layers for different primes at the same integer **$`n`$. A stronger local congruence at one prime is not enough.
---
# 11. Final obstruction statement
The precise conclusion is:
1. The forced factor $`2t+h+1=p`$ exhausts the mod-$`p`$ continuant certificate.
2. After division by that factor, the residual value is exactly an explicit unit times Gessel's derivative $`\dot b_t`$.
3. A zero pair does not constrain that derivative; $`(17,3,13)`$ gives $`\dot b_3\equiv1`$.
4. If the derivative exceptionally vanishes, the next quotient depends on a normalized derivative, the common beta layer, and a new reflection defect.
5. The mod-$`p^2`$ Lucas formula at the full moving index transports the same layers into $`b_n/p`$; it does not annihilate them.
6. Companion values only multiply the derivative by units and reparameterize the beta layer through the Wronskian.
7. Products over several reflected pairs become products or polynomials in their derivative defects. They have no forced zero.
8. Divisibility and normalized quotients of a single global integer admit arbitrary CRT prescriptions at linear height, so an ordinary product formula cannot supply the missing cross-prime restriction.
Therefore all reflected-continuant certificates obtainable from the presently known first-order p-adic identities fall into the dichotomy requested in the question:
$$
\boxed{
\text{eliminate the common beta layers and retain only unconstrained derivative defects,}
}
$$
or
$$
\boxed{
\text{retain the beta layers and therefore have no cross-prime certificate.}
}
$$
A proof of Problem 3.2 through this route now requires a new horizontal theorem about those layers, not another formal manipulation of the recurrence.
