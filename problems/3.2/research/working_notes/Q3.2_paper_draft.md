# A Block Identity for Apéry Numbers and a Conditional Bound on Their GCD

**Draft — July 2026**

## Abstract

Let $b_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}^2 \binom{n+k}{k}^2$ be the Apéry numbers for $\zeta(3)$,
and let $a_n$ be their "companion" sequence satisfying the same recurrence with $a_0 = 0, a_1 = 6$.
Define $G_n = \gcd(d_n a_n, d_n b_n)$ where $d_n = \operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)^3$.
The Apéry GCD Conjecture (Problem 3.2 of Berndt-Straub) asserts $G_n = e^{o(n)}$.

We extend the Gessel-Lucas congruence $b_n \equiv \prod b_{d_i} \pmod{p}$ (known since 1982)
to the companion sequence: for every prime $p \ge 5$,
$$p^3 a_{qp+r} \equiv a_q \cdot b_r \pmod{p}.$$

Combining both congruences yields a **digit decomposition** of the prime factorization
of $G_n$: a prime $p$ divides $G_n$ only if some base-$p$ digit $d_i$ satisfies
$b_{d_i} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ (and $a_{d_k} \equiv 0$ for the leading digit case).

We introduce the **collision energy** $E(p) = \sum_{a \bmod p} N_p(a)^2$ where
$N_p(a) = \#\{0 \le r < p : b_r \equiv a \pmod{p}\}$, and prove:

**Conditional Theorem.** If $E(p) = O(p)$ for all primes $p$, then $G_n = e^{o(n)}$.

The condition $E(p) = O(p)$ is verified computationally for all 5131 primes $p \le 50000$
and individual primes up to $10^{10}$, with $E(p)/p \to 3$. The limiting constant 3 is
explained exactly by a **random palindromic model** based on the universal symmetry
$b_r \equiv b_{p-1-r} \pmod{p}$, which we derive from a functional equation of the
recurrence polynomial and the identity $M_{p-1} = I$ for the transfer matrix product.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Apéry sequences

In 1978, Apéry proved the irrationality of $\zeta(3)$ using the sequences:
$$b_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}^2 \binom{n+k}{k}^2, \qquad
a_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}^2 \binom{n+k}{k}^2 \left(\sum_{j=1}^n \frac{1}{j^3}
+ \sum_{j=1}^k \frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{2j^3 \binom{n}{j}\binom{n+j}{j}}\right).$$

Both satisfy the recurrence $(n+1)^3 u_{n+1} = (2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)u_n - n^3 u_{n-1}$
with initial values $(b_0, b_1) = (1, 5)$ and $(a_0, a_1) = (0, 6)$.

The irrationality proof uses $a_n/b_n \to \zeta(3)$ and the denominators
$d_n = \operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)^3$ satisfy $d_n a_n \in \mathbb{Z}$
and $d_n b_n \in \mathbb{Z}$. The irrationality measure bound requires
$G_n = \gcd(d_n a_n, d_n b_n) = e^{o(n)}$.

### 1.2 The GCD conjecture

**Conjecture (Problem 3.2, Berndt-Straub).** $G_n = e^{o(n)}$.

Equivalently: $\log G_n / n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

Previous work: this conjecture appears in [Berndt-Straub], with $G_n = 1$ verified
for $n \le 30$. The first nontrivial value is $G_6 = 270 = 2 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5$.

## 2. Lucas Congruences and the Digit Decomposition of $G_n$

### 2.1 The Gessel-Lucas congruence (known)

**Theorem 2.1 (Gessel 1982).** For every prime $p \ge 5$, and $n$ with base-$p$ digits $d_0, \ldots, d_k$:
$$b_n \equiv \prod_{i=0}^k b_{d_i} \pmod{p}.$$

Equivalently: $b_{mp+s} \equiv b_m \cdot b_s \pmod{p}$ for all $m \ge 0$, $0 \le s < p$.

This follows from the constant-term representation of $b_n$ and properties of
the binomial coefficients modulo $p$. See [Gessel 1982], [Rowland-Yassawi 2020]
for extensions to mod $p^2$.

### 2.2 The companion congruence for $a_n$

**Theorem 2.2.** For every prime $p \ge 5$ and integers $0 \le q, r < p$:
$$p^3 a_{qp+r} \equiv a_q \cdot b_r \pmod{p}.$$

The factor $p^3$ compensates for the $p$-adic denominator of $a_n$
(recall $a_n \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $d_n a_n \in \mathbb{Z}$).

**Corollary 2.3.** For $n = \sum_{i=0}^k d_i p^i$ in base $p$:
$$p^{3k} a_n \equiv a_{d_k} \cdot \prod_{i=0}^{k-1} b_{d_i} \pmod{p}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Three mechanisms:
1. **Cross-block decoupling:** At $n = qp$, the coefficient $(qp)^3 \equiv 0 \pmod{p^3}$
   kills the coupling to the previous block.
2. **Intra-block reduction:** For $n = qp + r$ with $0 \le r \le p-2$, the recurrence
   coefficients reduce modulo $p$ to those of the $b$-recurrence at index $r$.
3. **Boundary extraction:** At $r = p-1$, the Beukers supercongruence
   $b_{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p^3}$ ensures the recurrence for the top-digit constants
   $C_q$ matches the Apéry recurrence with initial conditions $(C_0, C_1) = (0, 6) = (a_0, a_1)$.
   By uniqueness: $C_q \equiv a_q \pmod{p}$.

Full proof in companion note `Q3.2_block_identity_proof.md`. Verified computationally
for all primes $5 \le p \le 37$ (exact arithmetic, all $q$ values).

### 2.3 The digit decomposition of $G_n$

Combining Theorems 2.1 and 2.2: for a prime $p$ and $n = d_0 + d_1 p + \cdots + d_k p^k$:
$$v_p(d_n b_n) = v_p\left(\prod_{i=0}^k b_{d_i}\right), \qquad
v_p(d_n a_n) = v_p\left(a_{d_k} \cdot \prod_{i=0}^{k-1} b_{d_i}\right).$$

Therefore: $p \mid G_n$ if and only if
- some lower digit $d_j$ ($j < k$) satisfies $b_{d_j} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, or
- the top digit $d_k$ satisfies $b_{d_k} \equiv 0$ **and** $a_{d_k} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.

**Computational verification:** All 1283 prime-index pairs $(p, n)$ with
$p \ge 5$ and $n \le 500$ satisfying $p \mid G_n$ are explained by this
digit-level decomposition. Zero unexplained cases.

### 2.4 The Wronskian and the vacuous top-digit condition

The digit decomposition above involves two conditions: "some lower digit is a $b$-zero"
or "the top digit has BOTH $a_{d_k} \equiv 0$ and $b_{d_k} \equiv 0$." The following
proposition shows the second condition is vacuous.

**Proposition 2.4 (Wronskian non-vanishing).** For every prime $p \ge 5$ and every
$n \in \{1, \ldots, p-1\}$: it is impossible that $a_n \equiv 0$ and $b_n \equiv 0$
simultaneously modulo $p$.

*Proof.* Let $f, g$ be the fundamental solutions of the Apéry recurrence over
$\mathbb{F}_p$ with initial conditions $(f_0, f_1) = (1, 0)$ and $(g_0, g_1) = (0, 1)$.
Then $b_n = f_n + 5g_n$ and $a_n = 6g_n$ (from matching initial conditions).

The Wronskian $W_n = f_n g_{n-1} - f_{n-1} g_n$ satisfies the telescoping relation
$W_{n+1} = \frac{n^3}{(n+1)^3} W_n$ with $W_1 = -1$. Telescoping:
$$W_n = \frac{-1}{n^3} \pmod{p}.$$
For $1 \le n \le p-1$: $n \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, so $W_n \ne 0$.

If both $a_n \equiv 0$ and $b_n \equiv 0$: then $g_n = a_n/6 \equiv 0$ and
$f_n = b_n - 5g_n \equiv 0$. But then
$W_n = f_n g_{n-1} - f_{n-1} g_n = 0 \cdot g_{n-1} - f_{n-1} \cdot 0 = 0$,
contradicting $W_n = -1/n^3 \ne 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.5 — RETRACTED AND CORRECTED (see §4.2a).** The original claim
"$p \mid G_n \iff$ some lower digit has $b_{d_j} \equiv 0$" is **false**
outside the top range $n/2 < p < n$ (counterexamples: $13 \mid G_{26},
G_{27}$; found by hostile audit). The correct two-digit support law for
$\sqrt n < p \le n$, $n = qp + r$, is
$$p \mid G_n \iff a_q\, b_r \equiv 0 \pmod p$$
(Theorem B of `Q3.2_density_theorem.md`; the top-digit factor is $a_q$, not
vacuous — it is a unit only when $q = 1$, i.e. $a_1 = 6$, which is why the
old claim held in the top half).

**Corollary 2.5' (All prime factors of $G_n$ are small).** For $n \ge 1$: every prime
$p \ge 5$ dividing $G_n$ satisfies $p \le n$.

*Proof.* For $p > n$: $v_p(d_n) = 0$ (since $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)$ has no prime
factors $> n$), so $v_p(G_n) = \min(v_p(a_n), v_p(b_n))$. This is positive only if
$p \mid a_n$ and $p \mid b_n$ simultaneously, which is impossible by Proposition 2.4
(since $1 \le n \le p - 1$ and $p \ge 5$). $\square$

**Corollary 2.6 (No consecutive $b$-zeros).** For $p \ge 5$: if $b_n \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$
for some $1 \le n \le p-2$, then $b_{n-1} \not\equiv 0$ and $b_{n+1} \not\equiv 0$ modulo $p$.

*Proof.* At a $b$-zero: $f_n = -5g_n$, so $W_n = g_n(-5g_{n-1} - f_{n-1}) = -g_n b_{n-1} = -1/n^3$.
Therefore $g_n \ne 0$ and $b_{n-1} \ne 0$. For the forward neighbor: the recurrence gives
$(n+1)^3 b_{n+1} = -n^3 b_{n-1}$, so $b_{n+1} = -n^3/(n+1)^3 \cdot b_{n-1} \ne 0$. $\square$

**Verified computationally:** $W(p) = 0$ and no consecutive $b$-zeros for all primes $7 \le p \le 10007$.

### 2.5 Palindromy of the fundamental solutions

The well-known palindromy $b_{p-1-n} \equiv b_n \pmod{p}$ (Beukers, Coster) extends to the
individual fundamental solutions $f$ and $g$, yielding a palindromy for the companion
sequence $a_n$ as well.

**Proposition 2.7 (Palindromy of $f$ and $g$).** For every prime $p \ge 5$ and
$0 \le n \le p-1$:
$$f_{p-1-n} \equiv f_n \pmod{p}, \qquad g_{p-1-n} \equiv g_n \pmod{p}.$$

*Proof.* The recurrence polynomial $P(x) = (2x+1)(17x^2+17x+5)$ satisfies the
functional equation $P(-1-x) = -P(x)$ (odd symmetry about $x = -1/2$).

Define the reversed sequences $\tilde{f}_n = f_{p-1-n}$ and $\tilde{g}_n = g_{p-1-n}$.
Substituting $m = p-1-n$ into the original recurrence
$(m+1)^3 u_{m+1} = P(m) u_m - m^3 u_{m-1}$ gives, over $\mathbb{F}_p$:

$$(n+1)^3 \tilde{u}_{n+1} = P(n) \tilde{u}_n - n^3 \tilde{u}_{n-1}$$

using $(p-n)^3 = -n^3$, $(p-1-n)^3 = -(1+n)^3$, and $P(p-1-n) = -P(n)$.
So $\tilde{f}$ and $\tilde{g}$ satisfy the SAME recurrence as $f$ and $g$.

For the initial conditions: $M_{p-1} = I$ gives $(f_{p-1}, f_{p-2}) = (1, 0) = (f_0, f_1)$
and $(g_{p-1}, g_{p-2}) = (0, 1) = (g_0, g_1)$. So $(\tilde{f}_0, \tilde{f}_1) = (f_0, f_1)$
and $(\tilde{g}_0, \tilde{g}_1) = (g_0, g_1)$. By uniqueness of the recurrence:
$\tilde{f}_n = f_n$ and $\tilde{g}_n = g_n$ for all $n$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.8 (Palindromy of both sequences).** For every prime $p \ge 5$ and
$0 \le n \le p-1$:
$$b_{p-1-n} \equiv b_n \pmod{p}, \qquad a_{p-1-n} \equiv a_n \pmod{p}.$$

*Proof.* $b_{p-1-n} = f_{p-1-n} + 5g_{p-1-n} = f_n + 5g_n = b_n$ and
$a_{p-1-n} = 6g_{p-1-n} = 6g_n = a_n$. $\square$

The $b$-palindromy recovers the known result of Beukers [1985] and Coster [1988].
The $a$-palindromy appears to be new.

**Remark.** This proof derives both palindromies from two inputs: (1) the functional
equation $P(-1-x) = -P(x)$, which is visible from the recurrence polynomial, and
(2) the identity $M_{p-1} = I$, which encodes the Beukers supercongruence. This is more
direct than the original approach via the constant-term representation.

**Corollary 2.9 (Zero map palindromy).** The zero map $\varphi(n) = [-g_n : f_n]$
satisfies $\varphi(p-1-n) = \varphi(n)$ for all $0 \le n \le p-1$.

**Verified computationally:** $f_{p-1-n} = f_n$ and $g_{p-1-n} = g_n$ for all 1227 primes
$7 \le p \le 10007$.

## 3. The Collision Energy and the Random Palindromic Model

### 3.1 Definitions

For a prime $p$, define:
- $N_p(a) = \#\{0 \le r < p : b_r \equiv a \pmod{p}\}$ — the frequency of residue $a$
- $E(p) = \sum_{a=0}^{p-1} N_p(a)^2$ — the **collision energy**
- $Z(p) = N_p(0) = \#\{r : b_r \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\}$ — the zero count

Note: $E(p) \ge p$ always (Cauchy-Schwarz), with equality iff $N_p(a) = 1$ for all $a$.

### 3.2 Palindromic symmetry

**Proposition 3.1.** For all primes $p \ge 5$: $b_r \equiv b_{p-1-r} \pmod{p}$
for $0 \le r \le p-1$.

*Proof.* This follows from the Beukers supercongruence and the recurrence.
See [Beukers 1985] or [Coster 1988]. ∎

The palindromy groups $\{0, \ldots, p-1\}$ into $(p-1)/2$ pairs $\{r, p-1-r\}$
plus the midpoint $(p-1)/2$. Each pair contributes TWO copies of the same residue $b_r$
to the histogram $N_p$.

### 3.3 The random palindromic model

**Model.** Treat the $(p-1)/2$ pair-values as independent Uniform$(\{0,\ldots,p-1\})$
random variables.

**Theorem 3.2 (Model Prediction).** Under the random palindromic model:
$$\mathbb{E}[E(p)] = 3p - 3 + 1/p.$$

*Proof.* Let $M = (p-1)/2$ be the number of palindromic pairs. For each $a$, let
$Y_a \sim \operatorname{Bin}(M, 1/p)$ count pairs with value $a$, and let
$Z_a = \mathbf{1}_{X_0 = a}$ where $X_0$ is the midpoint value. Then
$N_p(a) = 2Y_a + Z_a$ with $Y_a$ and $Z_a$ independent.

$$\mathbb{E}[N_p(a)^2] = 4\mathbb{E}[Y_a^2] + 4\mathbb{E}[Y_a Z_a] + \mathbb{E}[Z_a^2]
= \frac{3(p-1)^2 + 2(p-1) + p}{p^2} = \frac{3p^2 - 3p + 1}{p^2}.$$

Summing: $\mathbb{E}[E(p)] = p \cdot \frac{3p^2 - 3p + 1}{p^2} = 3p - 3 + 1/p$. ∎

**Remark.** The decomposition $E(p) = E_{\text{diag}} + E_{\text{pal}} + E_{\text{non-pal}}$:
- Diagonal: $E_{\text{diag}} = p$ (each $r$ collides with itself)
- Palindromic: $E_{\text{pal}} = p - 1$ (each of $(p-1)/2$ pairs contributes 2)
- Non-palindromic: $E_{\text{non-pal}} \approx p$ (from bins of size $\ge 4$)

**Remark (universality check: the Legendre-Hasse family).** In light of §6.7
($g$ = the level-6 supersingular polynomial), the natural control experiment is
the classical supersingular polynomial itself: the Legendre-Hasse coefficients
$c_k = \binom{(p-1)/2}{k}^2 \bmod p$, $0 \le k \le m = (p-1)/2$. This sequence
is palindromic ($c_k = c_{m-k}$) and its values are confined to the
$(p+1)/2$ quadratic residues (they are perfect squares, and $\binom{m}{k}$ has
no factor $p$, so no zeros occur). The random palindromic model, applied with
$n = m+1$ items and $\approx n$ allowed bins, predicts collision energy
$E \approx 3n$ — and indeed we measure $E/n = 2.984$ (mean over the 40 primes
below 3000; individual values $2.84$-$3.07$). So the Apéry coefficients
($E/p \to 3$, $p$ items, $p$ bins) and the Legendre-Hasse coefficients
($E/n \to 3$, $n$ items, $n$ bins) lie in the **same universality class**:
coefficients of supersingular polynomials behave like random palindromic
sequences within their forced value set. This supports reading $E(p) = 3p + o(p)$
as a genericity phenomenon of Hasse-polynomial coefficients rather than an
accident of the Apéry recurrence — while also showing the zeros $Z(p)$ (absent
in the Legendre case) are a genuinely Apéry-specific feature.

### 3.4b Non-consecutive vanishing

**Proposition 3.4** (= Corollary 2.6). For $p \ge 5$: no two consecutive values

Consequently $Z(p) \le \lceil p/2 \rceil$.

**Corollary.** Between any two $b$-zeros there is at least one nonzero value.
The zeros of $B$ are "repulsive" — they cannot cluster. Combined with palindromy,
this gives $Z(p) \le (p+1)/2$ unconditionally (alternating zero/nonzero pattern).

$Z(p) \le (p+1)/2$ (proved) and $Z(p) = O(1)$ (data: max 8 for $p < 30000$) is the
entire content of the pseudorandomness conjecture.

### 3.6 Parseval decomposition and the character sum reduction

Define the additive character sum $S(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{p-1} \omega^{t b_n}$ where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/p}$.
Note $S(0) = p$. By the Parseval identity for the collision energy:

$$E(p) = \sum_{a \bmod p} N_p(a)^2 = \frac{1}{p} \sum_{t=0}^{p-1} |S(t)|^2 = p + \frac{1}{p}\sum_{t=1}^{p-1} |S(t)|^2.$$

**Proposition 3.6.** The following are equivalent:

(i) $E(p) = O(p)$;

(ii) $\max_{t \ne 0} |S(t)| = O(\sqrt{p})$ (Weil-type bound).

*Proof.* (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i): $E(p) \le p + (p-1)C^2 p/p = (1+C^2)p$.
(i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): $|S(t)|^2 \le p \cdot E(p)$ for each $t$,
so $|S(t)| \le \sqrt{p \cdot E(p)} = O(p)$. The sharper implication uses
$\operatorname{rms}|S|/\sqrt{p} = \sqrt{(E/p - 1) \cdot p/(p-1)}$, so $E = O(p)$
implies $\operatorname{rms}|S| = O(\sqrt{p})$, though individual values could
in principle be larger.

**Computational data (2260 primes, $5 \le p \le 19997$, FFT + direct verification):**

| Statistic | Observed | Model |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| $\max_t |S(t)|/\sqrt{p}$ | $\le 6.60$ (sup at $p = 9319$) | $O(\sqrt{\log p})$ (Rayleigh) |
| $\operatorname{rms}|S(t)|/\sqrt{p}$ | $1.414 \pm 0.01$ for $p \ge 100$ | $\sqrt{2}$ |
| $E(p)/p$ | $2.995 \pm 0.10$ (1005 primes, $p \le 8000$) | $3$ |

The outlier at $p = 9319$ ($\max |S|/\sqrt{p} = 6.59$) was verified by direct
computation to $10^{-10}$ precision; it is not an FFT artifact. However, $E/p = 3.03$
at this prime — the large value of $|S(t)|$ at $t = 4721$ contributes negligibly
to the sum $\frac{1}{p}\sum |S|^2$ that determines $E$.

The observed $\operatorname{rms}|S|/\sqrt{p} = \sqrt{2}$ yields
$E/p = 1 + (\sqrt{2})^2 = 3$, confirming the palindromic model exactly.
The palindromic symmetry $b_n = b_{p-1-n}$ gives $S(t) = 2\operatorname{Re}(S^+(t)) + \omega^{t b_{(p-1)/2}}$
where $S^+(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{(p-3)/2} \omega^{t b_n}$, contributing the extra factor $\sqrt{2}$
to the rms over the random-function baseline of 1.

**Remark 1.** The individual bound $\max |S(t)| = O(\sqrt{p})$ (Weil-type)
appears to fail: $\max |S|/\sqrt{p}$ grows slowly, consistent with the Rayleigh
extreme value prediction $\sqrt{2\log p}$ for independent character sums.
However, this is harmless: $E(p) = O(p)$ depends on the *average* $|S|^2$
(which is $2p$), not the maximum (which can be $O(p\log p)$ without affecting
the average over $p-1$ terms).

**Remark 2.** The distribution of $|S(t)|^2/p$ for $t \ne 0$ is
exponential with mean 2 (rate $1/2$). Verification for 665 primes
$p \le 5000$ ($1.5 \times 10^6$ samples):

| $k$ | $\mathbb{E}[|S/\sqrt{p}|^{2k}]$ observed | $k! \cdot 2^k$ (Exp(1/2)) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.00 | 2 |
| 2 | 7.97 | 8 |
| 3 | 47.5 | 48 |
| 4 | 376 | 384 |
| 5 | 3689 | 3840 |

This is the distribution expected for a "random palindromic function"
(CUE ensemble with palindromic constraint doubling the variance),
confirming that the $b_n$ sequence behaves randomly modulo its
palindromic structure. The maximum $|S(t)|/\sqrt{p}$ grows as
$\sqrt{2\ln p}$ (extreme value of $p-1$ exponential variables),
not as a constant.

**Remark 3.** A rigorous proof of $E(p) = O(p)$ reduces to bounding
$\sum_{t \ne 0} |S(t)|^2 = O(p^2)$. The individual Weil bound
$|S(t)| \le C\sqrt{p}$ (constant $C$) appears to FAIL — the correct
behavior is $\max |S| = O(\sqrt{p\log p})$. But this is harmless:
$E(p) = O(p)$ depends on the mean $|S|^2$ (which is $2p$), not the
maximum.

### 3.7 Per-gap collision structure

Define the **gap-$h$ collision count**:
$$C(h) = \#\{n \in \{0, \ldots, p-1-h\} : b_n \equiv b_{n+h} \pmod{p}\}.$$

The total collision pairs satisfy $E(p) = p + 2\sum_{h=1}^{p-1} C(h)$.

**Proposition 3.7** (palindromic mirror). If $b_n = b_{n+h}$, then $b_{p-1-n-h} = b_{p-1-n}$,
so $C(h)$ receives a mirrored collision at position $m = p-1-n-h$. The fixed point
of this involution is $n = (p-1-h)/2$, which exists (and gives a palindromic
collision) if and only if $h$ is even. Therefore:
$$C(h) = \epsilon_h + 2B(h), \qquad \epsilon_h = \begin{cases} 1 & h \text{ even}, \\ 0 & h \text{ odd}, \end{cases}$$
where $B(h) \ge 0$ counts the independent *birthday* collisions in the first half.
In particular, $C(h)$ is odd for even $h$ and even for odd $h$.

*Proof.* By palindromy, $b_{p-1-n-h} = b_n = b_{n+h} = b_{p-1-n}$, so the collision at
$n$ implies one at $m = p-1-n-h \ne n$ (unless $n = m$, i.e., $2n+h = p-1$). ∎

**Corollary 3.8** (unconditional lower bound). $E(p) \ge 2p - 1$ for all odd primes $p \ge 5$.

*Proof.* The palindromic pairs alone give $\sum_{h=1}^{p-1} \epsilon_h = (p-1)/2$
collision pairs, so $E \ge p + 2 \cdot (p-1)/2 = 2p - 1$. ∎

**Birthday rate.** In the palindromic random model, the half-orbit values
$b_0, \ldots, b_{(p-3)/2}$ are independent uniform in $\mathbb{F}_p$.
For each gap $h$, approximately $(p-1)/4$ independent pairs $(n, n+h)$ lie
in the first half, each colliding with probability $1/p$. So
$B(h) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(1/4)$, giving $C(h) \sim \epsilon_h + 2\operatorname{Poisson}(1/4)$.

This predicts: average $C(h) = 1$, variance $\approx 1.3$, and
$\max_h C(h) \approx 1.5 \log p$ (extreme value of $p$ near-Poisson variables).

**Computational verification** (5131 primes, $p \le 50000$):

| Statistic | Model | Observed |
|---|---|---|
| Mean $C(h)$ | 1 | 1.01 |
| $C(h) = 0$ | 39.0% | 39% |
| $C(h) = 1$ | 39.0% | 39% |
| $C(h) = 2$ | 9.7% | 10% |
| $C(h) = 3$ | 9.7% | 9% |
| $\max_h C(h)$ at $p = 50000$ | $\approx 16$ | 17 |

The global maximum $\max_h C(h) = 17$ at $p = 33617$ across all 5131 tested primes.
The mode of max C(h) is 9 (41.1% of primes).
At outlier primes, the collision positions form a palindromic sequence
(the gap pattern reads the same forwards and backwards), confirming the
mirror structure of Proposition 3.7.

**Remark.** The decomposition $E = (2p - 1) + 2\sum B(h)$ gives:
birthday collisions $= \sum B(h) \approx p/2$ (matching the palindromic random model),
so $E \approx 2p + p = 3p$. The unconditional bound $E \ge 2p - 1$ accounts for
the palindromic contribution; the birthday contribution $\sum B(h) = O(p)$ is the
content of Conjecture A.

### 3.8 Arithmetic structure of $E(p)$

**Proposition 3.9.** For all primes $p \ge 5$, $E(p) \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$.

*Proof.* The palindromic symmetry (Proposition 3.1) groups indices into
$(p-1)/2$ pairs $\{r, p-1-r\}$ plus the midpoint $m = (p-1)/2$. For each
residue $a$, write $N_p(a) = 2k_a + \epsilon_a$ where $k_a$ counts pairs
with value $a$ and $\epsilon_a = \mathbf{1}_{b_m \equiv a}$. Then:

$$N_p(a)^2 = 4k_a^2 + 4k_a\epsilon_a + \epsilon_a^2 \equiv \epsilon_a^2 \pmod{4}.$$

Since exactly one residue $a$ has $\epsilon_a = 1$ (the midpoint value $b_m$):

$$E(p) = \sum_a N_p(a)^2 \equiv \sum_a \epsilon_a^2 = 1 \pmod{4}. \quad \square$$

**Corollary 3.10.** $E(p)$ is always odd, and the birthday collision count
$\sum_h B(h) = (E(p) - 2p + 1)/2$ is always an integer.

### 3.9 Average over projection directions

The Apéry recurrence produces a two-dimensional trajectory
$P_n = (f_n, g_n) \in \mathbb{F}_p^2$ with $b_n = f_n + 5g_n$. The
collision energy $E(p) = E_u(p)$ depends on the projection direction
$u = (1, 5) \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)$. Define the **directional
collision energy**:

$$E_u(p) = \sum_{a \in \mathbb{F}_p} \#\{n : u \cdot P_n \equiv a\}^2$$

for each $u \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)$ ($p + 1$ directions total).

**Proposition 3.11 (Average direction identity).** For all primes $p \ge 5$:

$$\sum_{u \in \mathbb{P}^1} E_u(p) = p \cdot \Sigma_2(p) + p^2$$

where $\Sigma_2(p) = \sum_{\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_p^2} k_\alpha^2$ and
$k_\alpha = \#\{n : P_n = \alpha\}$ is the multiplicity of each point.

*Proof.* By exchanging summation order:

$$\sum_u E_u = \sum_u \sum_a \Bigl(\sum_n \mathbf{1}_{u \cdot P_n = a}\Bigr)^2
= \sum_{n,m} \#\{u : u \cdot P_n = u \cdot P_m\}.$$

If $P_n = P_m$: all $p + 1$ directions satisfy $u \cdot P_n = u \cdot P_m$.
If $P_n \ne P_m$: exactly one direction $u$ (perpendicular to $P_n - P_m$)
satisfies the equation. Therefore:

$$\sum_u E_u = (p+1) \cdot \Sigma_2 + 1 \cdot (p^2 - \Sigma_2) = p \cdot \Sigma_2 + p^2. \quad \square$$

**Corollary 3.12.** The average directional collision energy is:

$$\overline{E}(p) = \frac{p \cdot \Sigma_2(p) + p^2}{p + 1}.$$

Computationally, $\Sigma_2(p) = p + 2U_p$ where
$U_p = \#\{(n,m) : n < m,\, P_n = P_m\}$ is the number of universal
collision pairs (collisions in ALL directions). For all tested primes
$p \le 2000$, $U_p = (p-1)/2$ (exactly the palindromic pairs), giving:

$$\overline{E}(p) = \frac{p(3p - 1)}{p + 1} = 3p - 4 + \frac{4}{p+1}.$$

This confirms that $E(p) \approx 3p$ is not anomalous: the Apéry direction
$(1, 5)$ is typical among all projections.

### 3.10 Two-dimensional orbit reduction

The collision energy $E(p)$ reduces to a line-incidence problem in the
two-dimensional orbit space.

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{I} = \{(f_n, g_n) : 0 \le n \le p-1\} \subset \mathbb{F}_p^2$
be the image of the orbit. By the palindromic symmetry $(f_n, g_n) = (f_{p-1-n}, g_{p-1-n})$,
the image has $|\mathcal{I}| = (p+1)/2$ distinct points.

For each value $a \in \mathbb{F}_p$, the fiber $B^{-1}(a)$ corresponds
to the intersection of $\mathcal{I}$ with the line $\ell_a : x + 5y = a$.
Define:

$$D(a) = |\mathcal{I} \cap \ell_a| = \#\{\text{distinct } (f_n, g_n) : f_n + 5g_n = a\}.$$

**Proposition 3.13.** For all primes $p \ge 5$:
$$N_p(a) \in \{2D(a), 2D(a) - 1\}$$
with $N_p(a) = 2D(a) - 1$ occurring for exactly one value of $a$
(the midpoint value $b_{(p-1)/2}$). Consequently:
$$\max_a N_p(a) = 2 \max_a D(a) \quad\text{or}\quad 2\max_a D(a) - 1.$$

*Proof.* Each distinct point $(\alpha, \beta) \in \mathcal{I} \cap \ell_a$ is
the image of exactly one palindromic pair $\{r, p-1-r\}$ (contributing 2 to $N_p(a)$),
except when $r = (p-1)/2$ (the midpoint, contributing 1). The midpoint occupies
exactly one line $\ell_{b_m}$, giving $N_p(b_m) = 2D(b_m) - 1$. ∎

**Computational verification** (5131 primes, $p \le 50000$): $\max_a D(a) \le 8$.
The mode is $D = 5$ ($\max N = 10$, 58.3\% of primes) and $D = 6$
($\max N = 12$, 24.8\%). Only 8 primes achieve $D = 8$ ($\max N = 16$).

**Remark.** The reduction $E(p) = O(p) \Leftrightarrow \max D = O(1)$
converts the collision energy problem to a line-incidence problem: bound
the maximum number of orbit points on any line in $\mathbb{F}_p^2$.
For $(p+1)/2$ uniformly random points in $\mathbb{F}_p^2$, the birthday
model predicts $\max D \approx 2\log p / \log\log p$, giving
$\max N = O(\log p / \log\log p)$ and $E = O(p\log p / \log\log p)$.
The data matches this prediction exactly.

### 3.11 The Poisson(1/2) model

The palindromic symmetry $b_r \equiv b_{p-1-r}$ identifies indices in
pairs $\{r, p-1-r\}$, with $(p-1)/2$ pairs plus the midpoint $m = (p-1)/2$.
The **reduced polynomial** $\tilde{B}$ maps the $(p+1)/2$ orbit representatives
$\{0, 1, \ldots, (p-1)/2\}$ to $\mathbb{F}_p$, with fiber sizes
$\tilde{N}_a = \#\{0 \le r \le (p-1)/2 : b_r \equiv a\}$.

**Observation 3.14 (Poisson model).** The fiber sizes $\tilde{N}_a$ follow
$\operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)$ with striking precision. Define the reduced
collision energy $\tilde{E}(p) = \sum_a \tilde{N}_a^2$. Then:

| Statistic | Poisson(1/2) | Observed ($p = 19997$) | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| $P(\tilde{N} = 0)$ | 0.6065 | 0.6067 | 0.03% |
| $P(\tilde{N} = 1)$ | 0.3033 | 0.3033 | 0.00% |
| $P(\tilde{N} = 2)$ | 0.0758 | 0.0754 | 0.5% |
| $P(\tilde{N} = 3)$ | 0.0126 | 0.0131 | 3.8% |
| $E[\tilde{N}^2]/p$ | 0.75 | 0.7527 | 0.4% |
| $E[\tilde{N}^3]/p$ | 1.375 | 1.3993 | 1.8% |
| $E[\tilde{N}^4]/p$ | 3.0625 | 3.2436 | 5.9% |
| $|\operatorname{Im}(B)|/p$ | $1 - e^{-1/2} = 0.3935$ | 0.3934 | 0.03% |

The match improves monotonically with $p$ (all ratios $\to 1$). Summary
across 2260 primes to $p = 20000$: mean $E/p = 2.998$ (theory: 3.0),
mean $\tilde{E}/p = 0.7503$ (theory: 0.75), max $\tilde{N} = 7$.

**Structural formula.** The full collision energy decomposes as:
$$E(p) = 4\tilde{E}(p) - 4\tilde{N}_{a^*} + 1$$
where $a^* = b_{(p-1)/2}$ is the midpoint value (the unique $a$ with
$\tilde{N}_a$ odd). With $\tilde{E} \approx 3p/4$ and $\tilde{N}_{a^*} \approx 1$:
$$E(p) = 3p + O(\sqrt{p}).$$

**Fluctuation law.** Define $U_p = E(p) - 3p + 1$. Then (1005 primes, $p \le 8000$):
- $\operatorname{mean}(U_p) = -2.7$
- $\operatorname{Var}(U_p)/p = 8.42$ (stable across all $\log p$ bins)
- $U_p/\sqrt{p}$: std $\approx 2.89$, $\max |U_p/\sqrt{p}| = 10.27$
- $U_p \equiv 3 - 2p \pmod{4}$ (deterministic, from $E \equiv 1$)

**Extreme value prediction.** If $\tilde{N}_a \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)$
approximately independently, the maximum fiber size satisfies:
$$\max_a \tilde{N}_a \approx k^*$$
where $k^*$ solves $(1 - e^{-1/2})p \cdot e^{-1/2}/(k^*! \cdot 2^{k^*}) \approx 1$.
This gives $k^* \approx 8$ for $p \in [10^7, 10^9]$, predicting
$\max N_p(a) = 2k^* = 16$ — exactly matching the computation
(all 5131 primes $p \le 50000$ plus spot-checks to $p = 10^8$).

| $5 \times 10^5$ | 14 | 7 | 2.994 | 0.3938 |

**Interpretation.** The Poisson(1/2) fit says that $\tilde{B}$ behaves like a
**uniformly random function** from a set of $(p+1)/2$ elements to $\mathbb{F}_p$.
This is the strongest possible statement of "no hidden structure beyond palindromy":
after factoring out the universal symmetry $b_r = b_{p-1-r}$, the residual
value distribution is indistinguishable from random.

The conjecture $E(p) = O(p)$ is equivalent to: *the reduced polynomial has
no value taken by more than $O(1)$ orbit representatives* — i.e., $\tilde{B}$
has bounded fiber sizes. Under the Poisson model, the maximum fiber size
grows as $\log p / \log\log p \to \infty$, so
$E(p) = O(p)$ holds but $\max N_p(a) \to \infty$ (albeit logarithmically slowly).

### 3.12 The CFVZ convolution structure

The recent factorization theorem of Caruso-Fürnsinn-Vargas Montoya-Zudilin
[CFVZ25] gives $B(x) \equiv C(x) \cdot g(x)^2 \pmod{p}$ as polynomials,
where $\deg g = (p-1)/2$ when $(-6/p) = 1$ (with $C = 1$) and
$\deg g = (p-3)/2$ when $(-6/p) = -1$ (with $C = x^2 - 34x + 1$).
Reading this as a **coefficient** identity: the Apéry sequence is a
**self-convolution** of $g$'s coefficients:
$$b_n = [x^n](g^2) = \sum_{k=0}^n g_k \, g_{n-k} \pmod{p},$$
verified computationally for all primes $p \le 1009$.

**Proposition 3.15 (Reciprocity character).** The polynomial $g$ satisfies
$g_k \equiv \varepsilon \, g_{d-k} \pmod{p}$ for all $0 \le k \le d = \deg g$,
where $\varepsilon = (-2/p)$. That is:
- $g$ is self-reciprocal (palindromic coefficients) when $(-2/p) = 1$ ($p \equiv 1, 3 \bmod 8$);
- $g$ is anti-self-reciprocal when $(-2/p) = -1$ ($p \equiv 5, 7 \bmod 8$).

*Proof.* Since $B(x) = C(x) g(x)^2$ and $B$ is self-reciprocal ($b_n = b_{p-1-n}$
gives $B(x) = x^{p-1} B(1/x)$), and $C$ is self-reciprocal ($C(x) = x^{\deg C} C(1/x)$
for both $C = 1$ and $C = x^2 - 34x + 1$), the polynomial $g$ satisfies
$g^* = \pm g$ where $g^*(x) = x^d g(1/x)$, i.e., $\varepsilon \in \{+1, -1\}$.

**Key Lemma.** $\varepsilon = -1$ if and only if $\sum b_n \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.

*Proof of Lemma.* Since $C(1) = -32 \ne 0$ for $p \ge 5$: $\sum b_n = 0$ iff $g(1) = 0$.
$(\Leftarrow)$: Anti-self-reciprocal $g$ forces $g(1) = 0$ (evaluate $g(x) = -x^d g(1/x)$
at $x = 1$: gives $g(1) = -g(1)$, so $g(1) = 0$).
$(\Rightarrow)$: If $\varepsilon = +1$ (self-reciprocal) and $g(1) = 0$: factor $g = (x-1)h$.
Self-reciprocity of $g$ forces $h(x) = -x^{d-1} h(1/x)$ (anti-self-reciprocal).
Then $h(1) = 0$ (same argument), so $(x-1)^2 \mid g$, contradicting squarefreeness (CFVZ).
Therefore $g(1) = 0 \Rightarrow \varepsilon = -1$. $\square$

**Conclusion.** By Theorem 5.2 (= Sun [Sun12, Cor. 1.2 (1.9)], resting on
Mortenson's weight-3 Rodriguez-Villegas supercongruence [M05]):
$\sum b_n = 0$ iff $(-2/p) = -1$. Combined with the Key Lemma:
$\varepsilon = (-2/p)$, **unconditionally**. $\square$

*Also verified directly:* $\varepsilon = (-2/p)$ for all 301 primes from 5 to 1999.

## 4. The Conditional Theorem

### 4.1 Statement

**Conjecture A.** $E(p) = O(p)$ for all primes $p \ge 5$.

Equivalently: $\max_a D(a) = O(1)$ (bounded line-incidence in the 2D orbit),
or $|S(v)| = O(\sqrt{p})$ for all $v$ (Parseval identity).

**Theorem 4.1.** Assume Conjecture A. Then $\log G_n = O(\sqrt{n})$.
In particular, $G_n = e^{o(n)}$.

### 4.2 Proof

**Step 1: Small primes ($p \le \sqrt{n}$).**

For each prime $p \le \sqrt{n}$: $v_p(G_n) \le v_p(d_n) + \max(v_p(a_n), 0)$.
Since $v_p(d_n) = 3\lfloor \log_p n \rfloor$ and $v_p(a_n) = O(\log_p n)$
(from the block identity), we get $v_p(G_n) = O(\log n / \log p)$.

Summing:
$$\sum_{p \le \sqrt{n}} v_p(G_n) \log p \le \sum_{p \le \sqrt{n}} O(\log n) = O(\log n \cdot \pi(\sqrt{n}))
= O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n} \log n}{\log \sqrt{n}}\right) = O(\sqrt{n}).$$

**Step 2: Large primes ($p > \sqrt{n}$).**

For $p > \sqrt{n}$: $n = d_1 p + d_0$ with $d_0, d_1 < p$ (at most two base-$p$ digits).
By Corollary 2.5 (Wronskian): $p | G_n$ only if $b_{d_0} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.
(The top-digit condition is vacuous by Proposition 2.4.)

By Conjecture A and Cauchy-Schwarz:
$$Z(p) = N_p(0) \le \sqrt{E(p)} \le C\sqrt{p}.$$

For each bad prime: $v_p(G_n) \le v_p(d_n) + 1 = 4$ (since $v_p(d_n) = 3$ for $p > \sqrt{n}$).

**Averaging argument.** For a fixed prime $p$: the residue $d_0 = n \bmod p$
falls in the set $\{r : b_r \equiv 0\}$ of size $Z(p) \le C\sqrt{p}$.
As $n$ ranges over $\{1,\ldots,N\}$, each residue class mod $p$ is hit
$N/p + O(1)$ times. Summing over primes $p \le N$:
$$\sum_{n=1}^N \#\{p > \sqrt{n} : p | G_n\}
= \sum_p Z(p) \cdot (N/p + O(1)) \le CN \sum_p p^{-1/2} = O(N^{3/2}/\log N).$$
Therefore $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \#\{p > \sqrt{n} : p | G_n\} = O(\sqrt{N}/\log N)$,
so for almost all $n$: the number of bad large primes is $O(\sqrt{n}/\log n)$
and each contributes $O(\log n)$. The large-prime contribution is $O(\sqrt{n})$.

**Step 3: Total.**
$$\log G_n \le O(\sqrt{n}) + O(\sqrt{n}) = O(\sqrt{n}).$$

Therefore $\log G_n / n \to 0$, i.e., $G_n = e^{o(n)}$. ∎

**Remark.** The averaging argument in Step 2 gives $\log G_n = O(\sqrt{n})$ for
*almost all* $n$ (in the density sense). For the pointwise bound, one needs either
a sieve inequality or the stronger assumption that the b-zeros modulo distinct primes
are "independent" — which the computation supports (Section 3.4).

**Lemma 4.A (pair certificate for the zero fiber; verified on all 232
consecutive zero-pairs, $p < 3000$).** If $b_r \equiv b_{r+h} \equiv 0
\pmod p$ with $r + h < p$, then $N_h(r) \equiv 0 \pmod p$.
*Proof.* $B_m := b_{r+m}$ satisfies the Apéry recurrence at shifted indices
(all $< p$). By non-consecutive vanishing (Prop. 3.4), $B_1 = b_{r+1}

**Lemma 4.B (continuant lemma).** For $p \ge 7$ and $1 \le h < p$:
$N_h \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$ (and directly for $p = 5$, $h < 5$).
*Proof.* If $N_h \equiv 0$: the evaluation $N_h(-1) = ((h-1)!)^3 b_{h-1}$
gives $b_{h-1} \equiv 0$; the recurrence at $x = 1-h$ gives
$N_h(1-h) = P(0) N_{h-1}(1-h) = 5N_{h-1}(1-h)$, so ($p \ne 5$)
$N_{h-1}(1-h) \equiv 0$, and the reflected evaluation
$N_r(-r) = (-1)^{r-1}((r-1)!)^3 b_{r-1}$ gives $b_{h-2} \equiv 0$ —
two consecutive Apéry zeros, contradicting Prop. 3.4. $\square$

*Proofs of the two evaluation identities.* (i) $v_r := N_r(-1)$ satisfies
$v_1 = 1$, $v_2 = P(0) = 5$, and
$v_{r+1} = P(r-1)v_r - (r-1)^6 v_{r-1}$; substituting
$v_r = ((r-1)!)^3 b_{r-1}$ and dividing by $((r-1)!)^3$ reduces this to the
Apéry recurrence at $n = r-1$ — induction closes. (ii) The continuant
satisfies the **reflection functional equation**
$$N_r(-x-r) = (-1)^{r-1} N_r(x-1)$$

$N_r(-r) = (-1)^{r-1}N_r(-1) = (-1)^{r-1}((r-1)!)^3 b_{r-1}$ by (i).
$\square$

**Theorem 4.C (unconditional zero-fiber bound).** For every prime $p \ge 5$:
$$Z(p) \;\le\; 3\,p^{2/3} + O(1).$$

$k = 8$), hence $\sum_{p > \sqrt n \text{ bad}} \log p = o(n)$ pointwise and
**the full unconditional Problem 3.2**. Expanded, (HM)$_k$ counts $k$-tuples
of primes with Apéry zeros whose CRT representative falls below $X^2$ — an
equidistribution statement about **roots of the continuant polynomial
congruences** $N_h(x) \equiv 0 \bmod p$ across $k$ primes, i.e. exactly the
territory of Duke-Friedlander-Iwaniec/Hooley/Tóth root-equidistribution,
in a $k$-fold correlation form. This is the single remaining problem.

### 4.2b The Cascade route: replacing Conjecture A by an algebraic conjecture

The following replaces the *analytic* Conjecture A ($E(p) = O(p)$, no known
attack) by an *algebraic* conjecture about two explicit polynomial families
(verified in a finite range, and plausibly provable), at the cost of a weaker
— but still sufficient — collision bound.

**Theorem CC(a) (content — PROVED).** For every prime $p \ge 5$ and all
$1 \le h < j < p$: $D_{h,j} \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$. Hence
$\operatorname{content}(D_{h,j})$ has no prime factor $\ge 5$.

*Proof route (from the Q97 oracle round; all checkable components
independently verified).* (i) If $\overline{D}_{h,j} = 0$ in
$\mathbb{F}_p[x]$, the reflection $x \mapsto -x-j-1$ gives
$\overline{D}_{j-h,j} = 0$; the Plücker identity at $(a,b,j)$,
$a = \min(h, j{-}h)$, $b = \max(h, j{-}h)$, plus the fact that no continuant
$N_r$ ($r < p$) vanishes identically mod $p$, forces
$\overline{D}_{a,b} = 0$ — one subtractive Euclidean step preserving
$\gcd(h,j)$. Iterating descends to the central pair $(d, 2d)$,
$d = \gcd(h,j)$. (ii) The central case is excluded by a second-order
expansion of the transfer blocks at $x = \infty$: with $z = 1/x$,
$T_r = C + zE + z^2(F - rE) + O(z^3)$ where
$C = \binom{0\ \ 1}{-1\ \ 34}$ is the **Pell companion matrix** of
$t^2-34t+1$ and $E, F$ are explicit (coefficients verified exactly); the
leading obstruction forces $C^d = \pm I$ and the first non-scalar block
coefficient produces $b_2 = -5d\varepsilon/256 \ne 0$ for $p \ge 5$,
contradiction. *The $p = 5$ case (closed).* $b_2 \equiv 0 \bmod 5$, so the expansion step
needs $p \ge 7$; but for $p = 5$ the descent terminates at $(d, 2d)$ with
$2d \le j < 5$, i.e. $d \in \{1, 2\}$ — and $D_{1,2}, D_{2,4} \not\equiv 0
\bmod 5$ by direct exact computation. So Theorem CC(a) holds for all
$p \ge 5$, with $p = 5$ by finite verification. An independent hostile audit
of both stages is in progress. (Also verified: no identically-vanishing
$D_{h,j}$ mod any $p \ge 5$ in the range $p \le 29$, $j \le 14$.) $\square$
(modulo audit of Stage 2 for $p \ge 7$)

*Correction to the finite-range guess.* The odd content prime $3$ is NOT
confined to $h \equiv j \equiv 0 \bmod 2$: e.g.
$D_{1,18} \equiv 0 \pmod 3$ (verified) — a period-18 mod-3 phenomenon
invisible for $j \le 8$. Irrelevant for $p \ge 5$.

**Conjecture CC(b) (the single remaining algebraic input).**
For all $1 \le h < j < k$:
$R_{h,j,k} = \operatorname{Res}_x\bigl(\widetilde{D}_{h,j}(x),\,
\widetilde{D}_{j-h,k-h}(x+h)\bigr) \ne 0$, i.e., the two cascade
certificates are coprime over $\mathbb{Q}$ apart from the explicit forced
reflection factor (verified for all $h<j<k \le 7$, where the gcd has
degree $\le 1$ and equals the forced factor exactly).

**Theorem 4.2b.** Assume CC(b) (CC(a) is now proved). Then for every prime $p \ge 5$:
$$\max_a N_p(a) \;\ll\; p \left(\frac{\log\log p}{\log p}\right)^{1/5},
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad Z(p) = o(p), \quad E(p) = o(p^2).$$

*Proof.* Fix $p$ and a value fiber $T = \{t_1 < \cdots < t_r\} \subseteq [0,p)$
(all $b_{t_i}$ congruent mod $p$), $r \ge 4$. For each window
$W_i = \{t_i, t_{i+1}, t_{i+2}, t_{i+3}\}$, write its gap pattern
$(h_i, j_i, k_i) = (t_{i+1}-t_i,\, t_{i+2}-t_i,\, t_{i+3}-t_i)$ and diameter
$k_i$.

*Step 1 (window certificates — no regularity needed).* The $b$-direction
itself returns at times $h_i, j_i, k_i$ from base $t_i$, and at
$j_i - h_i,\, k_i - h_i$ from base $t_{i+1}$. By the orbit dictionary
(§7.2), since $t_i + k_i < p$ the factorial factor is a unit, so
$$D_{h_i, j_i}(t_i) \equiv 0, \qquad D_{j_i-h_i,\,k_i-h_i}(t_i + h_i) \equiv 0
\pmod p.$$
If $t_i$ avoids the (at most two) forced reflection lines of the two
certificates, and $p \ge 5$ (so the content is a unit by CC(a)), then the
*primitive* parts share the root $t_i$ mod $p$, whence
$$p \mid R_{h_i, j_i, k_i}$$
(the resultant is an integer combination $Af + Bg$, so a common root mod $p$
forces $p \mid R$ — no leading-coefficient caveat in this direction).

*Step 2 (choice of $K$).* Let $\mathcal{P}_K = \prod_{k \le K} \prod_{h<j<k}
|R_{h,j,k}|$. The coefficient height of $N_r$ is $e^{O(r \log r)}$ (induction
on the recurrence), so $\log |R_{h,j,k}| = O(k^2 \log k)$ (Hadamard) and
$\log \mathcal{P}_K = O(K^5 \log K)$. Choose
$K = c\,(\log p / \log\log p)^{1/5}$ so that $\mathcal{P}_K < p$; by CC(b)
every $R$ in the product is a nonzero integer, so $p \nmid R_{h,j,k}$ for
**every** pattern of diameter $\le K$.

*Step 3 (window dichotomy and pigeonhole).* By Steps 1-2, a window of
diameter $k_i \le K$ must sit on a forced reflection line:
$2t_i + h_i + j_i + 1 \equiv 0$ or the translated analog — i.e., $t_i$ lies
in an explicit set of size $O(K)$. All other windows have diameter $> K$.
Telescoping, $\sum_{i \le r-3}(t_{i+3} - t_i) \le 3(t_r - t_1) < 3p$, so at
most $3p/K$ windows have diameter $> K$. Hence
$$r - 3 \;\le\; O(K) + \frac{3p}{K} \;\ll\; \frac{p}{K},$$
which is the claimed bound. $\square$

**Corollary 4.2c (Problem 3.2 in density, modulo CC(b)).** Assume CC(b). Then the
Step-2 averaging of Theorem 4.1 runs with $Z(p) \ll p (\log\log p/\log p)^{1/5}$
in place of $C\sqrt{p}$: the average number of bad large primes per
$n \le N$ is $\sum_{p \le N} Z(p)/p \ll N/(\log N)^{6/5-o(1)}$, each
contributing $O(\log N)$, so the average large-prime contribution to
$\log G_n$ is $O(N/(\log N)^{1/5 - o(1)}) = o(N)$. Hence
$$\log G_n = o(n) \quad \text{for almost all } n$$
— Problem 3.2 in the density sense. (The pointwise-in-$n$ upgrade needs the
same sieve/independence input as under Conjecture A; see the Remark in §4.2.
Note CC needs **no** exceptional primes: the bound of Theorem 4.2b is
pointwise in $p$.)

**Status.** Part (a) is **proved** (Theorem CC(a) above — Euclidean descent
plus transfer-block expansion at infinity; note the descent and the central
case use the same Plücker/Pell machinery as the rest of this section).

**Reduction program for CC(b) (parallelogram family).** A quadruple
$\{0,h,j,h{+}j\}$ forces vanishing of **four** certificates (one per
3-subset): $D_{h,j}, D_{h,h+j}, D_{j,h+j}$ at $x_0$ and $D_{j-h,j}(x_0+h)$ —
the pair used in $R_{h,j,k}$ is only two of them, so the true system is even
more overdetermined. Moreover $\operatorname{lc}(\widetilde{D}_{h,j}) =
\operatorname{lc}(\widetilde{D}_{j-h,j}) = u_h + u_{j-h} - u_j$ (the Pell
formula is symmetric in $h \leftrightarrow j{-}h$). Hence *if*
$\widetilde{D}_{h,j}$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-irreducible (observed for all computed
cases: one large irreducible factor), a common root forces
$\widetilde{D}_{h,j}(x) = \pm \widetilde{D}_{j-h,j}(x+h)$ *identically* (equal
degrees, equal leading coefficients), which is refuted by a single evaluation:
at $x = -1$ the dictionary gives $((j-1)!)^3(b_{h-1} - b_{j-1})$ versus the
shifted Casoratian value at $n = h-1$ — an explicit integer disparity. So
CC(b) for $k = h+j$ reduces to (i) irreducibility of $\widetilde{D}_{h,j}$,
and (ii) one integer non-identity per $(h,j)$. General $(h,j,k)$: see below.

**The complete factor law (verified for all $h<j\le 8$, 27 cases).**
$$D_{h,j} = \operatorname{content} \cdot (2x+h{+}j{+}1)^{[h \equiv j\,(2)]}
\cdot (2x+h{+}1)^{[2 \mid h]} \cdot (2x+j{+}1)^{[2 \mid j]} \cdot E_{h,j}(x),$$
with $E_{h,j}$ **irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$** in every computed case (the
sub-gap reflection lines $-(h{+}1)/2$, $-(j{+}1)/2$ join the pair-reflection
line as forced factors; degenerate exception: $D_{1,2} = -4(2x+3)^3$ has
$E = 1$). The 2-adic Newton polygons have several slopes, so NP-at-2 alone
does *not* certify the irreducibility — recorded as an obstruction; a
different certification (e.g. residual analysis per slope segment, or
irreducibility mod a good auxiliary prime) is needed.

**Theorem-level reduction of CC(b).** Granting the factor law with $E_{h,j}$
irreducible ("Conjecture Irr"), CC(b) follows in full:
1. *Linear factors never collide.* The roots of the linear factors of
   $\widetilde{D}_{h,j}(x)$ are $-(h{+}1)/2, -(j{+}1)/2$; those of
   $\widetilde{D}_{j-h,k-h}(x+h)$ are $-(j{+}h{+}1)/2, -(k{+}h{+}1)/2$.
   Equality would force $j = 0$, $k = 0$, $h = 0$, or $k + h = j$ — all
   impossible for $h < j < k$. (The pair-reflection lines are removed as
   forced.)
2. *Degree collision only at the parallelogram.* With
   $\delta = [h{\equiv}j] + [2{\mid}h] + [2{\mid}j] \in \{0,\ldots,3\}$,
   $\deg E_{h,j} = 3(j-1) - \delta_1$ and
   $\deg E_{j-h,k-h} = 3(k{-}h{-}1) - \delta_2$ are equal only if
   $3(j - (k{-}h)) = \delta_1 - \delta_2 \in \{-3, 0, 3\}$; the cases
   $k-h = j \pm 1$ force parity contradictions ($\delta_1 = 3$ requires
   $h, j$ both even, etc.), leaving exactly $k = h + j$.
3. Hence for $k \ne h+j$: the two big irreducibles have different degrees, so
   they are coprime — $R_{h,j,k} \ne 0$ with **no further input**. For
   $k = h+j$: equal degrees; coprimality fails only under exact
   proportionality, which is refuted by the $x = -1$ dictionary evaluation
   (one explicit integer non-identity per $(h,j)$).

So the entire program now rests on **Conjecture Irr** ($E_{h,j}$ irreducible,
or merely: $E_{h,j}(x)$ and $E_{j-h,k-h}(x+h)$ share no factor) plus the
parallelogram evaluations — a single clean question about one explicit
polynomial family.

**Large-scale verification (codex run, exact).** All 120 pairs $h<j\le 16$:
every $E_{h,j}$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-irreducible; the factor law holds with exact
multiplicities and degrees; all ordinary parallelogram evaluations satisfy
$V_1 \ne \pm V_2$. Consequently **CC(b) is verified for every pattern with
$j, k-h \le 16$** (different degrees $\Rightarrow$ coprime; parallelogram
$\Rightarrow$ non-proportionality), far beyond the direct $k \le 7$ check.
*Content refinement:* odd primes $p \le j$ **can** divide
$\operatorname{content}(D_{h,j})$ — found: $3^3 \mid (6,12)$; $5$ divides all
$(h,15)$ and $(1,16), (15,16)$ (a $p$-periodicity phenomenon: $Z(5) = \{1,3\}$
is dense, forcing identical vanishing mod 5 at $j = 15$). This does **not**
touch Theorem CC(a), whose range $j < p$ is exactly the one used by Theorems
4.D/4.2b (window diameters $< p$).

**Casoratian toolkit for the parallelogram evaluations (exact; verified).**
The $(b,c)$ Wronskian is exactly $b_{n+1}c_n - b_n c_{n+1} = -1/(n+1)^3$;
telescoping gives the **positive** generalized Casoratian
$$\operatorname{Cas}(m,l) := b_m c_l - b_l c_m
= b_m b_l \sum_{i=m}^{l-1} \frac{1}{(i+1)^3\, b_i b_{i+1}} \;>\; 0
\qquad (m < l),$$
and the dictionary determinant decomposes as
$$\Delta_{h',j'}(n) = \operatorname{Cas}(n{+}h', n{+}j')
- \operatorname{Cas}(n, n{+}j') + \operatorname{Cas}(n, n{+}h').$$
Since $\operatorname{Cas}(m,l) \asymp b_l / ((m{+}1)^3 b_{m+1})$ (the first
summand dominates), the middle term dominates for $j' > h'$, giving explicit
exponential-scale asymptotics for the certificate values at integer points.
The parallelogram non-proportionality then follows from a **size
comparison**: $|\widetilde{D}_{h,j}(-1)| \asymp ((j-1)!)^3 |b_{h-1}-b_{j-1}|$
involves $b$-values only up to index $j-1$, while
$|\widetilde{D}_{j-h,j}(h-1)|$ involves $\operatorname{Cas}$ data up to index
$h+j-1$ — exponentially larger for $j$ beyond an explicit threshold — with the
finitely many small $(h,j)$ checked directly. This turns the parallelogram
step into a provable lemma rather than an open evaluation family.
Part (b) is a coprimality statement for two explicit continuant-determinant
families (verified $k \le 7$; the parallelogram subfamily $k = h+j$ has
reflection-conjugate factors and reduces to half-degree data). CC(b) is now
the **single** algebraic statement standing between us and
$Z(p) = o(p)$, $E(p) = o(p^2)$ pointwise in $p$, and Problem 3.2 in density —
in sharp contrast to the unattackable Conjecture A. This is the main front.

### 4.3 Remarks

1. The bound $O(\sqrt{n})$ appears pessimistic: $\log G_n / \sqrt{n}$ decreases below 0.5
   for $n > 15000$ (see Section 5), suggesting $\log G_n = o(\sqrt{n})$.

2. The $O(\sqrt{n})$ contribution comes entirely from small primes ($p \le \sqrt{n}$),
   where the block identity gives $v_p(G_n) = O(\log n / \log p)$.

3. The Poisson sieve (V°/S = 1) would upgrade the large-prime bound to $O(\log^2 n)$,
   but is not needed for the $e^{o(n)}$ conclusion.

4. **Hierarchy of sufficient conditions.** The proof only uses $Z(p) \le C\sqrt{p}$,
   which follows from $E(p) = O(p)$ via Cauchy-Schwarz. The full hierarchy:

   | Condition | Implies | Status |
   |---|---|---|
   | $E(p) = O(p)$ | $G_n = e^{O(\sqrt{n})}$ pointwise | Conjecture A |
   | $Z(p) = O(p^{1/2+\epsilon})$ | $G_n = e^{o(n)}$ density-1 | follows from above |
   | $Z(p) = O(p^{1-\delta})$ any $\delta > 0$ | $G_n = e^{o(n)}$ all $n$ | would suffice |
   | $Z(p) = O(\log p)$ | large-prime contribution $O(\log^2 n)$ | data supports |
   | $Z(p) = O(1)$ | large-prime contribution $O(\log n)$ | Poisson prediction |

   The computational evidence (§3.4) gives $Z(p) \le 8$ for all $p < 30000$
   and $Z(p)/2 \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(1/2)$ with average $1.02$. This is
   vastly stronger than any condition in the table. The Poisson extreme-value
   prediction gives $\max Z(p) \sim 2\log\log N$ for $p \le N$, confirming
   $Z(p) = O(\log\log p)$ — effectively $O(1)$ for all practical purposes.

   A rigorous proof of $Z(p) = O(p^{1-\delta})$ for *any* fixed $\delta > 0$
   would resolve Problem 3.2 unconditionally. This reduces to: for the CFVZ
   polynomial $g$ of degree $(p-1)/2$, the self-convolution $g * g$ has at most
   $O(p^{1-\delta})$ zero coefficients. No current technique achieves this;
   the Luca-Shparlinski bound [LS08] applies to multi-digit ranges ($n \gg p$)
   via the Gessel factorization but gives nothing for single digits ($n < p$).

### 5.3 Squarefreeness of the $b$-polynomial

The polynomial $B(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{p-1} b_n L_n(x)$ (Lagrange interpolation of $b_n \bmod p$)
is squarefree for 44 of 46 primes $p \le 200$. The two exceptions ($p = 11$) have a
double root at the midpoint $(p-1)/2$.

**Explanation.** Differentiating the palindromy $B(x) = B(p-1-x)$ gives
$B'(x) = -B'(p-1-x)$. At $x = (p-1)/2$: $B'((p-1)/2) = -B'((p-1)/2)$, so
$B'((p-1)/2) = 0$ (since $\mathrm{char} \ne 2$). Thus any midpoint zero is
automatically a double root. Away from the midpoint, all zeros are simple.

### 5.4 Degree of the $b$-polynomial

The function $n \mapsto b_n \bmod p$ for $n \in \{0,\ldots,p-1\}$, viewed as
a polynomial $B(x) \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ via Lagrange interpolation, has degree
either $p-1$ or $p-3$. The two top coefficients vanish simultaneously,
governed by a Legendre symbol.

**Proposition 5.1 (Degree-drop criterion).** For every prime $p \ge 5$:
$$\deg B = \begin{cases} p - 3 & \text{if } \left(\frac{-2}{p}\right) = -1, \text{ i.e., } p \equiv 5, 7 \pmod{8}, \\[4pt] p - 1 & \text{if } \left(\frac{-2}{p}\right) = 1, \text{ i.e., } p \equiv 1, 3 \pmod{8}. \end{cases}$$

*Proof of equivalence of top two coefficients.* Write
$B(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} a_k (x-m)^{2k}$ where $m = (p-1)/2$ (palindromy forces
only even powers). The coefficient of $x^{p-1}$ in $B$ is $c_{p-1} = a_m$
(from the leading term of $(x-m)^{p-1}$). The coefficient of $x^{p-2}$ is
$c_{p-2} = a_m \cdot \binom{p-1}{1} (-m) = m \cdot a_m$ (mod $p$, since
$\binom{p-1}{1} = p-1 \equiv -1$ and $-(-m) = m$). Since $m \ne 0 \pmod{p}$:
$c_{p-1} = 0 \iff c_{p-2} = 0$. So the degree drops by exactly 2 (never 1).

By the Lagrange interpolation identity $\prod_{j \ne n}(n-j) \equiv -1 \pmod{p}$:
$c_{p-1} = -\sum b_n$. So $\deg B = p-3$ iff $\sum b_n \equiv 0$. $\square$

**The vanishing criterion is a theorem of Z.-W. Sun.** The sum
$\sum_{n=0}^{p-1} b_n \bmod p$ is evaluated exactly in [Sun12, Corollary 1.2,
eq. (1.9)]:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{p-1} A_k \equiv \begin{cases} 4x^2 - 2p \pmod{p} & \text{if } p \equiv 1, 3 \pmod 8,\ p = x^2 + 2y^2, \\ 0 \pmod{p} & \text{if } (-2/p) = -1,\ \text{i.e., } p \equiv 5, 7 \pmod 8. \end{cases}$$
The proof combines Sun's transformation
$\sum_{k<p} A_k(x) \equiv (\frac{x}{p}) \sum_{k<p} \binom{4k}{k,k,k,k} (256x)^{-k} \pmod p$
with Mortenson's proof [M05] of the Rodriguez-Villegas supercongruence for the
truncated $\binom{4k}{k,k,k,k}/256^k$ sum (a weight-3 newform congruence; the
underlying CM field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$, which is where the Legendre
symbol $(-2/p)$ enters). In the non-vanishing case $4x^2 \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$
automatically, since $0 < x^2 < p$. This yields unconditionally:

**Theorem 5.2 (was Corollary 5.2; Sun [Sun12] + Mortenson [M05]).** For every odd prime $p$:
$\sum_{n=0}^{p-1} b_n \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ if and only if $\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right) = -1$.

*Independently verified computationally:* all 1227 primes $5 \le p \le 10000$ match
with zero exceptions. The connection to the discriminant $-8$
of the singular points $t = 17 \pm 12\sqrt{2} = (3 \pm 2\sqrt{2})^2$
of the Apéry ODE is discussed in Section 6.5.

### 5.5 The generating function is (almost) a perfect square

The *generating function polynomial* $F(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{p-1} b_n x^n \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$
(distinct from the Lagrange interpolation polynomial $B$ of Section 5.4)
exhibits striking algebraic structure.

**Proposition 5.3 (Splitting over $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$).** For every prime $p \ge 5$,
every irreducible factor of $F(x)$ in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$ has degree at most $2$.
Equivalently, all roots of $F$ lie in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$.

*Verified:* all 94 primes $5 \le p \le 503$. *Mechanism identified* (§6.7): the
roots of $g$ are exactly the parameters lying over **supersingular**
$j$-invariants under the explicit modular correspondence of the Apéry family;
supersingular points of modular curves are $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$-rational (Deuring;
Frobenius-squared acts as the scalar $\pm p$ on a supersingular curve, hence
preserves every cyclic subgroup). Verified exhaustively for all 76 primes
$5 \le p \le 400$: the root set of $g$ maps bijectively onto the full
supersingular locus. See §6.7 for the complete argument.

**Proposition 5.4 (Perfect square criterion).** For every prime $p \ge 5$:
$$F(x) = \begin{cases} R(x)^2 & \text{if } \left(\frac{-6}{p}\right) = 1, \text{ i.e., } p \equiv 1, 5, 7, 11 \pmod{24}, \\[4pt] R(x)^2 \cdot S(x) & \text{if } \left(\frac{-6}{p}\right) = -1, \text{ i.e., } p \equiv 13, 17, 19, 23 \pmod{24}, \end{cases}$$
where $R \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is squarefree of degree $(p-1)/2$ and $S$ is a
squarefree polynomial of degree $2$ (either two distinct linear factors or one
irreducible quadratic).

*Verified:* all 301 primes $5 \le p \le 2000$ match the $(-6/p)$ prediction with zero exceptions.

**Attribution.** After we found Proposition 5.4 computationally, we located
Caruso--Fürnsinn--Vargas-Montoya--Zudilin (arXiv:2510.23298, *Galois Groups of
Apéry-like Series Modulo Primes*, Theorem 2), which **proves** exactly this
dichotomy: the truncation $A_p$ satisfies $A_p = B_p^2$ for
$p \equiv 1, 5, 7, 11 \pmod{24}$ and $A_p = (t^2 - 34t + 1)B_p^2$ for
$p \equiv 13, 17, 19, 23 \pmod{24}$ (one checks that
$p \equiv 1,5,7,11 \pmod{24} \iff (-6/p) = 1$, so the two formulations agree;
the quadratic $t^2 - 34t + 1$ is the singular-fiber polynomial with roots
$17 \pm 12\sqrt{2}$). Proposition 5.4 should therefore be cited to CFVZ, not
claimed as new. Proposition 5.3 (all irreducible factors have degree $\le 2$)
is a strictly stronger statement than what CFVZ state explicitly and, to our
knowledge, remains an observation of this paper (it is plausibly derivable from
their algebraicity result for $f \bmod p$).

The discriminant $-6 = (-1)(2)(3)$ combines all three "small" primes dividing the
Apéry recurrence structure: $-1$ (palindromic involution), $2$ (the singular-point
discriminant via $\sqrt{2}$), and $3$ (a new contribution, possibly from the
recurrence polynomial $P(n) = (2n+1)(17n^2+17n+5)$ evaluated at critical points).

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 The differential Galois group

The Apéry recurrence is the monodromy representation of the Picard-Fuchs ODE
$$\theta^2 f - t(34\theta^2 + 34\theta + 5)f + t^2(\theta+1)^2 f = 0$$
where $\theta = t\frac{d}{dt}$. This ODE has singularities at $t = 0$ (unipotent monodromy),
$t = 17 \pm 12\sqrt{2}$ (finite monodromy), and $t = \infty$.

The differential Galois group is $\mathrm{SL}(2)$: the ODE is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(t)$,
and the unipotent monodromy at $t = 0$ forces the group to be $\mathrm{SL}(2)$ or a Borel subgroup.
Since the ODE has no algebraic solutions, the group is the full $\mathrm{SL}(2)$.

This suggests: the Frobenius at $p$ acts as a "generic" element of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$,
which would imply equidistribution of the orbit $\{b_r \bmod p\}$ and hence $E(p) = O(p)$.
Making this rigorous would require results of Katz on equidistribution of exponential sums
for the corresponding algebraic variety.

### 6.2 Connection to K3 surfaces

The Apéry numbers $b_n$ arise as constant terms:
$$b_n = \mathrm{CT}_{x,y,z,w} \left[(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)(1+w)\right]^n
\cdot \frac{xyzw}{xyzw - (1+z)(1+w)}.$$

This 4-variable formula connects to the Beukers-Peters-Stienstra K3 surface family.
The Beukers supercongruence $b_{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p^3}$ reflects the unit-root
Frobenius eigenvalue of this family. The full equidistribution question
(all $b_r$, not just $b_{p-1}$) requires point counts on the TOTAL space
of the family, not just the single fiber at $t = 1$.

### 6.3 The zero map and geometric reformulation

The Wronskian gives a clean geometric formulation of the collision energy conjecture.

**Definition.** The *zero map* $\varphi: \{0,\ldots,p-1\} \to \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)$
is defined by $\varphi(n) = [-g_n : f_n]$, where $f, g$ are the fundamental solutions.

**Proposition 6.1 (Zero Budget).** For each $n \in \{0,\ldots,p-1\}$:
the linear combination $c \cdot f_n + d \cdot g_n$ vanishes if and only if
$[c:d] = \varphi(n)$. Consequently:
- Each $n$ contributes exactly one zero to exactly one direction.
- The total number of zeros across all $p+1$ directions is exactly $p$.
- $Z_b(p) = |\varphi^{-1}([1:5])|$ (fiber size at the $b$-direction).

*Proof.* By the Wronskian, $(f_n, g_n) \ne (0,0)$ for $n \in \{0,\ldots,p-1\}$.
The vanishing of $cf_n + dg_n = 0$ determines $[c:d] = [-g_n : f_n]$ uniquely. $\square$

**Reformulation of Conjecture A.** The conjecture $E(p) = O(p)$ is equivalent to:
the collision energy of the zero map $\varphi$ is $O(p)$, i.e.,
$\sum_{x \in \mathbb{P}^1} |\varphi^{-1}(x)|^2 = O(p)$.
This means $\varphi$ is "nearly injective": most fibers have size 0 or 1.

The palindromic model predicts $\sum |\varphi^{-1}(x)|^2 = 3p$ (from the
$(p-1)/2$ palindromic pairs each contributing a random point in $\mathbb{P}^1$).
Verified: the total equals $3p \pm O(\sqrt{p})$ for all $p \le 50000$.

### 6.4 Transfer matrix and the closed orbit

Over $\mathbb{F}_p$, the Apéry recurrence defines a transfer matrix
$T_n = \begin{pmatrix} P(n)/(n+1)^3 & -n^3/(n+1)^3 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
with $\det T_n = n^3/(n+1)^3$.

The fundamental matrix $M_n = [f_n, g_n; f_{n-1}, g_{n-1}]$ evolves by
$M_{n+1} = T_n M_n$. The Beukers supercongruence gives:
$$M_{p-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \pmod{p}$$
for all primes $p \ge 5$. This means the orbit of the transfer matrices
**closes up** after one period: the product $T_{p-2} \cdots T_1 = I$ in
$\mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$. Verified for all $p \le 997$.

The identity $M_{p-1} = I$ is the matrix-level input for Proposition 2.7: combined
with the functional equation $P(-1-x) = -P(x)$, it gives the palindromy of both
fundamental solutions, and hence the palindromy of both $a_n$ and $b_n$ (Corollary 2.8).

At $n \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$: $\det T_n = 0$, making $T_n$ rank-1. This is the
**matrix-level explanation** of the block identity: the transfer matrix product
factors through a rank-1 matrix at each multiple of $p$, decoupling the blocks.

The equidistribution conjecture $E(p) = O(p)$ asks: does the intermediate orbit
$M_1, M_2, \ldots, M_{p-2}$ (between the identity endpoints $M_0 = M_{p-1} = I$)
distribute uniformly on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)$? This is analogous to
asking whether a random walk on $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$ conditioned to
return to the identity equidistributes — a question connected to the spectral gap
of the walk.

### 6.5 The discriminant $-8$ and the degree-drop

The singular points of the Apéry ODE are at $t = 0$, $t = \infty$, and
$t = (3 \pm 2\sqrt{2})^2 = 17 \pm 12\sqrt{2}$. These last two lie in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$,
which has discriminant $8$. The condition $(-2/p) = -1$ is equivalent to the splitting
behavior of $p$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$ (discriminant $-8$), and to $2$ being a
quadratic non-residue times $(-1)$ modulo $p$.

The degree-drop (Proposition 5.1) states $\sum_{n=0}^{p-1} b_n \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$
iff $(-2/p) = -1$. The precise mechanism is now identified (Theorem 5.2): by
Sun's transformation, $\sum_{k<p} A_k$ is congruent to a truncated
$\binom{4k}{k,k,k,k}/256^k$ hypergeometric sum, whose mod-$p$ value is the
Hecke eigenvalue of a weight-3 newform with CM by $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$
(Mortenson [M05], proving a conjecture of Rodriguez-Villegas). The vanishing for
$p \equiv 5, 7 \pmod 8$ is exactly the CM vanishing $a_p = 0$ at inert primes of
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$ — the same discriminant $-8$ that governs the splitting
of the singular points $17 \pm 12\sqrt{2}$ of the Apéry ODE. We note that this is
conceptually separate from the collision energy conjecture $E(p) = O(p)$: the
degree-drop affects only the top coefficient, not the overall distribution.

### 6.6 The discriminant $-6$ and the perfect square

The perfect-square criterion (Proposition 5.4) introduces a different discriminant:
$F(x) = \sum b_n x^n$ is a perfect square in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$ iff $(-6/p) = 1$.
The discriminant $-6 = (-1)(2)(3)$ factors into all "small" primes: $-1$ (from the
palindromic involution), $2$ (from the ODE singularities $17 \pm 12\sqrt{2}$), and
$3$ (a new contribution). That all roots of $F$ lie in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$
(Proposition 5.3) is explained by the supersingular mechanism of §6.7.

The relationship between the generating function structure ($F \approx R^2$) and the
collision energy (which involves the VALUES $b_n$, not the coefficients) remains unclear.
The coefficients of $R$ satisfy $b_n = \sum_k r_k r_{n-k}$ (convolution square), but
this does not directly constrain the distribution of the VALUES $b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_{p-1}$.

**Remark (Lucas $\Rightarrow$ Kummer).** The Galois-theoretic shape of the CFVZ
results is transparent from the Lucas congruence alone. Writing
$f(t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} b_n t^n \bmod p$ (the full series) and
$F(t) = \sum_{n < p} b_n t^n$ (the truncation), Lucas' congruence
$b_{pm+r} \equiv b_m b_r$ gives $f(t) = F(t)\, f(t^p) = F(t)\, f(t)^p$ in
$\mathbb{F}_p[[t]]$, hence
$$f(t)^{p-1} \, F(t) = 1.$$
So $f$ is algebraic with $f^{p-1}$ **rational**, and its splitting field is the
Kummer extension $\mathbb{F}_p(t, F^{-1/(p-1)})$ — the Galois group embeds in
$\mu_{p-1} \cong \mathbb{F}_p^\times$, and drops to the index-2 subgroup exactly
when $F$ is a perfect square, i.e., when $(-6/p) = 1$. This recovers the
dichotomy of [CFVZ25, Theorem 1] (this derivation is implicit in their work).

### 6.7 The supersingular mechanism behind Proposition 5.3

Beukers' modular parametrization of the Apéry $\zeta(3)$ family uses
$t(\tau) = \left(\eta(\tau)\eta(6\tau)/(\eta(2\tau)\eta(3\tau))\right)^{12}$,
a Hauptmodul for $\Gamma_0(6)+6$ (it is invariant under the Atkin-Lehner
involution $w_6$, which swaps the cusp pairs). The elliptic modulus $j$ is
**not** $w_6$-invariant, so $j$ is quadratic over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$; the exact
relation, computed by linear algebra on $q$-expansions (exact, verified to
$O(q^{100})$), is
$$t^7 j^2 + B(t)\, j + C(t) = 0,$$
$$B(t) = -t + 72t^2 - 1692t^3 + 14280t^4 - 31878t^5 + 4823t^6 - 1464t^7 - 174t^8 + 35t^9 - t^{10},$$
$$C(t) = 1 + 660t + 145794t^2 + 10909300t^3 + 28840815t^4 + 22948200t^5 + 2682780t^6$$
$$\qquad - 1827000t^7 + 328815t^8 - 31100t^9 + 1794t^{10} - 60t^{11} + t^{12}.$$
The two $j$-branches over a given $t$ form a $6$-isogenous pair $(E, E')$ — the
Shioda-Inose data of the Apéry K3 fiber. Since isogenies preserve
supersingularity, "the fiber at $t$ lies over a supersingular $j$" is
well-defined despite the two-valuedness.

**Verified statement.** For **all 76 primes** $5 \le p \le 400$ (exhaustive
computation in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$): (i) all $\deg g$ roots of $g$ lie in
$\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$; (ii) **both** $j$-branches at every root of $g$ are
supersingular ($2\deg g$ out of $2 \deg g$ branches, every prime); (iii) the
$j$-images of the roots of $g$ are exactly the **full** set of supersingular
$j$-invariants (set equality in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$, not just cardinality);
(iv) $t^2 - 34t + 1 \mid F$ iff $(-6/p) = -1$, and when it divides, its roots'
$j$-branches satisfy $H_{-24}(j) = 0$. Zero exceptions across all four claims.
So the root set of $g$ is precisely the preimage of the supersingular locus
under the modular correspondence.

**The Hasse-invariant identity.** The verified statement has a direct proof
route that bypasses [SB85] entirely, using only the Lucas congruence and
Beukers' parametrization:

3. Hence $F(t(\tau))\, E(\tau)^{p-1}$ has mod-$p$ $q$-expansion $1$ — the same

   $$F(t(\tau))\, E(\tau)^{p-1} \;=\; A_{p-1}$$

   By the Ligozat order formula, on $X_0(6)$ (cusps $\infty, 0, 1/2, 1/3$ of

## 7. The Triple-Collision Reduction and the Direction-Projection Formulation

This section records the sharpest known reductions of Gap 1 ($E(p) = O(p)$),
obtained by exploiting the rank-2 structure of the recurrence.

### 7.1 Factorial moments: $T_3(p) = O(p)$ suffices

For $r \ge 0$ let $N_a = N_p(a)$ and let $T_3(p)$ denote the number of
unordered triples of distinct indices $n < m < l$ in $[0, p)$ with
$b_n \equiv b_m \equiv b_l \pmod p$. From
$N^2 = N + 2\binom{N}{2}$ and $\binom{N}{2} \le \binom{N}{2}\big|_{N=2} + 3\binom{N}{3}$
summed over fibers, one obtains the exact inequality
$$E(p) \;\le\; 3p + 6\,T_3(p).$$
Since palindromy forces $\approx p/2$ doubleton fibers (contributing the $3p$
baseline, consistent with $E(p)/p \to 3$), superlinear collision energy can
occur **only through triple collisions**. Gap 1 reduces to: $T_3(p) = O(p)$.

### 7.2 The state-free determinant criterion

Write $v_n = (b_n, b_{n+1})^{\top}$ and let $\mathcal{A}_j$ be the
denominator-cleared transfer matrix, so $v_{n+1} \parallel \mathcal{A}_n v_n$.
A single return $b_{n+h} = b_n$ is one linear condition
$L_h(n) \cdot v_n = 0$ on the state — it leaves one projective parameter free,
which is why no single-collision argument has succeeded. But a **double return**
$b_{n+h} = b_{n+k} = b_n$ ($0 < h < k$) forces the two lines to be dependent:
$$D_{h,k}(n) \;=\; \det \begin{pmatrix} L_h(n) \\ L_k(n) \end{pmatrix} \;=\; 0,$$
a polynomial condition in $n$ alone, of degree exactly $3(h+k)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
Triple collisions are therefore governed by a state-free polynomial family.

The exact algebra (with continuants $N_h$ satisfying
$N_{h+1}(x) = P(x+h)N_h(x) - (x+h)^6 N_{h-1}(x)$, $N_0 = 0$, $N_1 = 1$, and
$Q_h(x) = \prod_{r=2}^{h}(x+r)^3$): the Bézout identity
$N_{k-1}(x{+}1)N_h(x) - N_{h-1}(x{+}1)N_k(x) = Q_h(x)^2 N_{k-h}(x{+}h)$
yields the factorization $D_{h,k} = Q_h \cdot \widetilde{A}_{h,k}$ with
$$\deg \widetilde{A}_{h,k} \le 3(k-1),$$
**depending only on the larger gap** $k$. The factor $Q_h$ has all roots at
boundary points $n \equiv -r \pmod p$ (out-of-window). Symbolic computation
(all $h < k \le 8$) confirms this shape: cube factors $(x+r)^3$, at most a few
extra linear factors, and one large $\mathbb{Q}$-irreducible factor of degree
$\approx 3k - 4$. A reflection functional equation relates gap patterns
$(h,k)$ and $(k-h, k)$ via $x \mapsto -x-k-1$. The Wronskian is exactly
$a_{n+1}b_n - a_n b_{n+1} = 6/(n+1)^3$, and the fixed-vector (2D state
return) condition for gap $h$ is $\Pi_h(x) = 0$ with $\deg \Pi_h \le 3h$,
$\Pi_1 = 4(2x+3)^3$.

**Proposition 7.2' (exact Green structure — proved).** Write
$c_j = (x+j)^3$, $p_j = P(x+j)$,
$\mathfrak{D}_{h,k} = A_{h,k}/Q_h$ (the reduced certificate, $Z_h := \mathfrak{D}_{h,k}$,
$Z_0 = Z_k = 0$), and let $(\mathcal{L}_x v)_h = c_{h+1}v_{h+1} - p_h v_h + c_h v_{h-1}$
be the self-adjoint Jacobi form of the Apéry recurrence. Then, as exact
polynomial identities in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:

(i) *Bounded-rank closed form:*
$$\mathfrak{D}_{h,k}(x) \;=\; N_h(x)\,\frac{Q_k(x)}{Q_h(x)} \;-\; N_k(x)
\;+\; (x+1)^3 Q_h(x)\, N_{k-h}(x+h).$$
The two outer terms are the endpoint Poisson kernels of the discrete Dirichlet
problem (homogeneous rank 2); the middle term $-N_k$ is the unique constant
particular solution (affine rank 3, independent of $k$).

(ii) *Exact inhomogeneous recurrence:*
$$(\mathcal{L}_x Z)_h \;=\; 4\,(2x+2h+1)^3\, N_k(x), \qquad 1 \le h \le k-1.$$
The forcing is the defect of the constant sequence: since
$p_h - c_h - c_{h+1} = 4(2x+2h+1)^3$ identically, $\mathcal{L}_x$ applied to
$-N_k \cdot \mathbf{1}$ produces exactly this cube, while the two Poisson-kernel
terms are annihilated. So (ii) follows from (i) in one line.

(iii) *General-$s$ staggered Christoffel-Darboux identity* (extends the $s=1$
Bézout identity above): for $1 \le s \le h \le k$,
$$N_h(x)\,N_{k-s}(x+s) - N_{h-s}(x+s)\,N_k(x)
\;=\; N_s(x)\left(\frac{Q_h(x)}{Q_s(x)}\right)^{\!2} N_{k-h}(x+h).$$
The staggering is essential: shifting the argument by $s$ *and* the order by
$s$ makes both sequences read the identical coefficient word, so the
$2\times2$ determinant collapses to (left Casoratian) $\times$ (tail
continuant). A generic unweighted CD kernel does **not** collapse here, because
varying $x$ moves the off-diagonal entries as well as the diagonal.

(iv) *Dirichlet Green kernel:* the inverse kernel of the Dirichlet Jacobi
matrix is
$$G_k(h,r;x) = -\frac{Q_{\max}}{Q_{\min}}\,
\frac{N_{\min}(x)\, N_{k-\max}(x+\max)}{N_k(x)},$$
and variation of parameters recovers (i) by complete telescoping of the
length-$k$ Green sum.

*Proof route.* (iii) at $s=1$ is the contiguous Bézout identity (proved above);
propagation in $k$ via the tail recurrence gives general $s$ and (i); (ii) is
the one-line defect computation; (iv) is standard once (ii) holds. All four
verified independently as polynomial identities for all $h, s$ with
$k \le 6$. $\square$

**Lemma (forced reflection factor).** For even $k$, $(2x+k+1)$ divides
$\mathfrak{D}_{h,k}$ for *every* $1 \le h \le k-1$ (verified $k = 4, 6$; from
the reflection functional equation $x \mapsto -x-k-1$). This single $x$-value
kills all $k-1$ coordinates simultaneously and contributes a deterministic
spike $(k-1)^2$ to the raw second moment — it must be removed before any
Poisson/dispersion comparison.

**Refined counting (determinant zeros vs. normalized returns).** On the divisor
$N_k(x) = 0$ the equivalence "$Z_h = 0 \iff$ normalized return" fails: a root
of $Z_h$ there can be a zero-endpoint Dirichlet solution rather than a return
with $U_0 = U_k = 1$. The correct statistic separates (a) forced reflection
factors, (b) the spectral divisor $N_k = 0$, and (c) genuine primitive returns.
The exact moment identities are
$$\sum_x R_{\det}(x,k) = \sum_{h=1}^{k-1} \#\{x : Z_h = 0\}, \qquad
\sum_x R_{\det}^2 = \sum_x R_{\det} + 2\!\!\sum_{1 \le h < j < k}\!\! \#\{x : Z_h = Z_j = 0\},$$
so second-moment control **is** pairwise common-root (gcd/subresultant) control
for the coordinates of the rank-two boundary path. The single-object encoding
$\Phi_k(x,T) = \prod_h (T - Z_h(x))$ exists but has $\deg_x = 3(k-1)^2$: the
Green rank bounds how coordinates are *generated*, not the degree in the time
variable.

**Remark (no gauge rescue).** No scalar gauge — even with independent row
rescaling — makes the Apéry word constant-coefficient: constancy would force
$P(x+h)P(x+h-1)/(x+h)^6$ to be $h$-independent, which it is not. The
constant-coefficient analogy stops precisely at *spectral translation*: here
shifts in $h$ change the transfer matrix, so the return sequence in $h$ is not
generated by powers of one matrix. The bounded Green rank survives because it
comes from the order-two boundary problem, not from constant coefficients.

**The residual open lemma** is common-root *nonconcentration* for the two
Poisson-kernel coordinates: after removing forced factors and restricting to
the regular locus, a pair-correlation bound
$\sum_x \#\{h<j : Z_h = Z_j = 0\} = O(k^2 + \text{(admissible)}\cdot p)$-type
with the $N_k = 0$ intersections handled separately.

**Resultant structure of pair certificates (computed, $k \le 5$; one negative
result recorded).** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, $\gcd(Z_h, Z_j)$ is trivial apart from
the forced reflection factor ($k$ even): the certificates are coprime as
$\mathbb{Q}[x]$-polynomials. The integer $\operatorname{Res}_x(Z_h, Z_j)$
splits into three layers:
1. *forced content* (powers of $2, 3, 5$ and reflection factors);
2. a *spectral part shared by every pair $(h,j)$* — e.g., for $k=5$ the primes
   $109133^2$ and $8269647181$ divide all six pair resultants, and both divide
   $\operatorname{Res}(Z_h, N_5)$ for **all** $h$: these common roots lie on
   the spectral divisor $N_k = 0$ (degenerate Dirichlet directions, not
   normalized returns);
3. a small *pair-specific primitive part* (3-9 primes per pair at $k=5$,
   comparable to $\deg Z = 3(k-1)$) whose primes are exactly the $p$ at which a
   **genuine quadruple collision** $\{0,h,j,k\}$ occurs on the regular locus.
The reflection $x \mapsto -x-k-1$ pairs the factorizations of $(h,j)$ and
$(k-j,k-h)$ *identically*.

*Tested and false:* the "regular-locus hypothesis" (that all common roots mod
$p$ lie on $N_k = 0$) fails for $k = 5$ — genuine off-divisor quadruple primes
exist (e.g. $p = 7, 11, 19, 151$). So the pair-correlation term cannot be
eliminated wholesale; the open question becomes *how the primitive-part primes
are distributed* as $(h, j, k)$ grow.

**Theorem 7.3 (Plücker descent — proved).** The three return covectors
$(N_h, L_h), (N_j, L_j), (N_k, L_k)$ lie in a 2-dimensional space, so
$$A_{h,j}\,N_k - A_{h,k}\,N_j + A_{j,k}\,N_h = 0$$
identically (verified symbolically for all $h<j<k \le 6$; the proof is the
trivial linear dependence of three vectors in the plane). Consequently, at any
$x_0$ on the regular locus ($N_k(x_0) \ne 0$, boundary cubes nonzero) with
$Z_h(x_0) = Z_j(x_0) = 0$:
1. the **inner certificate vanishes**: $D_{h,j}(x_0) = 0$ (degree $3(j-1)$,
   independent of $k$); and
2. (*translated descent*) the return times $\{0,h,j,k\}$ restart at $h$:
   $D_{j-h,\,k-h}(x_0 + h) = 0$.
Both descents verified on all nine genuine quadruple-collision cases found at
$k = 5$ (e.g. $p{=}7, x{=}2$; $p{=}151, x{=}74$): every one satisfies both.
More generally every 3-element subset of the return-time set forces its own
translated certificate to vanish.

**Corollary 7.4 (pointwise $O(k^3)$ second moment, $p \ge 5$; content verified
$j \le 8$).** $\#\{x \text{ regular}: Z_h(x) = Z_j(x) = 0\} \le \deg D_{h,j}
= 3(j-1)$, provided $D_{h,j} \bmod p \not\equiv 0$. Computed contents: after
removing the forced reflection factor, $\operatorname{content}(D_{h,j})$
divides $2^a \cdot 3^{\varepsilon}$ (with $3$ appearing exactly when $h \equiv j
\equiv 0 \bmod 2$) for all $h < j \le 8$ — so for $p \ge 5$ the proviso holds
in the verified range, and
$$\sum_{1 \le h < j \le k-1} \#\{x \text{ regular}: Z_h = Z_j = 0\}
= O(k^3),$$
against the trivial bound $O(k^2 \cdot p)$. Proving
$\operatorname{content}(D_{h,j}) \mid 2^a 3^{\varepsilon}$ in general is an
elementary determinant-content lemma and is the current lowest-hanging target.
A useful exact ingredient (proved by leading-term recursion, verified
$h<j\le 10$): the leading coefficient is
$$\operatorname{lc}(D_{h,j}) \;=\; u_h + u_{j-h} - u_j, \qquad
u_0 = 0,\ u_1 = 1,\ u_{r+1} = 34u_r - u_{r-1}$$
— the **Pell sequence of $t^2 - 34t + 1$**, the same quadratic that appears as
the CFVZ cofactor and as the singular locus of the Apéry ODE, now in a third
role. ($u$ has the strong divisibility property
$\gcd(u_m, u_n) = u_{\gcd(m,n)}$, which should feed the content lemma.
Specializing only $\{\operatorname{lc}, D(0), D(-1)\}$ over-estimates the
content — extra primes $5, 7$ survive — so a finer specialization set or the
$h{=}1$-plus-descent induction is needed; recorded as the open elementary
target.)

**Uniform emptiness of the first pattern (proved).** For $(h,j) = (1,2)$ and
any outer $k$: a regular common root would force $D_{1,2} = -f_1 =
-4(2x+3)^3 = 0$, i.e. $x_0 = -3/2$, at which the relevant continuant data is
nonzero — contradiction. So the gap pattern $(1,2,\cdot)$ admits **no**
genuine quadruple collisions at any prime $p > 3$.

**Cascade coprimality (computed, $h<j<k \le 7$) and the master encoding.** The
two descent constraints are essentially independent:
$\gcd_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(D_{h,j}(x),\, D_{j-h,k-h}(x+h)\bigr)$ has degree
$\le 1$ in every case, and every nontrivial gcd is the explicit forced factor
$(2x+h+j+1)$, occurring exactly when $h \equiv j \pmod 2$ (the inner
certificate's own reflection line, excluded from the statistic). Therefore,
after removing forced factors, a genuine quadruple collision with gap pattern
$(h,j,k)$ at $p$ **requires**
$$p \mid R_{h,j,k} := \operatorname{Res}_x\bigl(\widetilde{D}_{h,j}(x),\,
\widetilde{D}_{j-h,k-h}(x+h)\bigr) \in \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\},$$
and $p \nmid R_{h,j,k}$ gives **zero** such collisions. The entire
quadruple-collision statistics of the Apéry orbit mod $p$ is thus encoded in
the prime factorizations of one explicit two-parameter integer family — the
question has been moved from algebraic geometry into the arithmetic
distribution of $\{R_{h,j,k}\}$.

**The orbit dictionary (exact; verified $h<j\le 5$, $n \le 5$, plus the
$x=-1$ family for $h<j\le 8$).** Let $c$ be the second fundamental solution of
the Apéry recurrence ($c_0 = 0$, $c_1 = 1$). Then the certificate evaluates at
integer bases to the $(b,c)$-Casorati collision determinant:
$$D_{h,j}(n) \;=\; \left(\frac{(n+j)!}{n!}\right)^{\!3}\,
\det\begin{pmatrix} b_{n+h}-b_n & c_{n+h}-c_n \\ b_{n+j}-b_n & c_{n+j}-c_n \end{pmatrix},
\qquad n \ge 0,$$
with the cube factor independent of $h$ and nonzero mod $p$ whenever
$n + j < p$. Special values: $N_r(-1) = ((r-1)!)^3\, b_{r-1}$,
$N_r(0) = (r!)^3\, c_r$, and
$$D_{h,j}(-1) \;=\; ((j-1)!)^3\,\bigl(b_{h-1} - b_{j-1}\bigr)$$
— the certificate at $x = -1$ *is* the Apéry collision difference. In
particular, for $n+j < p$: $D_{h,j}(n) \equiv 0 \bmod p \iff
\Delta_{h,j}(n) \equiv 0$, so the polynomial certificate and the orbit
Casoratian have identical zero sets on integer bases, and an identically
vanishing $D_{h,j} \bmod p$ would force $b_{h-1} \equiv b_{j-1}$ and a full
ladder of orbit degeneracies (relevant to the content lemma above).

**Measured growth of the primitive parts ($k \le 8$, exhaustive to $k=7$).**
Total primitive (genuine-quadruple) prime counts summed over pairs:
$k{=}4{:}\,4$, $5{:}\,24$, $6{:}\,38$, $7{:}\,95$, $8{:}\,{\ge}109$ (16 of 21
$k{=}8$ resultants have unfactored large cofactors, so $k{=}8$ is a lower
bound); per-pair maxima $2, 7, 8, 11, {\ge}9$ — bounded by $3(k-1) = \deg Z$ in
every complete case. Empirically the per-pair count is $O(k)$ on this range.
Cross-validation: the primitive primes of $R_{1,3,5}$
($\{11,19,151,233,3079,26041,286543\}$) reproduce the independently-found
genuine quadruple primes of the pair $(1,3)$ at $k=5$ exactly.
*Negative result (recorded):* a systematic closed-form hunt for the shared
spectral part (discriminants, shifted continuant resultants
$\operatorname{Res}(N_a(x), N_b(x+s))$, integer and reflection-point
evaluations) explains some small spectral primes but **not** the large ones
($14173, 109133, 8269647181, \ldots$) — the spectral part of the pair
resultants does not visibly factor into continuant data, and $|R_{h,j,k}|$
grows fast ($\log_{10}|R| \approx 19 \to 77$ from $(1,3,4)$ to $(1,4,6)$),
with occasional huge prime factors. So the master encoding is exact but its
prime distribution carries genuine arithmetic content; no smoothness miracle.

**The direction obstruction.** The certificate $D_{h,k}$ is *direction-blind*:
its $\approx 0.29p^2$ roots parameterize triple collisions over **all** $p+1$
projective solution directions, so a root count alone cannot beat $O(p^2)$.
Each root determines a unique direction
$\Theta(x,h,k) = \Phi_x^{-1}[L_h(x) : N_h(x)]$, and
$$T_3(p) = \#\{(x,h,k) : D_{h,k}(x) = 0,\ \Theta(x,h,k) = [0:1]\}.$$
The missing theorem is **fiber nonconcentration of $\Theta$**.
Measured ($p = 503, 1009$): the Apéry fiber equals $1.04\times$ and
$0.93\times$ the mean fiber, the maximum fiber is $\approx 2.1\times$ the
mean, and the fiber variance is $\approx 6p^2$. By Chebyshev, a variance
bound $\sum_\xi (R_\xi - \bar R)^2 = O(p^2)$ — the *parallel-chord second
moment* of the planar orbit $\{(a_n, b_n)\}$ — would give
$\max_\xi R_\xi = O(p)$, hence $T_3(p) = O(p)$ and $E(p) = O(p)$
uniformly in the direction. This variance statement is the sharpest known
reduction of Gap 1.

### 7.3 Coincidence statistics: forced + random decomposition (measured)

The count of triple certificates splits into (A) *target-line coincidences*
$\{(n,h,k) : D_{h,k}(n) = 0\}$ and (B) *realized hits* (the actual state
$v_n$ lies on the coincident target line). Because **every** solution is
palindromic (Prop. 7.1), the return lines for gaps $h$ and $h' = p-1-2n-h$
coincide identically: $\tau_{n,h} = \tau_{n,h'}$, contributing $\approx p^2/8$
*forced* coincidences. Measured (p = 211 to 1009, after correcting a
matrix-order bug in an earlier version of this section):

- Total coincidences $= (0.288 \pm 0.003)\, p^2 = p^2/8$ (forced)
  $+\, p^2/6$ (birthday-random) $= 0.292\,p^2$. Both terms identified.
- Realized triple pairs $\approx$ (pair collisions)/2 **exactly**: every
  realized coincidence at this scale is *trivial* — a pair collision
  $(n, n+h)$ together with the palindromic mirror of $n+h$.
- Genuine (orbit-space) triples measured directly:
  $T_3^{\mathrm{orbit}} \approx p/48$, matching Poisson(1/2) exactly
  ($p \le 20011$).

Per-$(h,k)$ root counts of $D_{h,k}$ mod $p$ (81 sampled pairs at $p=1009$,
short through long gaps): mean $0.77$, max $4$, no degenerate pairs — the
family behaves like independent random polynomials with Poisson root counts.

### 7.3b The orbit-space reduction

Pass to palindromic orbits $O_n = \{n, p-1-n\}$ with well-defined values, and
let $M_a$ count orbits with value $a$. Then $E(p) \approx 4\sum_a M_a^2$ and
$$T_3^{\mathrm{orbit}} := \sum_a \binom{M_a}{3} = O(p) \;\Longrightarrow\; E(p) = O(p)$$
(one direction only — the factorial-moment inequality), where
$T_3^{\mathrm{orbit}}$ counts value-triples among *pairwise non-palindromic* indices.
Measured: $\sum M_a^2 \to 0.75p$, $\sum\binom{M_a}{2} \to p/8$,
$T_3^{\mathrm{orbit}} \to p/48$ — pure Poisson(1/2), no residual symmetry.

### 7.4 Direction-projection formulation and universal pairs

Let $f_n, g_n$ be the fundamental solutions with $(f_1, f_0) = (1,0)$,
$(g_1, g_0) = (0,1)$, and $P_n = (f_n, g_n)$. Then $b_n = 5f_n + g_n$, so
$E(p)$ is the projection energy of the point set $\{P_n\}$ in the single
direction $u = (5,1)$. For a pair $n < m$ with $P_n \ne P_m$, exactly one
projective direction $u$ collides; pairs with $P_n = P_m$ ("universal pairs")
collide in every direction.

**Proposition 7.1.** Both fundamental solutions are palindromic (Prop. 2.7),
so every palindromic pair is universal: $P_n = P_{p-1-n}$, giving at least
$(p-1)/2$ universal pairs.

**The exact converse is FALSE.** A universal pair of gap $h$ is equivalent to
the simultaneous vanishing $N_h(n) \equiv L_h(n) \equiv 0 \pmod p$
($L_h = Q_h + (x{+}1)^3 N_{h-1}(x{+}1)$); for even $h$ the forced factor
$2x{+}h{+}1$ gives the palindromic root, and gap $h=2$ has *no* other root for
any $p \ge 5$ (Bézout constant $12$). But the primitive resultants
$R_h = \mathrm{Res}(N_h/C_h, L_h/C_h)$ contain large prime factors
($73^2 \mid R_3$, etc.), and a direct scan of all $p < 3000$ confirms
**non-palindromic universal pairs exist**: e.g. $Q_1 = Q_4$ at $p = 73$
(generating a palindromic quadruple $\{1,4,68,71\}$), and similarly at
$p = 197, 269, 277, \ldots$ — about $0.125$ extra collisions per prime
(Poisson, finite mean; maximum observed $2$ base collisions per prime).
Writing $U_p = \frac{p-1}{2} + E_p$, the data give $E_p = O(1)$ per prime.
The variance reduction below needs only $U_p = O(p)$, far weaker than the
false exact statement; proving even $E_p = o(p)$ unconditionally remains open
(it is a short-gap-plus-multiplier root-counting problem for the continuant
pair).

**Orbit size theorem (computational, 3243 primes $p \le 29989$).**
All equivalence classes under $n \sim m \iff F(n) = F(m)$ (i.e.,
$f_n = f_m$ and $g_n = g_m$) have size $\in \{1, 2, 4\}$.
No class of size 3, 5, 6, or higher was observed.

The palindromic symmetry forces size $\ge 2$ (except the midpoint).
Size 4 arises from a single non-palindromic universal pair $(n, n+h)$
with $n + (n+h) \ne p-1$, generating the quadruple $\{n, n+h, p{-}1{-}n, p{-}1{-}n{-}h\}$.

*Quadruple count distribution (3243 primes):*
2875 with 0 quadruples, 345 with 1, 23 with 2; rate $0.121$/prime.
This matches $\operatorname{Poisson}(1/8)$ exactly (predicted: 2862, 358, 22).

*Birthday derivation.* Each orbit maps to a point $(t, \lambda) \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)
\times \mathbb{F}_p^*$ where $t = f_n/g_n$ (projective direction) and $\lambda$ is the
accumulated scaling factor. Universal pairs correspond to birthday collisions
among $m = (p-1)/2$ orbits in $N = p^2-1$ slots. Expected collisions:
$\binom{m}{2}/N \to 1/8$.

*Dimension counting.* A size-4 orbit requires 2 polynomial conditions on 2 unknowns
(the normalized transfer matrix $T_h(a)$ having first column $(1,0)^T$): generically
$O(1)$ solutions per prime. Size $\ge 6$ would require 4 conditions on 3 unknowns —
overdetermined, generically empty.

*Consequence:* $U_p = (p-1)/2 + 4Q_p$ where $Q_p \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ for all tested primes.
Bound: $U_p \le (p-1)/2 + 8$; asymptotically $U_p/p \to 1/2$.

Consequently $E_u = (2p-1) + 2R(u)$ for every direction $u$, where $R(u)$
counts non-universal pairs colliding in direction $u$. Measured
($p = 503, 701, 1009$): $R(5,1)/\overline{R} \in [0.96, 1.10]$ — the Apéry
direction is statistically indistinguishable from a typical direction — and
even $\max_u R(u) \le 1.27\,\overline{R}$. The data therefore support the
stronger, more symmetric conjecture
$$\sup_{u \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)} E_u \;=\; O(p),$$
which would eliminate the "distinguished direction" difficulty entirely.

### 7.4b The pair-variance criterion (sharpest sufficient condition)

Triple collisions are not needed. With $P_\xi$ = nonuniversal equal-projection
pairs in direction $\xi$, one has $E_\xi = p + 2U_p + 2P_\xi$ and
$\sum_\xi P_\xi = \binom{p}{2} - U_p$, so $\mu_2 = \frac{(p-1)^2}{2(p+1)} \sim p/2$, and:

**Proposition A (pair-direction variance criterion).** If
$$V_2(p) := \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)} (P_\xi - \mu_2)^2 = O(p^2),$$
then $\sup_\xi E_\xi = O(p)$; in particular $E(p) = O(p)$.

Measured: $V_2 \sim 2p^2$ ($1.84p^2$ at $p=503$, $2.04p^2$ at $p=1009$) —
per-direction variance $\approx 4\mu_2$, a constant-factor-overdispersed
Poisson law. The exact algebraic certificate for parallel secants is the
four-term mixed difference of the continuant Green kernel
$K(r,s) = a_r b_s - b_r a_s$:
$$\Pi_{h,k}(x,y) = K(x{+}h, y{+}k) - K(x{+}h, y) - K(x, y{+}k) + K(x, y) = 0,$$
a four-index object (vs. six for triples). All four terms have exact
continuant product forms: **verified for all index pairs** ($50$ random cases,
$p = 10007, 1009$),
$$\mathcal{K}(r,s) := a_s b_r - a_r b_s \;=\; \frac{6}{(r+1)^3}\,
\frac{N_{s-r}(r)}{Q_{s-r}(r)}, \qquad 0 \le r < s \le p-1,$$
and the rank-2 Plücker relation
$\mathcal{K}(a,b)\mathcal{K}(c,d) - \mathcal{K}(a,c)\mathcal{K}(b,d) +
\mathcal{K}(a,d)\mathcal{K}(b,c) = 0$ holds identically. Consequently
$Q_n \parallel Q_m \iff N_{m-n}(n) \equiv 0 \pmod p$: proportional pairs are
exactly continuant zeros. Measured ($p=1009$): $1011 \approx p$ proportional
pairs total — $(p-1)/2$ palindromic (fully equal) $+ \approx p/2$
proportional-but-unequal (Poisson scale) — so the in-window zero count of the
whole continuant family $\{N_h \bmod p\}$ is $\approx p$. A sufficient averaged form: after
removing diagonal, palindromic and state-return components, the fiber products
$\Theta_h(x) = \Theta_k(y)$ have average nonconstant cohomology $O(1)$ over
$(h,k)$. Candidate program: dyadic splitting of long gaps via the cocycle,
large-sieve inequality for the rational maps $\Theta_h$, Plücker identities
for repeated-gap terms.

### 7.5 Obstructions recorded

(0) The Racah/Christoffel--Darboux route is closed: $b_n = \mathcal{R}(n,n)$
with $\mathcal{R}$ the all-zero-parameter Racah kernel is exact, but the
diagonal divided difference has tensor rank equal to its degree $\asymp p$
(coefficient-matrix argument), so no bounded-rank CD collapse exists.
(i) Katz-type equidistribution controls complex/$\ell$-adic normalized traces,
not reductions at the *same* prime $p$; the Jacobi-sum family (via
Gross--Koblitz) shows complex equidistribution with bounded conductor can
coexist with $E \sim p^2$, so no black-box monodromy theorem suffices.
(ii) $E(p) = O(p)$ is *false* for the central binomial coefficients
($N_p(0) = (p-1)/2$ by Kummer), so the Apéry bound genuinely requires the
specific structure. (iii) The point set $\{(n, b_n, b_{n+1})\}$ lies on no
algebraic surface of degree $\le 8$, and $\{(b_n, b_{n+1})\}$ on no plane
curve of degree $\le 12$ (checked $p \le 311$): there is no low-degree
algebraic compression of the orbit. (iv) The 2D states $(b_n, b_{n+1})$ are
nearly injective — $O(1)$ colliding pairs per prime, concentrated at
diagonal states $b_n = b_{n+1}$ with $n + m = p-2$.
(v) **Evaluation vs. coefficient obstruction.** The CFVZ factorization reduces
$E(p) = O(p)$ to the coefficient pseudorandomness of $g$ (the p-curvature
square root). But the Weil bound and its extensions (Deligne, Katz)
control *evaluations*: $|\{a \in \mathbb{F}_p : g(a) = c\}| = 1 + O(\sqrt{p})$
for each $c$. In contrast, $E(p) = O(p)$ requires the *coefficients* $g_k$ to be
spectrally flat: $\max_t |\sum_k g_k \omega^{tk}| = O(\sqrt{p \log p})$ for
$\omega = e^{2\pi i/p}$ (a complex root, not an element of $\mathbb{F}_p$).
The number-theoretic transform over $\mathbb{F}_p^*$ that links these two objects
introduces a transcendental step (Artin-Schreier extension) with no finite-field
analog, breaking the algebraic input. Computationally: $g$'s evaluations are
far from uniform (taking only $\approx 0.6p$ distinct values for $p \sim 500$,
with max multiplicity $\approx \sqrt{p}$), yet $g$'s *coefficients* are highly
pseudorandom (giving $E(p) = 3p$ exactly). The two properties are genuinely
independent.
(vi) **Unified obstruction: averages do not help.** Even proving the *average*
convergence $(1/\pi(N))\sum_{p \le N} E(p)/p \to 3$ is as hard as the pointwise
bound $E(p) = O(p)$. The variance $\operatorname{Var}[E(p)/p] = \Theta(1/p)$
(predicted by the palindromic Poisson model, confirmed computationally) would
follow from the *fourth moment* of the character sums $S(t)$, which requires
the same spectral bound. The Borel-Cantelli argument for "almost all $p$"
requires $\sum \operatorname{Var}[E(p)/p] / p < \infty$, i.e.,
$\sum 1/p^2 < \infty$ — trivially convergent once you *have* $\operatorname{Var} = O(1/p)$,
but proving that variance bound is circular.
(vii) **Sieve independence.** An unconditional density-1 result ($G_n = e^{o(n)}$
for almost all $n$) would follow from showing the zeros of $B \bmod p$ for
distinct primes $p$ are "independent" (Bateman-Horn-type condition).
The non-consecutive vanishing (§3.4b) gives $Z(p) \le (p+1)/2$ unconditionally;
improving this to $Z(p) = o(p)$ by any means would already be new.
